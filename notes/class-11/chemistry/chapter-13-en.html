Chapter 13 : States of Matter

Class 11 Chemistry (UP Board) • handwritten-style notes

Core idea: state of matter = balance between intermolecular forces (pull molecules together) and thermal energy (pushes them apart).

1. Intermolecular forces

ForceBetweenExample
Dispersion / LondonAll molecules; instantaneous dipole ↔ induced dipole; ∝ 1/r⁶, stronger with sizeAr, Cl₂, noble gases
Dipole–dipolePermanent polar molecules; ∝ 1/r³ (fixed), 1/r⁶ (rotating)HCl, SO₂
Dipole–induced dipolePolar + non-polar; ∝ 1/r⁶HCl + Ar, O₂ in water
Hydrogen bondH (bonded to N/O/F) ↔ N/O/F; strongest (≈ 10–40 kJ/mol)H₂O, HF, NH₃

All these together are called van der Waals forces; they are fairly weak (0.1–10 kJ/mol). Due to H-bonding, H₂O boils far higher than H₂S.

2. Gas laws

LawStatement & formula
Boyle (T, n const.)P ∝ 1/V ⇒ P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ (isotherm = hyperbola)
Charles (P, n const.)V ∝ T (in K) ⇒ V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂; volume changes by 1/273.15 of its 0°C value per °C; at –273.15°C V → 0 (absolute zero)
Gay-Lussac (V, n const.)P ∝ T ⇒ P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂
Avogadro (P, T const.)V ∝ n; equal volumes of gases contain equal molecules; at STP 1 mol = 22.4 L; N_A = 6.022×10²³

3. Ideal gas equation

Derivation: V ∝ 1/P (Boyle), V ∝ T (Charles), V ∝ n (Avogadro) ⇒ V ∝ nT/P ⇒ PV = nRT. Values of R: 0.0821 L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 8.314 kPa dm³ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 1.987 cal K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Density: n = w/M ⇒ PV = (w/M)RT ⇒ d = PM/RT ⇒ M = dRT/P.

Dalton's law of partial pressures

For non-reacting gases P_total = p₁ + p₂ + p₃…; p_i = x_i·P_total (x = mole fraction). Moist gas: P_dry = P_total – aqueous tension.

4. Kinetic theory of gases

Postulates: (1) Gas has very many molecules in continuous random motion. (2) Molecular volume is negligible compared to container volume. (3) No attraction/repulsion between molecules. (4) Collisions are perfectly elastic. (5) Average kinetic energy ∝ absolute temperature; KE = (3/2)RT per mole. (6) Pressure arises from collisions with the wall.

Kinetic equation: PV = ⅓ mN u²_rms. Three speeds: u_mp = √(2RT/M); u_av = √(8RT/πM); u_rms = √(3RT/M); u_mp : u_av : u_rms = 1 : 1.128 : 1.224.

Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

On heating, the peak (u_mp) moves right and its height falls (flatter curve), but the area under the curve (total number of molecules) stays the same. For heavier molecules the curve lies to the left and is narrower.

5. Real gases

Real gases deviate from ideality at high pressure and low temperature, because molecular volume and intermolecular attraction can no longer be ignored. Compressibility factor Z = PV/nRT. Ideal gas: Z = 1; Z < 1 (attraction dominates, e.g. CO₂, NH₃); Z > 1 (repulsion/volume dominates, e.g. H₂, He at high P).

van der Waals equation (n mol): (P + an²/V²)(V – nb) = nRT. a – measure of attraction (atm L² mol⁻²); b – effective molecular volume (L mol⁻¹, ≈ 4× real volume). Larger a ⇒ easier liquefaction.

Boyle temperature: temperature at which a gas obeys Boyle's law over a wide pressure range; T_B = a/Rb.

6. Liquefaction of gases

Critical temperature T_c: above this a gas cannot be liquefied by any pressure. P_c: minimum pressure to liquefy at T_c. V_c: volume of 1 mol at T_c, P_c. T_c = 8a/27Rb, P_c = a/27b², V_c = 3b. CO₂ has T_c = 304 K (31.1°C), so it liquefies under pressure below that temperature. Andrews' isotherms confirm this.

7. Liquid state

Vapour pressure: pressure of vapour in equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature; rises with temperature; weaker intermolecular forces ⇒ higher. When vapour pressure = external pressure ⇒ boiling (normal boiling point at 1 atm).

Surface tension (γ): force/work needed to increase the surface by unit area; unit N m⁻¹. Cause: surface molecules feel a net inward pull ⇒ drops are spherical (minimum surface area). Decreases with temperature. Examples: capillary rise, insects walking on water.

Viscosity (η): internal resistance to flow between layers. F = ηA(du/dz). Unit: poise (1 P = 0.1 N s m⁻²). Viscosity of liquids falls with temperature; stronger intermolecular forces (H-bonding, e.g. glycerol) ⇒ higher viscosity.

8. Solved examples (step by step)

Example 1 (easy): 2 L gas at 1 atm is compressed to 0.5 L; new pressure?

Boyle: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ ⇒ 1×2 = P₂×0.5 ⇒ P₂ = 4 atm.

Example 2 (medium): Density of CO₂ at 27°C and 1 atm?

T = 300 K, M = 44 g/mol. d = PM/RT = (1×44)/(0.0821×300) = 44/24.63 = 1.786 g/L.

Example 3 (medium): Mixture of 0.5 mol N₂ and 1.5 mol O₂ at total 2 atm; partial pressures?

x(N₂) = 0.5/2 = 0.25; x(O₂) = 0.75. p(N₂) = 0.25×2 = 0.5 atm; p(O₂) = 0.75×2 = 1.5 atm.

Example 4 (hard): u_rms of O₂ at 300 K?

M = 0.032 kg/mol. u = √(3×8.314×300/0.032) = √(233,831) = ≈ 484 m/s.

Prove (hard): Boyle temperature T_B = a/Rb

At low pressure, for one mole Z = PV/RT ≈ 1 + (b – a/RT)P/RT. When b = a/RT, Z = 1 (Boyle's law obeyed). Hence T_B = a/Rb.

9. Questions & Answers

Very short / short

Q1. What causes London forces?

Instantaneous dipoles from momentary distortion of the electron cloud.

Q2. Why does H₂O boil higher than H₂S?

H₂O has hydrogen bonding; H₂S does not.

Q3. What is absolute zero?

–273.15°C (0 K), where an ideal gas would have zero volume.

Q4. Volume of 1 mol gas at STP?

22.4 L (273.15 K, 1 atm); (IUPAC STP at 1 bar: 22.7 L).

Q5. Value of R in L atm?

0.0821 L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

Q6. What is the most probable speed?

Speed at the peak of the distribution, u_mp = √(2RT/M).

Q7. What does Z < 1 mean?

Gas is more compressible than ideal; attraction dominates.

Q8. Units of a and b?

a: atm L² mol⁻²; b: L mol⁻¹.

Q9. What is critical temperature?

Highest temperature at which a gas can be liquefied by pressure.

Q10. Unit of surface tension?

N m⁻¹ (SI), dyne cm⁻¹ (CGS).

Q11. How does viscosity change with temperature?

Falls for liquids, rises for gases.

Q12. Why are drops spherical?

Surface tension minimises surface area; a sphere has the least area for a given volume.

Long answer

Q1. Derive the van der Waals equation.

For an ideal gas V is the free volume. In a real gas molecules have their own volume, so free volume is (V – nb). Attraction reduces the pressure on the wall, so measured P is lower: P_ideal = P + an²/V² because attraction ∝ (n/V)². Substituting in PV = nRT gives (P + an²/V²)(V – nb) = nRT.

Q2. Express critical constants in terms of a and b.

Writing the vdW equation as a cubic in V and equating all three roots at the critical point gives V_c = 3b, P_c = a/27b², T_c = 8a/27Rb. Hence Z_c = P_cV_c/RT_c = 3/8.

Q3. State conclusions of the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.

(i) Molecules have different speeds; most are near average. (ii) Higher T shifts the peak right and flattens it. (iii) u_mp < u_av < u_rms. (iv) Area is constant. (v) Heavier molecules shift the curve left.

MCQs

Q1. Strongest intermolecular force? (a) London (b) Dipole-dipole (c) H-bond (d) Dipole-induced dipole

Correct answer: (c) H-bond – Hydrogen bond.

Q2. u_mp : u_av : u_rms? (a) 1:1.128:1.224 (b) 1:1.224:1.128 (c) 1.224:1.128:1 (d) 1:1:1

Correct answer: (a) 1:1.128:1.224 – Standard ratio.

Q3. Boyle temperature? (a) a/Rb (b) 8a/27Rb (c) 2a/Rb (d) a/b

Correct answer: (a) a/Rb – T_B = a/Rb.

Q4. When is Z > 1? (a) H₂ at high P (b) CO₂ at low T (c) Ideal gas (d) NH₃ at low P

Correct answer: (a) H₂ at high P – Repulsion/volume effect dominates.

Q5. T_c of CO₂? (a) 304 K (b) 190 K (c) 33 K (d) 150 K

Correct answer: (a) 304 K – ≈ 304 K (31.1°C).
