Class 11 Chemistry · Ch 9: Hydrocarbons

Syllabus: Alkanes · Alkenes · Alkynes · Aromatic hydrocarbons (benzene)

1. Alkanes (CnH2n+2, sp³, σ-bonds only)

Nomenclature: longest chain + "ane"; lowest locants; alphabetical substituents. Isomerism: chain isomerism (C₄H₁₀ → butane, 2-methylpropane; C₅H₁₂ has 3). Conformations of ethane: staggered (stable) and eclipsed; rotation about C–C is almost free (barrier 12.5 kJ/mol).

Physical: C₁–C₄ gases, C₅–C₁₇ liquids, above solids; bp rises with mass, falls with branching; insoluble in water.

Free radical halogenation (CH₄ + Cl₂ →hν CH₃Cl + HCl)

Initiation: Cl–Cl →hν 2Cl·
Propagation: Cl· + CH₄ → CH₃· + HCl; CH₃· + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl·
Termination: Cl· + Cl·, CH₃· + CH₃· (→ C₂H₆), CH₃· + Cl·
Reactivity: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂; H-abstraction ease 3° > 2° > 1°.

Combustion: CnH2n+2 + (3n+1)/2 O₂ → n CO₂ + (n+1) H₂O, highly exothermic. Pyrolysis (cracking): heating at high temperature, no air, breaks higher alkane into smaller alkane + alkene (e.g. C₁₂H₂₆ → C₇H₁₆ + C₅H₁₀).

2. Alkenes (CnH2n)

Double bond: each C is sp², one σ + one π (sideways p-overlap, weaker, electron-rich, planar, 120°). Geometrical isomerism: restricted rotation → cis (same side) / trans (opposite side); trans has higher mp and is more stable, cis has higher bp (polar).

Preparation: (1) Dehydration: C₂H₅OH →conc. H₂SO₄, 443 K CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O (2) Dehydrohalogenation with alc. KOH (Saytzeff: more substituted alkene major) (3) Alkyne + H₂/Pd–C, Lindlar → cis-alkene.

Markovnikov rule: in addition of HX to unsymmetrical alkene, negative part goes to the C with fewer H (the more stable carbocation forms). Peroxide (Kharasch) effect: only HBr, via free radical; HCl/HI do not show it.
Mechanism (electrophilic addition): (1) π electrons attack H⁺ → carbocation (slow step) (2) X⁻ attacks carbocation (fast).

3. Alkynes (CnH2n−2)

Triple bond: sp, one σ + two π, linear 180°, C≡C = 120 pm. Preparation: CaC₂ + 2H₂O → C₂H₂ + Ca(OH)₂; dehydrohalogenation of vic-dihalides with alc. KOH then NaNH₂.
Acidic character: sp C–H (50% s) is more acidic than alkene/alkane: HC≡CH + NaNH₂ → HC≡CNa + NH₃; terminal alkynes give white ppt with ammoniacal AgNO₃ and red ppt with Cu₂Cl₂ (test). Order: alkyne > alkene > alkane.
Addition: H₂ → alkene → alkane; X₂ (decolourises Br₂ water); HX (Markovnikov, 2 steps: CH₃CHX₂); water: HgSO₄/H₂SO₄ → enol → CH₃CHO (ethyne) or ketone (other alkynes).

4. Aromatic Hydrocarbons (Benzene, C₆H₆)

Aromaticity: planar, cyclic, fully conjugated, (4n+2) π electrons (Hückel; benzene 6π, n=1). Naming: methylbenzene (toluene), 1,2-/1,3-/1,4- = o/m/p.

Mechanism (SEAr): (1) generate electrophile E⁺ (2) E⁺ attacks π cloud → arenium ion (sigma complex, slow, aromaticity lost) (3) loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity.
Directive influence: o,p-directing and activating: –OH, –NH₂, –CH₃ (donating). o,p-directing but deactivating: –Cl, –Br. m-directing and deactivating: –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COOH (withdrawing).
Carcinogenicity: polycyclic aromatics (benzo[a]pyrene, 1,2-benzanthracene) from coal tar and smoke cause cancer. Toxicity: benzene harms bone marrow (leukaemia risk).

5. Solved Examples

Easy: Propene + HBr, without and with peroxide?

Without: H goes to CH₂ (more H) → CH₃CHBrCH₃ (2-bromopropane). With peroxide: Br· adds first to CH₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₂Br.

Medium: 10 mL propane burns completely. O₂ and CO₂ volumes?

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. Ratio 1:5:3 → O₂ = 10 × 5 = 50 mL, CO₂ = 10 × 3 = 30 mL.

Hard: Alkene C₅H₁₀ on ozonolysis gives acetone and acetaldehyde. Identify it.

Step 1: join the C=O carbons by a double bond: (CH₃)₂C=O + O=CHCH₃. Step 2: (CH₃)₂C=CH–CH₃. Answer: 2-methylbut-2-ene, formula C₅H₁₀ ✓.

6. Q & A

Q1. Why is ethane's staggered form more stable?

H atoms are farthest apart, so repulsion (torsional strain) is minimum.

Q2. Why does only HBr show peroxide effect?

For HCl the H–Cl bond is too strong to break by Cl· step; for HI the I· is too unreactive to add to alkene.

Q3. Why is acetylene acidic but ethene is not?

sp carbon (50% s-character) is more electronegative, holds C–H electrons tightly, so H⁺ leaves easily.

Q4. MCQ: Hückel's rule for aromatic: (a) 4n (b) 4n+2 (c) 2n (d) 4n−2

Answer: (b) 4n+2 π electrons.

Q5. Why does benzene undergo substitution, not addition?

Addition would destroy stable delocalised π system (resonance energy ≈ 150 kJ/mol); substitution retains it.

Q6. Why is –NO₂ m-directing?

It withdraws electrons, lowering electron density most at o/p positions, so m position is relatively richer for E⁺.

Q7. Write Lindlar reduction of propyne.

CH₃C≡CH + H₂ →Pd/BaSO₄, quinoline CH₃CH=CH₂ (stops at alkene).
