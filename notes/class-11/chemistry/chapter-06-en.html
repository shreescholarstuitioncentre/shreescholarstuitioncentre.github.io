Class 11 Chemistry — Chapter 6: Equilibrium

Handwritten-style notes (UP Board / NCERT pattern). Easy, Medium, Hard.

A. PHYSICAL AND CHEMICAL EQUILIBRIUM

1. Dynamic Nature of Equilibrium

Physical equilibria: solid ⇌ liquid (ice/water at 273 K, 1 atm), liquid ⇌ vapour (constant vapour pressure in closed vessel), solid ⇌ vapour (I₂, camphor), solute(s) ⇌ solute(aq) (saturated solution). Chemical equilibrium is reached in a closed system when rate of forward reaction = rate of reverse reaction; concentrations stay constant but both reactions continue — hence dynamic. It can be approached from either side.

2. Law of Mass Action and Equilibrium Constant

Guldberg–Waage: rate of a reaction ∝ product of active masses (molar concentrations) of reactants. For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD:

Kc = [C]c[D]d / ([A]a[B]b)    Kp = Kc(RT)Δn

Pure solids and liquids are omitted. Features: depends only on temperature; reverse reaction K′ = 1/K; coefficients multiplied by n → Kn; adding reactions multiplies K's. Large K (>10³) products favoured; small K (<10⁻³) reactants favoured. Reaction quotient Q: Q < K → forward; Q > K → reverse; Q = K → equilibrium.

3. Le Chatelier's Principle and Factors

If a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to oppose the change.
Concentration: add reactant → forward. Pressure (gases): increase → side with fewer moles. Temperature: heating favours endothermic direction (K changes). Catalyst: no shift, only reaches equilibrium faster. Inert gas at constant volume: no shift.
Haber: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, ΔH = −92.4 kJ: favoured by high pressure (~200 atm), moderate T (~700 K), Fe catalyst. Contact process: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (V₂O₅).

B. IONIC EQUILIBRIUM

4. Electrolytes, Acids and Bases

Strong electrolytes ionize completely (HCl, NaOH, NaCl); weak partly (CH₃COOH, NH₄OH). Arrhenius: acid gives H⁺, base gives OH⁻. Brønsted–Lowry: acid = proton donor, base = proton acceptor; conjugate pairs (HCl/Cl⁻, NH₃/NH₄⁺). Lewis: acid = electron-pair acceptor (BF₃), base = donor (NH₃).

5. Ionization of Weak Acids/Bases, Degree of Ionization

Degree of ionization α = fraction ionized. For HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻: Ka = Cα²/(1−α) ≈ Cα², so α = √(Ka/C) (Ostwald dilution law: α increases on dilution), [H⁺] = √(KaC). Similarly Kb and [OH⁻] = √(KbC). Ka × Kb = Kw = 10⁻¹⁴ (298 K). Polyprotic acids ionize stepwise with Ka1 > Ka2.

6. pH Scale

pH = −log[H⁺], pOH = −log[OH⁻], pH + pOH = 14 at 298 K. pH < 7 acidic, = 7 neutral, > 7 basic. [H⁺][OH⁻] = Kw.

7. Hydrolysis of Salts

Strong acid + strong baseNaClneutral, pH 7

Weak acid + weak baseCH₃COONH₄depends on Ka, Kb

Kh = Kw/Ka (anionic), Kw/Kb (cationic). pH (weak acid + strong base) = ½(pKw + pKa + log C); (strong acid + weak base) = ½(pKw − pKb − log C); (weak + weak) = 7 + ½(pKa − pKb).

8. Buffer Solutions and Henderson Equation

Buffer resists change of pH on adding small amounts of acid/base. Acidic buffer: weak acid + its salt (CH₃COOH + CH₃COONa). Basic buffer: weak base + its salt (NH₄OH + NH₄Cl). Added H⁺ is consumed by the conjugate base; added OH⁻ by the weak acid.

pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid])    pOH = pKb + log([salt]/[base])

Best buffer when [salt] = [acid], pH = pKa.

9. Solubility Product and Common Ion Effect

Ksp: product of ion concentrations (powered to coefficients) in a saturated solution of sparingly soluble salt. AxBy ⇌ xAy+ + yBx−; Ksp = [Ay+]x[Bx−]y. AB type: Ksp = s²; AB₂/A₂B: Ksp = 4s³. Ionic product > Ksp → precipitation.
Common ion effect: adding an ion common to a weak electrolyte/sparingly soluble salt suppresses its ionization/solubility (NH₄Cl in NH₄OH lowers [OH⁻]; NaCl lowers solubility of AgCl). Used in qualitative analysis (group III hydroxides, group II sulphides).

10. Solved Numericals

Easy. pH of 0.01 M HCl.
HCl is strong: [H⁺] = 0.01 = 10⁻² M. pH = −log 10⁻² = 2.
Medium (weak acid). 0.1 M CH₃COOH, Ka = 1.8×10⁻⁵. Find [H⁺], pH and α.
[H⁺] = √(KaC) = √(1.8×10⁻⁶) = 1.34×10⁻³ M. pH = 3 − log 1.34 = 3 − 0.13 = 2.87. α = [H⁺]/C = 1.34×10⁻² = 1.34%.
Medium (Kc). H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI: 1 mol each in 1 L; at equilibrium 1.56 mol HI. Find Kc.
HI = 1.56 → x = 0.78 mol each of H₂, I₂ reacted → each left 1 − 0.78 = 0.22. Kc = (1.56)² / (0.22 × 0.22) = 2.434 / 0.0484 = 50.3.
Hard (buffer). Buffer: 0.1 M CH₃COOH + 0.2 M CH₃COONa (pKa = 4.74). Find pH; then pH after adding 0.01 mol HCl per litre.
pH = 4.74 + log(0.2/0.1) = 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04. After HCl: acetate falls to 0.19, acid rises to 0.11. pH = 4.74 + log(0.19/0.11) = 4.74 + 0.24 = 4.98 (tiny change — buffering).
Hard (Ksp). Ksp (AgCl) = 1.8×10⁻¹⁰. Solubility in water and in 0.1 M NaCl.
Water: s² = 1.8×10⁻¹⁰ → s = 1.34×10⁻⁵ M. In 0.1 M NaCl: [Cl⁻] ≈ 0.1, s = Ksp/0.1 = 1.8×10⁻⁹ M (common ion effect: ~7500× less).

11. Questions & Answers

Q1. Why is chemical equilibrium called dynamic?
Forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates; no net change is seen.
Q2. State law of mass action.
Rate of reaction is proportional to product of active masses of reactants.
Q3. Effect of catalyst on equilibrium?
None on position or K; only the time to reach equilibrium decreases.
Q4. Define pH. pH of 10⁻⁸ M HCl is it 8?
pH = −log[H⁺]. No; acid solution cannot be basic. Include water's 10⁻⁷: [H⁺] ≈ 1.05×10⁻⁷, pH ≈ 6.98.
Q5. What is common ion effect?
Suppression of ionization/solubility of a weak electrolyte or sparingly soluble salt by adding a common ion.
Q6. Long: Explain Le Chatelier's principle with Haber process.
N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (exothermic, 4→2 moles). High pressure favours NH₃; low T favours yield but slows rate, so ~700 K with Fe catalyst; removing NH₃ shifts forward.
Q7. Long: Explain hydrolysis of CH₃COONa and NH₄Cl.
CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻ (basic); NH₄⁺ + H₂O ⇌ NH₃ + H₃O⁺ (acidic).
Q8. MCQ: pH of 0.001 M NaOH? (a) 3 (b) 11 (c) 10 (d) 14
(b) pOH = 3, pH = 11.
Q9. MCQ: Which is a basic buffer? (a) CH₃COOH+CH₃COONa (b) NH₄OH+NH₄Cl (c) HCl+NaCl (d) NaOH+NaCl
(b).
Q10. MCQ: Solubility product of A₂B in s: (a) s² (b) 4s³ (c) 27s⁴ (d) s³
(b) 4s³.
