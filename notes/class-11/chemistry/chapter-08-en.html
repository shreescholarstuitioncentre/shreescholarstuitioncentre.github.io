1. Introduction

Organic chemistry = chemistry of hydrocarbons and their derivatives. Earlier, the Vital Force Theory (Berzelius) said organic compounds come only from living things. Wöhler (1828) disproved it by making urea from NH₄CNO: NH₄CNO →(heat) NH₂CONH₂. Carbon is special due to tetravalency, catenation (self-linking) and multiple bonds.

Chromatography

Components separate between a stationary phase and a mobile phase. Types: Column (adsorption), TLC, Paper (partition). Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent front (always < 1).

4. Quantitative Analysis (formulas)

%C = (12/44) × (mass CO₂/mass of compound) × 100
%H = (2/18) × (mass H₂O/mass of compound) × 100
Kjeldahl: %N = 1.4 × N × V ÷ m (N = normality of acid, V = mL acid neutralised)
Carius (Cl): %Cl = (35.5/143.5) × (mass AgCl/m) × 100

5. Classification & IUPAC

Open-chain (acyclic) vs closed-chain (alicyclic, aromatic). Steps: (1) longest chain = root, (2) lowest locants to the principal functional group, (3) name substituents alphabetically. Example: CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃ = 2-methylbutane.

6. Electronic Effects

Inductive: permanent polarisation of σ-bond. −I: NO₂ > CN > F > Cl > Br > I > OH > C₆H₅; +I: tertiary > secondary > primary alkyl.
Electromeric: temporary, only in presence of attacking reagent, in π-bonds.
Resonance (mesomeric): delocalisation of π/lone-pair electrons, e.g. benzene has two Kekulé structures; the hybrid is more stable.
Hyperconjugation: σ(C–H) electrons overlap with adjacent π/p-orbital ("no-bond resonance"). More α-H → more stable: CH₃–CH₂⁺ has 3, (CH₃)₃C⁺ has 9.

7. Bond Fission, Intermediates, Reagents

Homolytic: A–B → A· + B· (free radicals, by heat/light). Heterolytic: A–B → A⁺ + B⁻ (ions).
Stability: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃ for carbocation and free radical; reverse for carbanion.
Electrophile: electron-loving (H⁺, NO₂⁺, AlCl₃). Nucleophile: nucleus-loving (OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃).
Reactions: substitution, addition, elimination, rearrangement.

8. Solved Examples

Easy: 0.2 g compound gives 0.44 g CO₂. Find % C.

Given: m = 0.2 g, CO₂ = 0.44 g. Step 1: C in CO₂ = 12/44 × 0.44 = 0.12 g. Step 2: %C = 0.12/0.2 × 100 = 60%

Medium (Kjeldahl): 0.3 g compound, NH₃ neutralised 20 mL of 0.2 N H₂SO₄. Find % N.

%N = 1.4 × N × V/m = 1.4 × 0.2 × 20 ÷ 0.3 = 18.67%

9. Q & A

Q1. Why is distillation under reduced pressure used for glycerol?

Glycerol decomposes at its normal boiling point (563 K). Lowering pressure lowers bp, so it distils without decomposing.

Q2. Why is HNO₃ boiled with Lassaigne's extract before adding AgNO₃?

To decompose NaCN and Na₂S, which would otherwise form Ag₂S/AgCN and interfere with halogen test.

Q3. Arrange in order of stability: (CH₃)₃C⁺, CH₃⁺, (CH₃)₂CH⁺

(CH₃)₃C⁺ > (CH₃)₂CH⁺ > CH₃⁺ — due to +I effect and hyperconjugation (9, 6, 0 α-H).

Q4. MCQ: Which is a nucleophile? (a) BF₃ (b) NH₃ (c) H⁺ (d) NO₂⁺

Answer: (b) NH₃ — it has a lone pair to donate.

Q5. Define homolytic and heterolytic fission with an example each.

Homolytic: each atom keeps one electron, e.g. Cl–Cl → 2Cl·. Heterolytic: one atom takes both electrons, e.g. CH₃–Br → CH₃⁺ + Br⁻.

Q6. What is Rf value? Can it be > 1?

Rf = distance by substance ÷ distance by solvent. It cannot exceed 1, since the substance cannot outrun the solvent.
