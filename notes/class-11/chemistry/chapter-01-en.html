Class 11 Chemistry — Chapter 1: Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

Handwritten-style notes (UP Board / NCERT pattern). Colour key: Easy, Medium, Hard.

1. Importance and Scope of Chemistry

Chemistry is the study of composition, structure, properties and changes of matter. It helps in medicines (paracetamol, antibiotics), fertilisers, fuels, polymers, food preservation, and environment protection (CFC-free coolants, catalytic converters). Branches: organic, inorganic, physical, analytical, biochemistry.

2. Historical Approach to Particulate Nature of Matter

Ancient Indian philosopher Kanad (Vaisheshika) proposed "Parmanu" — indivisible particles. Greek Democritus called them "atomos". Dalton (1808) gave the first scientific atomic theory. Matter = solid / liquid / gas (particles differ in spacing & energy); pure substance = element or compound; mixture = homogeneous or heterogeneous.

3. Laws of Chemical Combination

(a) Law of Conservation of Mass — Lavoisier, 1789

Matter can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction: mass of reactants = mass of products. Eg. 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O : 4 g + 32 g = 36 g.

(b) Law of Constant (Definite) Proportion — Proust, 1799

A pure compound always has the same elements in the same mass ratio, whatever the source. Water: H : O = 1 : 8 by mass (11.1% H, 88.9% O) always.

(c) Law of Multiple Proportions — Dalton, 1803

If two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element combining with a fixed mass of the other are in a simple whole-number ratio. CO: 12 g C + 16 g O; CO₂: 12 g C + 32 g O → O ratio 16:32 = 1:2.

(d) Gay-Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes — 1808

Gases react in simple whole-number volume ratios at same T and P. H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl : 1 vol : 1 vol : 2 vol. 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O : 2 : 1 : 2.

(e) Avogadro's Law — 1811

Equal volumes of all gases at same T and P contain equal number of molecules. At STP, 1 mole of any gas = 22.4 L (22.7 L at 1 bar, 273 K). It explained why gases like H₂, O₂ are diatomic.

4. Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808)

  1. Matter is made of indivisible atoms. 2. Atoms of one element are identical in mass and properties; different for different elements. 3. Compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in fixed whole-number ratios. 4. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in reactions — only rearranged.

Limitations: atoms are divisible (electron, proton, neutron); isotopes (same element, different masses) and isobars exist; does not explain Gay-Lussac's law (molecule concept missing); no explanation of how atoms combine.

5. Elements, Atoms and Molecules

Atom: smallest particle of an element taking part in reaction. Molecule: smallest particle of a substance able to exist independently (H₂, O₂, H₂O). Element: one kind of atom. Compound: two or more elements chemically combined in fixed ratio.

6. Atomic Mass and Molecular Mass

Atomic mass unit (amu / u) = 1/12 of mass of one ¹²C atom = 1.66056 × 10⁻²⁴ g. Average atomic mass = Σ(isotopic mass × abundance)/100. Eg. Cl: (35×75.77 + 37×24.23)/100 = 35.45 u.
Molecular mass = sum of atomic masses of all atoms. H₂O = 2(1)+16 = 18 u; H₂SO₄ = 2+32+64 = 98 u. Formula mass is used for ionic compounds (NaCl = 58.5 u).

7. Mole Concept and Molar Mass

1 mole = amount of substance having 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro number, NA) = number of atoms in 12 g of ¹²C. Molar mass = mass of 1 mole in grams (numerically = atomic/molecular mass).

Moles n = given mass ÷ molar mass = N ÷ NA = V(STP) ÷ 22.4 L

8. Percentage Composition

% of element = (mass of element in 1 mole of compound ÷ molar mass) × 100. Eg. H₂O: H = 2/18 × 100 = 11.11%; O = 88.89%.

9. Empirical and Molecular Formula

Empirical: simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. Molecular: actual number of atoms. Molecular = n × Empirical, n = molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass.

Steps: (1) write % as grams (2) divide by atomic mass → moles (3) divide by smallest → ratio (4) multiply to get whole numbers (1.5 ×2, 1.33 ×3) (5) write formula.

10. Chemical Reactions and Stoichiometry

Balanced equation shows mole ratio. Types: combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement, redox. Limiting reagent: reactant that is consumed first and decides the product amount. Percent yield = actual/theoretical × 100. Concentration terms: mass %, mole fraction, molarity M = mol/L, molality m = mol/kg solvent.

Stoichiometric calculation steps: balance → convert given to moles → use mole ratio → convert to required unit.

11. Solved Numericals (step by step)

Easy. Find molecules in 9 g water.
Step 1: M(H₂O) = 18 g/mol. Step 2: n = 9/18 = 0.5 mol. Step 3: N = 0.5 × 6.022×10²³ = 3.011 × 10²³ molecules.
Medium. Compound: C 40%, H 6.7%, O 53.3%, molar mass 180. Find molecular formula.
Moles: C = 40/12 = 3.33; H = 6.7/1 = 6.7; O = 53.3/16 = 3.33. Divide by 3.33 → C:H:O = 1:2:1. Empirical CH₂O (mass 30). n = 180/30 = 6. Molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose).
Hard. 28 g N₂ reacts with 10 g H₂ (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃). Find limiting reagent, NH₃ formed and excess.
n(N₂) = 28/28 = 1 mol; n(H₂) = 10/2 = 5 mol. 1 mol N₂ needs 3 mol H₂, only 3 of 5 used → N₂ is limiting. NH₃ = 2 × 1 = 2 mol = 34 g. Excess H₂ = 5 − 3 = 2 mol = 4 g.

12. Questions & Answers (short, long, MCQ)

Q1. State law of conservation of mass.
Mass of reactants equals mass of products in a chemical change (Lavoisier).
Q2. What is Avogadro number?
6.022×10²³ particles per mole.
Q3. Define mole.
Amount of substance containing as many particles as atoms in 12 g of carbon-12.
Q4. Difference between empirical and molecular formula?
Empirical: simplest ratio (CH₂O). Molecular: actual atoms (C₆H₁₂O₆). Molecular = n × empirical.
Q5. What is a limiting reagent?
The reactant used up first; it limits the amount of product.
Q6. Long: State and explain the law of multiple proportions with example.
Dalton: when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one that combine with fixed mass of the other are in simple whole ratio. Carbon–oxygen: CO has 12 g C : 16 g O; CO₂ has 12 g C : 32 g O. Ratio of O = 1:2, a simple ratio. Nitrogen oxides N₂O, NO, NO₂ also follow it (14 g N with 16, 32, 64 g O → 1:2:4 for N₂O:NO:NO₂ scaled).
Q7. Long: Write postulates and limitations of Dalton's theory.
See section 4: four postulates; limitations include divisible atoms, isotopes, no molecule concept.
Q8. MCQ: Mass of 1 mole of CO₂? (a) 28 (b) 44 g (c) 32 (d) 12
(b) 44 g — 12 + 32.
Q9. MCQ: Volume of 1 mole gas at STP? (a) 11.2 L (b) 22.4 L (c) 44.8 L (d) 1 L
(b) 22.4 L.
Q10. MCQ: Law shown by CO and CO₂? (a) Constant (b) Multiple proportions (c) Gay-Lussac (d) Avogadro
(b) Multiple proportions.
Q11. MCQ: Molality is moles per (a) L solution (b) kg solvent (c) kg solution (d) L solvent
(b) kg solvent; unlike molarity it does not change with temperature.
