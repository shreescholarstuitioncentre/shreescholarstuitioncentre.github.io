Chapter 12 : Hydrogen

Class 11 Chemistry (UP Board) • handwritten-style notes

Remember: Hydrogen is the lightest element (1s¹) and the most abundant in the universe; on earth it occurs mainly in water.

1. Position in the Periodic Table

Configuration 1s¹ places it in group 1 (alkali metals), but it also resembles halogens, so its position is unique.

Similarity with alkali metalsSimilarity with halogens
One valence e⁻ (ns¹)One electron short of a full shell
Forms H⁺ (like Na⁺)Forms H⁻ (like Cl⁻)
Forms oxide (H₂O)Diatomic molecule H₂, X₂
Reducing agentHigh ionization enthalpy (1312 kJ/mol)

Differences: H⁺ is extremely small (~1.5×10⁻³ pm), never free — always hydrated (H₃O⁺); H is a non-metal while alkali metals are metals; IE is far higher.

Laboratory preparation

Granulated zinc with dilute H₂SO₄ (or HCl): Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂↑. Gas is collected by downward displacement of water. (Pure Zn reacts slowly; a drop of CuSO₄ speeds it up.)

Industrial methods

(i) Syngas: CH₄ + H₂O →(1270 K, Ni) CO + 3H₂. (ii) Water-gas shift: CO + H₂O →(673 K, Fe₂O₃) CO₂ + H₂ (CO₂ removed by water/alkali under pressure). (iii) Electrolysis of acidified/alkaline water with Pt electrodes – pure H₂ at cathode. (iv) By-product of NaCl electrolysis.

Properties

Colourless, odourless, tasteless, combustible; sparingly soluble in water. High bond enthalpy (436 kJ/mol) ⇒ unreactive at room temperature. 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O; H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl; N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (Haber, 673 K, 200 atm, Fe); CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O (reducing); hydrogenation of vegetable oils (Ni) → vanaspati ghee. Uses: NH₃, methanol, rocket fuel, oxy-hydrogen flame (~2800 K).

4. Water (H₂O)

Structure: O is sp³ hybridised with 2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs ⇒ bent (V) shape, bond angle 104.5°, O–H = 95.7 pm. Hydrogen bonding between molecules ⇒ high boiling point. Ice has an open hexagonal lattice ⇒ less dense than water (water is densest at 277 K).

Chemical properties: amphoteric (H₂O + NH₃ ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻; H₂O + H₂S ⇌ H₃O⁺ + HS⁻); redox: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂; 2F₂ + 2H₂O → 4HF + O₂; hydrolysis: SiCl₄ + 4H₂O → Si(OH)₄ + 4HCl; forms hydrates: CuSO₄·5H₂O.

Hard and soft water

Soft water lathers easily with soap. Hard water contains hydrogencarbonates, chlorides, sulphates of Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺; soap gives a scum (2C₁₇H₃₅COONa + Ca²⁺ → (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₂Ca↓ + 2Na⁺).

Temporary hardnessPermanent hardness
Due to Ca/Mg HCO₃⁻; removed by boilingDue to Ca/Mg Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻; not removed by boiling

Removal: (1) Boiling: Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O + CO₂ (temporary). (2) Clark's method: Ca(HCO₃)₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2CaCO₃↓ + 2H₂O (temporary). (3) Washing soda: CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃↓ + 2NaCl (permanent). (4) Calgon (Na₆P₆O₁₈): binds Ca²⁺ into soluble complex [Na₄P₆O₁₈]²⁻. (5) Permutit/zeolite (NaZ): 2NaZ + M²⁺ → MZ₂ + 2Na⁺; regenerated with NaCl. (6) Ion-exchange: cation exchanger (H⁺ resin) + anion exchanger (OH⁻ resin) ⇒ deionised water.

5. Hydrogen Peroxide (H₂O₂)

Preparation: (i) BaO₂·8H₂O + H₂SO₄ → BaSO₄↓ + H₂O₂ + 8H₂O (cold, filter). (ii) Auto-oxidation of 2-ethylanthraquinol (industrial). (iii) Electrolysis of acidic sulphate.

Structure: half-open-book, non-planar; O–O–H angle 94.8°, dihedral angle 111.5° (gas), O–O = 147.5 pm.

Oxidising (acidic): 2Fe²⁺ + 2H⁺ + H₂O₂ → 2Fe³⁺ + 2H₂O; PbS + 4H₂O₂ → PbSO₄ + 4H₂O. Reducing: 2MnO₄⁻ + 6H⁺ + 5H₂O₂ → 2Mn²⁺ + 8H₂O + 5O₂. Decomposition (disproportionation): 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂.

Storage: decomposes with light/heat ⇒ keep in plastic or coloured bottles in the dark; stabilisers – urea / phosphoric acid. Uses: bleach (paper, cloth), antiseptic, pollution control, rocket fuel. Volume strength: 10 vol ⇒ 1 mL gives 10 mL O₂ at STP.

6. Heavy Water (D₂O)

Water of deuterium; m.p. 276.8 K, b.p. 374.4 K, higher density (1.1 g/mL), slower acid-base/redox reactions. Made by prolonged electrolysis of water and fractional distillation. Uses: moderator in nuclear reactors (slows fast neutrons with little absorption), tracer, making D-labelled compounds.

7. Hydrogen Economy

Storing and transporting energy as H₂. Advantages: burns to water only, non-polluting, high heat per gram (≈143 kJ/g); used in fuel cells. Challenges: storage (high pressure/liquid/metal hydrides), production cost, safety. Green H₂ = water splitting using solar energy.

8. Solved examples (step by step)

Example 1: Molarity of 20-volume H₂O₂?

Step 1: 20 vol ⇒ 1 L gives 20 L O₂ (STP). Step 2: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ ⇒ 2 mol H₂O₂ per 22.4 L O₂. Step 3: (20/22.4)×2 = 1.786 mol. Molarity = 1.79 M (formula: vol/11.2).

Example 2: Water has 16.2 mg/L Ca(HCO₃)₂. Hardness in ppm (as CaCO₃)?

Ca(HCO₃)₂ = 162, CaCO₃ = 100. Equivalent CaCO₃ = 16.2×(100/162) = 10 mg/L. Hardness = 10 ppm.

Example 3: Number of neutrons in 10 g D₂O?

Molar mass of D₂O = 20 g; moles = 10/20 = 0.5. Neutrons per molecule = 2(D) + 8(O) = 10. Total = 0.5×10×6.022×10²³ = 3.011×10²⁴.

9. Questions & Answers

Very short / short

Q1. Why does hydrogen have a unique position?

It resembles both alkali metals (ns¹, H⁺) and halogens (H⁻, H₂).

Q2. Why is tritium radioactive?

High n/p ratio (2:1) ⇒ unstable nucleus, emits β⁻.

Q3. Why is H₂ a reducing agent?

It loses e⁻ to form H⁺ / reduces oxides to metals.

Q4. Why does ice float on water?

H-bonds make an open hexagonal structure ⇒ lower density.

Q5. What is water gas?

A mixture of CO + H₂ (syngas).

Q6. Why does hard water give scum with soap?

Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ form insoluble calcium/magnesium stearate.

Q7. Write the formula of Calgon.

Na₆P₆O₁₈ (sodium hexametaphosphate).

Q8. Why not store H₂O₂ in glass bottles?

Alkali from glass catalyses decomposition; use plastic/wax-lined bottles.

Q9. Why is H₂O₂ both oxidising and reducing?

O has oxidation state –1, which can go to –2 or 0.

Q10. Why is D₂O called a moderator?

It slows fast neutrons without absorbing them.

Q11. Two advantages of H₂ as fuel?

Non-polluting combustion; highest heat per gram.

Q12. Example of an ionic hydride and its reaction with water?

NaH + H₂O → NaOH + H₂↑.

Long answer

Q1. Explain methods of removing hardness of water.

Temporary: boiling and Clark's method. Permanent: washing soda, Calgon, permutit, ion exchange. Equations are in the notes above; in ion exchange the H⁺ resin swaps Ca²⁺ for H⁺ and the OH⁻ resin swaps Cl⁻/SO₄²⁻ for OH⁻, H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

Q2. Discuss oxidising and reducing properties of H₂O₂ with examples.

Oxidising in acidic medium: 2Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ + 2H⁺ → 2Fe³⁺ + 2H₂O. In basic: 2Cr³⁺ + 3H₂O₂ + 10OH⁻ → 2CrO₄²⁻ + 8H₂O. Reducing with strong oxidants like MnO₄⁻, releasing O₂.

Q3. Classify hydrides.

Ionic (NaH), molecular (CH₄, NH₃; electron-precise/rich/deficient) and metallic (PdH₀.₈); see the table above for features and blocks.

MCQs

Q1. Most abundant isotope? (a) Protium (b) Deuterium (c) Tritium (d) None

Correct answer: (a) Protium – ¹H ≈ 99.985%.

Q2. H–O–H angle in water? (a) 90° (b) 104.5° (c) 109.5° (d) 120°

Correct answer: (b) 104.5° – Lone pair repulsion gives 104.5°.

Q3. Temporary hardness is due to? (a) CaCl₂ (b) MgSO₄ (c) Ca(HCO₃)₂ (d) CaSO₄

Correct answer: (c) Ca(HCO₃)₂ – Hydrogencarbonates.

Q4. Oxidation state of O in H₂O₂? (a) –2 (b) –1 (c) 0 (d) +1

Correct answer: (b) –1 – –1.

Q5. Which is used as a moderator? (a) H₂O (b) D₂O (c) H₂O₂ (d) T₂O

Correct answer: (b) D₂O – D₂O.
