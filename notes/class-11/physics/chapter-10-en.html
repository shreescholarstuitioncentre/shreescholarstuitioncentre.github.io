✎ Class 11 Physics Ch 10: Mechanical Properties of Fluids

A1. Fluid, Pressure, Density & Pascal's Law

Liquids and gases are fluids (they flow). Pressure P = F/A (Pa, dim ML⁻¹T⁻²); 1 atm = 1.013×10⁵ Pa = 76 cm Hg. Density ρ = m/V; relative density = ρ/ρwater.

Pascal's law: pressure applied to enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions.

A2. Pressure by Liquid Column & Effect of Gravity

P = P₀ + hρg   (gauge pressure = hρg)

Pressure depends only on depth, density and g, not on shape or area of the vessel (hydrostatic paradox). Pressure is the same at all points on one horizontal level.

A3. Barometer, Manometer & Applications of Pascal's Law

Mercury barometer: Patm = hρg = 0.76×13600×9.8. Open-tube manometer: measures gauge pressure = hρg. Hydraulic lift/press/brakes: small force on small piston gives large force on large piston.

F₂ = F₁ (A₂/A₁)

B1. Ideal Liquid, Streamline & Turbulent Flow

Ideal liquid: incompressible and non-viscous. Streamline flow: every particle follows the path of the one before it (velocity below critical value); streamlines never cross. Turbulent flow: irregular, eddies form above critical velocity.

B2. Continuity Equation, Bernoulli's Theorem & Applications

A₁v₁ = A₂v₂
P + ρgh + ½ρv² = constant

Based on conservation of mass and energy. Where speed is more, pressure is less. Uses: venturimeter, atomiser/sprayer, aeroplane wing lift, curved path of spinning ball (Magnus effect), blowing off of roofs in storms.

B3. Velocity of Efflux (Torricelli)

v = √(2gh)  |  Range R = 2√(h(H − h))

Speed equals that of a body falling freely through h. Range is maximum (R = H) when hole is at depth h = H/2.

B4. Viscosity, Stokes' Law & Reynolds Number

F = ηA(dv/dx)  (η: Pa·s, dim ML⁻¹T⁻¹; 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s)
Stokes: F = 6πηrv  |  vt = 2r²(ρ − σ)g / 9η
Reynolds number Re = ρvD/η

Viscosity decreases with temperature for liquids, increases for gases. Re < 1000 streamline, > 2000 turbulent. Applications: raindrops, oil for lubrication, parachute.

C1. Surface Tension, Cohesion & Adhesion, Surface Energy

S = F/l (N/m, dim MT⁻²)  |  Surface energy = S × ΔA

Molecules inside feel balanced cohesive forces; surface molecules feel net inward pull, so liquid surface acts like stretched membrane and tries to have minimum area. Examples: round drops, needle floating, insects on water. S decreases with temperature and with detergents/impurities.

C2. Meniscus, Angle of Contact & Excess Pressure

Angle of contact θ acute (water–glass): concave meniscus, liquid wets glass. Obtuse (mercury–glass): convex meniscus.

Drop/bubble in liquid: ΔP = 2S/R
Soap bubble (2 surfaces): ΔP = 4S/R

C3. Capillarity & Detergents

h = 2S cosθ / (rρg)

Rise (θ<90°) or fall (θ>90°) in thin tubes. Examples: oil rising in wick, ink in blotting paper, sap in plants. Detergents lower S and θ, so water enters fabric pores and removes dirt.

✎ Solved Examples

Ex1. Gauge pressure at 10 m depth in water (g=10).
P = hρg = 10×1000×10 = 10⁵ Pa (about 1 atm).
Ex2. Hydraulic lift: A₁ = 10 cm², A₂ = 1000 cm², F₁ = 100 N. Find F₂.
F₂ = F₁A₂/A₁ = 100×100 = 10⁴ N.
Ex3. Pipe diameter 4 cm narrows to 2 cm; v₁ = 2 m/s. Find v₂.
A ∝ d², ratio 4. v₂ = 4×2 = 8 m/s.
Ex4. Hole 5 m below water surface (g=10). Efflux speed?
v = √(2gh) = √(2×10×5) = 10 m/s.
Ex5. Steel ball r = 1 mm (ρ = 8000) in glycerine (σ = 1260, η = 0.8 Pa·s). Terminal velocity?
v = 2r²(ρ−σ)g/9η = 2×10⁻⁶×6740×10/(9×0.8) ≈ 1.87×10⁻² m/s.
Ex6. Reynolds number: water ρ=1000, v=0.05 m/s, D=0.02 m, η=10⁻³.
Re = 1000×0.05×0.02/10⁻³ = 1000 → streamline.
Ex7. Excess pressure in soap bubble r = 2 cm, S = 0.03 N/m.
ΔP = 4S/R = 4×0.03/0.02 = 6 Pa.
Ex8. Capillary r = 0.5 mm, S = 0.07, θ = 0°, ρ = 1000, g = 10. Find h.
h = 2S/(rρg) = 0.14/(5×10⁻⁴×1000×10) = 0.028 m = 2.8 cm.
Ex9. Surface energy of soap bubble r = 1 cm, S = 0.03.
Area = 2×4πr² = 8π×10⁻⁴ = 2.51×10⁻³ m²; E = S×A ≈ 7.5×10⁻⁵ J.

✎ Q & A (tap)

Q1. State Pascal's law and one application.Pressure on enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished everywhere; hydraulic lift.
Q2. Derive P = P₀ + hρg.Weight of column = hAρg acts over area A, so extra pressure = hρg.
Q3. State and derive the equation of continuity.Mass entering = mass leaving: ρA₁v₁ = ρA₂v₂, so A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ for incompressible liquid.
Q4. Why does a spinning ball curve in flight?Air speed differs on two sides; pressure difference (Bernoulli) gives sideways force (Magnus effect).
Q5. How does an aeroplane get lift?Air moves faster over curved upper surface, lower pressure above, higher below, net upward force.
Q6. Why do raindrops fall with constant speed?Weight is balanced by viscous drag + buoyancy: terminal velocity.
Q7. Why is a soap bubble spherical?Surface tension minimises surface area; for given volume sphere has least area.
Q8. Why does a hot liquid flow more easily than a cold one?Viscosity of liquids falls with temperature, so it flows easily; surface tension also lowers.
Q9. Why does mercury not wet glass?Cohesion between mercury atoms exceeds adhesion with glass; obtuse angle of contact, convex meniscus.
Q10. How do detergents clean clothes?They reduce surface tension and angle of contact so water penetrates fibres and lifts dirt.
Q11. Why does blotting paper absorb ink?Fine pores act as capillaries; liquid rises by h = 2S cosθ/rρg.
