🌿 Class 11 Biology – Photosynthesis in Higher Plants

1. Site & Pigments

Photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 12H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O (light, chlorophyll). It occurs in chloroplasts (mesophyll cells): grana (thylakoids) = light reaction; stroma = dark (biosynthetic) reaction.

Pigments: Chl a (bright blue-green; main pigment, only one that does photochemistry), Chl b (yellow-green), Xanthophylls (yellow), Carotenoids (yellow-orange) – accessory pigments that absorb other wavelengths and protect Chl a from photo-oxidation. Action spectrum matches the absorption spectrum of Chl a: peaks in blue (430 nm) and red (662 nm) light. Engelmann's experiment (Cladophora + aerobic bacteria) proved it.

Photosystems: Light-harvesting complex (LHC) = 100s of pigment molecules + reaction centre. PS I: Chl a P700, in stroma lamellae. PS II: Chl a P680, in grana lamellae.

2. Light Reaction (Photochemical phase)

Steps: light absorption, water splitting, O₂ release, ATP and NADPH formation. Water splitting (by PS II, Mn, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻ in lumen): 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + O₂ + 4e⁻. Hill proved that O₂ comes from water.

Non-cyclic photophosphorylation

Both PS work in series. Light excites P680 → e⁻ to primary acceptor → plastoquinone → cyt b6f → plastocyanin → P700. Light excites P700 → e⁻ to acceptor → ferredoxin → NADP reductase → NADP⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADPH. Products: ATP + NADPH + O₂.

Cyclic photophosphorylation

Only PS I (stroma lamellae, no NADP reductase). e⁻ from P700 → acceptor → Fd → cyt b6f → PC → back to P700. Only ATP; no NADPH, no O₂. Occurs when light is of >680 nm or NADPH is abundant.

Chemiosmotic hypothesis (Mitchell)

ATP synthesis is linked to a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane. H⁺ in the lumen increase from (i) water splitting inside lumen, (ii) PQ carrying H⁺ from stroma to lumen as e⁻ pass, (iii) NADP⁺ reduction using stroma H⁺ (lowers stroma H⁺). Gradient drives H⁺ through the F₀ channel of ATP synthase; F₁ (CF₁) makes ATP.

3. Dark Reaction – Calvin Cycle (C3)

Occurs in stroma of all plants. First stable product: 3-phosphoglycerate (PGA, 3C). Enzyme RuBisCO (most abundant enzyme). For 1 CO₂ fixed: 3 ATP + 2 NADPH; for 1 glucose (6 turns): 18 ATP + 12 NADPH.

4. C4 (Hatch & Slack) Pathway

In maize, sugarcane, sorghum. Kranz anatomy: bundle sheath cells with large chloroplasts around vascular bundle. In mesophyll: PEP (3C) + CO₂ → OAA (4C) by PEP carboxylase (no O₂ affinity) → malate/aspartate → bundle sheath: releases CO₂ → Calvin cycle by RuBisCO. Pyruvate returns to mesophyll, converted to PEP using 2 ATP. Needs 5 ATP per CO₂ (30 ATP + 12 NADPH per glucose). Bundle sheath CO₂ is high so photorespiration is absent/negligible.

5. Photorespiration

RuBisCO binds O₂ (when O₂ high, CO₂ low) → RuBP + O₂ → phosphoglycolate + PGA. Pathway: chloroplast → peroxisome → mitochondrion; no ATP/NADPH made, CO₂ released – wasteful. Absent in C4 plants.

6. Factors & Blackman's Law

Law of limiting factors (Blackman, 1905): If a process depends on several factors, its rate is limited by the factor nearest its minimal value. Light: rate rises linearly at low intensity, saturates at ~10% of full sunlight. CO₂: 0.03–0.04% in air; C3 saturate ~450 µL/L, C4 ~ 360 µL/L. Temperature: C4 respond to higher temp. Water (stomatal closure) and mineral/chlorophyll are also internal factors.

7. Solved Examples

Ex.1 (Easy) ATP needed to fix 1 CO₂ in C3?
Carboxylation needs none; reduction of 2 PGA uses 2 ATP; regeneration of RuBP uses 1 ATP → 3 ATP, 2 NADPH.
Ex.2 (Medium) Energy for 2 glucose in C4?
Per glucose, 6 CO₂ × 5 ATP = 30 ATP; 6 × 2 = 12 NADPH. For 2 glucose: 60 ATP, 24 NADPH.
Ex.3 (Hard, to find) How many turns of Calvin cycle give 1 sucrose (C₁₂)?
12 carbons need 12 CO₂; one turn fixes 1 CO₂, so 12 turns; ATP = 36, NADPH = 24.
Ex.4 (Proof) Show that photolysis gives the O₂: Ruben & Kamen used H₂¹⁸O. With H₂¹⁸O + C¹⁶O₂ released O₂ was ¹⁸O₂; with C¹⁸O₂ and H₂¹⁶O it was ¹⁶O₂ → O₂ comes from water.
Ex.5 (Blackman) Rate stays 20 units though light is doubled; CO₂ is at 0.03%. Limiting factor? → CO₂, since increasing light brought no change.

8. Short & Long Q&A (tap to open)

Q1. Why is Chl a called the primary pigment?

Only Chl a has the reaction centre (P680/P700) that converts light into chemical energy; others are accessory.

Q2. Role of accessory pigments?

Absorb other wavelengths, pass energy to Chl a, and protect it from photo-oxidation.

Q3. What is the Z-scheme?

Zig-zag path of e⁻ in non-cyclic photophosphorylation from PS II to PS I, rising in energy at two light-driven steps.

Q4. Differences between cyclic and non-cyclic photophosphorylation.

Cyclic: only PS I, only ATP, no O₂, stroma lamellae. Non-cyclic: PS II + PS I, ATP + NADPH + O₂, in grana.

Q5. State the chemiosmotic hypothesis.

ATP is synthesised as H⁺ flow down their gradient from lumen to stroma through ATP synthase.

Q6. Why is RuBisCO most abundant enzyme?

It is in huge amounts since it is a slow enzyme (low turnover) with dual oxygenase/carboxylase activity.

Q7. What is the first stable product in C3 and C4?

C3: PGA (3C). C4: OAA (4C).

Q8. What is Kranz anatomy?

Wreath-like arrangement of bundle sheath cells with large chloroplasts around vascular bundles in C4 leaves.

Q9. Why is C4 more efficient?

CO₂ concentrated in bundle sheath; PEP carboxylase has no O₂ affinity; so no photorespiration, high yield at high temperature.

Q10. Define photorespiration.

Light-dependent O₂ uptake and CO₂ release by RuBisCO oxygenation in chloroplast–peroxisome–mitochondria; no sugar/ATP produced.

Q11. State Blackman’s law.

Rate of a process under several factors is limited by the one at minimum.

Q12. Why are C4 plants not light-saturated?

They use more light efficiently; saturation is seen in C3 only at ~10% full sunlight.
