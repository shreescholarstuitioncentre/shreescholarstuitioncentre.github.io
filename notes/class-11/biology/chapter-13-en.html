🌱 Class 11 Biology – Plant Growth and Development

1. Growth

Growth is an irreversible, permanent increase in size, volume, weight or cell number. Plants show open (indeterminate) growth – meristems (apical, lateral, intercalary) keep dividing throughout life, unlike animals. Growth is measurable, at cell level it is increase in protoplasm.

Three phases of growth: (i) Meristematic – cell division at root/shoot tips (rich protoplasm, large nucleus, thin wall). (ii) Elongation – cells enlarge by vacuolation, new cell wall deposited. (iii) Maturation – cells attain final size and specialise.

Growth rate & sigmoid curve

Lag phase – slow growth; Log (exponential) phase – fastest growth; Stationary phase – growth stops due to limited nutrients (senescence). Curve is S-shaped (sigmoid).

Arithmetic growth: only one daughter cell keeps dividing; Lt = L0 + rt (linear curve; root elongating at constant rate). Geometric growth: both daughter cells divide; W1 = W0 ert. Absolute growth rate = total growth per unit time. Relative growth rate = growth per unit time per initial parameter.

Conditions: water (cell enlargement), O₂ (energy), nutrients, temperature, light. Measurement: Direct – horizontal microscope; auxanometer / arc auxanometer; J.C. Bose's crescograph (magnifies 10,000×).

2. Differentiation, Dedifferentiation, Redifferentiation

Differentiation: meristematic cells mature to perform specific functions (e.g. tracheary elements lose protoplasm, get thick walls). Dedifferentiation: permanent cells regain ability to divide (interfascicular cambium, cork cambium). Redifferentiation: dedifferentiated cells lose division power again and mature (secondary xylem/phloem, cork). Development = growth + differentiation. Callus formation in tissue culture is dedifferentiation.

Plasticity: ability of plants to follow different pathways in response to environment or phases of life. Eg. Heterophylly: cotton, coriander, larkspur (juvenile and mature leaves differ); buttercup (air vs water leaves). Growth is determined by intrinsic (genetic, PGRs) and extrinsic (light, temp, water, O₂, nutrients) factors.

3. Plant Growth Regulators (PGRs)

Small, simple molecules (indole compounds, adenine derivatives, carotenoid derivative, terpenes, gas). Two groups: promoters (auxin, GA, cytokinin) and inhibitors (ABA, ethylene – ethylene can do both).

Auxins

Discovery: Darwin – coleoptile tip senses light; Boysen-Jensen – mobile substance passes through gelatin; Paal – tip produces substance; F.W. Went isolated it from oat coleoptile tip on agar blocks (1928) and called it auxin (Greek auxein); Kögl & Haagen-Smit isolated IAA from human urine. Types: IAA, IBA (natural); NAA, 2,4-D (synthetic). Functions: cell elongation; apical dominance (shoot tip inhibits lateral buds; decapitation promotes branching); root initiation in cuttings; parthenocarpy (tomato); prevents premature fruit/leaf drop but promotes abscission of older leaves; flowering in pineapple; 2,4-D kills dicot weeds; controls xylem differentiation.

Gibberellins

Discovery: Kurosawa studied the bakanae ("foolish seedling") disease of rice caused by fungus Gibberella fujikuroi; Yabuta isolated GA. Over 100 known (GA₃ most studied). Functions: increase internode length (stem elongation) and grape stalk length; bolting in beet, cabbage; delay senescence (apples keep fresh); malting – speeds brewing; increase sugarcane stem length and sugar yield (~20 tonnes/acre); speed up maturity in conifers; promote seed germination, break dormancy; cause parthenocarpy.

Cytokinins

Discovery: Skoog & Miller found kinetin from autoclaved herring sperm DNA – promoted cell division in tobacco pith; zeatin (Letham) from maize endosperm/coconut milk. Functions: cell division (cytokinesis); lateral bud growth (overcome apical dominance); new leaves, chloroplast, adventitious shoots; delay senescence (Richmond-Lang effect) by nutrient mobilisation; tissue culture: high auxin:cytokinin → roots; high cytokinin:auxin → shoots.

Ethylene

Discovery: Cousins – ripened oranges hastened ripening of bananas. A gaseous hormone, from ripening fruit and senescing tissue. Functions: fruit ripening (apple, banana, tomato; climacteric rise in respiration); epinasty; breaks seed/bud dormancy; promotes rapid internode/petiole elongation in deep-water rice; root-hair growth; flowering in pineapple, mango; promotes femaleness in cucumber; abscission; senescence. Ethephon (releases ethylene) is used commercially.

Abscisic acid (ABA)

Discovery: Addicott – abscission-promoting substance in cotton fruits (abscisin II, "dormin"). Stress hormone. Functions: closes stomata under water stress (increases drought tolerance); induces seed dormancy and seed development; inhibits seed germination; promotes abscission and senescence; antagonist to GA.

4. Solved Examples

Ex.1 (Easy) A root grows from 10 cm to 12 cm in 1 day. Absolute growth rate? 12 − 10 = 2 cm/day.
Ex.2 (Easy) Relative growth rate = 2/10 = 0.2 = 20% per day.
Ex.3 (Medium) Arithmetic growth: L0 = 2 cm, r = 0.5 cm/day, t = 10 days. Lt = L0 + rt = 2 + 0.5×10 = 7 cm.
Ex.4 (Medium) Geometric growth: W0 = 2 g, r = 0.1/h, t = 5 h. W = 2 × e0.5 = 2 × 1.65 = 3.3 g.
Ex.5 (Hard, to find) Arc auxanometer: pointer length (radius R) = 20 cm, pulley radius r = 2 cm, pointer moves 5 cm on arc. Growth = arc × r/R = 5 × 2/20 = 0.5 cm.
Ex.6 (Hard) Tobacco callus with high cytokinin, low auxin: what forms? → shoots (reverse gives roots).
Ex.7 (Proof) Auxin moves from tip to the shaded side: Went cut tips, placed on agar, put the agar off-centre on decapitated coleoptile in the dark. The stump bent to the side lacking the block → block diffused a growth-promoting substance (auxin) that causes elongation on its own side. Hence unequal auxin gives bending.
Ex.8 (Application) A gardener cuts the tip of a hedge. Why does it become bushy? Removal of the apex removes auxin source, so apical dominance ends and lateral buds grow.

5. Short & Long Q&A (tap to open)

Q1. Define growth.

Irreversible permanent increase in size, weight, volume or number of cells.

Q2. Why is plant growth called open?

Meristems keep dividing throughout life, adding new organs.

Q3. Name the three phases of growth.

Meristematic, elongation and maturation.

Q4. Explain sigmoid growth curve.

S-shaped; slow lag phase, rapid log phase, then stationary phase as nutrients limit growth.

Q5. Arithmetic vs geometric growth.

Arithmetic: one daughter cell divides; linear curve. Geometric: both divide; exponential curve.

Q6. What is an auxanometer?

Instrument for measuring growth in length of plants.

Q7. Define dedifferentiation with example.

Mature cells regain division power, e.g. formation of interfascicular and cork cambium.

Q8. What is plasticity? Example.

Ability of a plant to alter form with environment; heterophylly in cotton, coriander, buttercup.

Q9. Who discovered auxin and how?

Went, 1928, using oat coleoptile tips on agar blocks.

Q10. What is apical dominance?

Shoot apex inhibits growth of lateral buds, due to auxin; removing tip promotes branching.

Q11. Role of GA in brewing.

GA speeds malting of barley by inducing α-amylase in seeds.

Q12. Why is ethylene called the ripening hormone?

It is a gas that triggers fruit ripening with a rise in respiration.

Q13. Why is ABA called stress hormone?

It closes stomata and promotes dormancy under drought and other stress.

Q14. Why is ABA called antagonist to GA?

GA promotes germination, growth; ABA inhibits germination and induces dormancy.
