Chapter 18: Neural Control and Co-ordination

Class 11 Biology • Unit 5: Human Physiology

1. Nervous System & Neuron

The nervous system gives fast, short-lived, point-to-point control (the endocrine system gives slow, long-lasting control). Its unit is the neuron.

Structure of a neuron (animated impulse below)

Cell body (cyton/soma): nucleus + Nissl's granules (rough ER + ribosomes). Dendrites: short branched fibres that carry impulses towards the cell body. Axon: single long fibre carrying impulses away; ends in axon terminals with synaptic knobs holding vesicles of neurotransmitter. Axon may be covered by myelin sheath (Schwann cells); gaps are nodes of Ranvier.

Types: Unipolar (embryo), Bipolar (retina, olfactory epithelium), Multipolar (cerebral cortex). Myelinated fibres (white matter, fast – saltatory conduction) vs non-myelinated (grey matter, slow).

2. Nerve Impulse

StateMembraneIon movement
RestingPolarised: outside +ve, inside −ve; −70 mVK⁺ leak out; Na⁺ kept out; Na⁺–K⁺ pump moves 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in (ATP)
DepolarisationNa⁺ channels open; inside becomes +ve (~ +30 mV)Na⁺ rushes in
RepolarisationK⁺ channels open; Na⁺ channels closeK⁺ moves out → −70 mV restored

Action potential = brief reversal of polarity. It is all-or-none and travels as a wave: the depolarised patch makes the neighbouring patch reach threshold. In myelinated fibres it jumps node to node (saltatory conduction) – faster, saves energy. Refractory period: no new impulse can start until the membrane resets.

Example (step-by-step): Resting potential is −70 mV and threshold is −55 mV. How much depolarisation is needed?
Required change = (−55) − (−70) = +15 mV. A stimulus giving only +10 mV (→ −60 mV) fails to trigger an action potential; +15 mV or more triggers a full one.

3. Synapse

A synapse is the junction between two neurons (pre-synaptic axon terminal and post-synaptic dendrite/soma) with a synaptic cleft.

FeatureElectricalChemical
GapVery narrow; gap junctionsSynaptic cleft (~20 nm)
TransmissionDirect ion flowNeurotransmitter
SpeedFaster, bidirectionalSlower, one-way
OccurrenceRare in humans (some brain areas, cardiac)Most common
Chemical transmission steps: 1. Impulse reaches the axon terminal. 2. Ca²⁺ enters; vesicles fuse with the membrane. 3. Acetylcholine (or other transmitter) released into the cleft. 4. It binds receptors on the post-synaptic membrane → ion channels open → new potential. 5. Enzyme (acetylcholinesterase) breaks it down so the signal ends.

4. Central Nervous System

CNS = brain + spinal cord, protected by skull/vertebral column and three meninges: dura mater (outer), arachnoid (middle), pia mater (inner), with cerebrospinal fluid between.

RegionPartsMain functions
ForebrainCerebrum, thalamus, hypothalamusCerebrum: intelligence, memory, speech, voluntary action (4 lobes: frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital; corpus callosum joins hemispheres). Thalamus: relay centre. Hypothalamus: temperature, hunger, thirst, hormones.
MidbrainCorpora quadrigeminaReflexes of sight and hearing, eye movement
HindbrainCerebellum, pons, medulla oblongataCerebellum: balance, posture. Pons: relay, breathing regulation. Medulla: heartbeat, breathing, swallowing, vomiting.

Limbic system (hippocampus, amygdala, parts of hypothalamus) = emotions, motivation, memory. Brain stem = midbrain + pons + medulla.

Spinal cord: in the vertebral canal, continues from medulla; grey matter inside (H-shaped; cell bodies), white matter outside (axons). Dorsal root = sensory; ventral root = motor. Controls reflex actions.

Reflex arc: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord (interneuron) → motor neuron → effector (e.g. knee jerk, withdrawing a hand from fire).

5. Solved Questions

Q1. What are Nissl's granules?

Clusters of rough ER and ribosomes in the cell body that make proteins for the neuron.

Q2. Why is conduction faster in myelinated fibres?

The impulse jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next (saltatory conduction) instead of passing along the whole membrane.

Q3. Role of the Na⁺–K⁺ pump?

It maintains resting potential by sending 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in using ATP.

Q4. Why are chemical synapses one-way?

Vesicles with transmitter are only in the pre-synaptic knob and receptors only on the post-synaptic membrane.

Q5. Long: Describe the generation and conduction of a nerve impulse.

Explain resting (−70 mV, Na⁺–K⁺ pump), depolarisation (Na⁺ in), repolarisation (K⁺ out), then spread to the next patch; add saltatory conduction. Draw the action-potential curve.

Q6. Name the part of the brain controlling (a) balance (b) breathing (c) body temperature.

(a) Cerebellum (b) Medulla oblongata (c) Hypothalamus.
