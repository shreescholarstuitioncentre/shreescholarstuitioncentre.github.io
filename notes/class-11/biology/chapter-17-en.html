Chapter 17: Locomotion and Movement

Class 11 Biology • Unit 5: Human Physiology

1. Movement & Locomotion

Movement = any change of position of body or its parts. Locomotion = movement that shifts the whole body from one place to another (walking, swimming, flying). All locomotion is movement, but not all movement is locomotion (e.g. blinking, heartbeat).

Types of movement in cells: Amoeboid (pseudopodia; WBCs, macrophages, Amoeba – uses microfilaments), Ciliary (trachea, oviduct), Muscular (limbs, jaws, tongue).

3. Structure of Skeletal Muscle

Muscle → fascicles (bundles) → muscle fibre (cell; plasma membrane = sarcolemma, cytoplasm = sarcoplasm, ER = sarcoplasmic reticulum, stores Ca²⁺) → myofibrils → myofilaments.

Sarcomere = functional unit, the portion between two successive Z-lines. Dark A-band (anisotropic) = myosin (+ overlapping actin). Light I-band (isotropic) = only actin; Z-line passes through its middle. H-zone = centre of A-band with only myosin. M-line = middle of H-zone, holds myosin.

Contractile proteins

Actin (thin filament): two F-actin strands twisted like a helix, each a polymer of G-actin; tropomyosin runs along the groove; troponin sits on tropomyosin at intervals. At rest tropomyosin covers the myosin-binding sites.

Myosin (thick filament): polymer of meromyosins. Each has HMM (head + short arm, projects as cross-arm; has ATPase and actin-binding sites) and LMM (tail).

4. Sliding Filament Theory (Animated)

Steps (Huxley & Hanson):
1. Nerve impulse reaches the neuromuscular junction; acetylcholine is released → action potential in the sarcolemma.
2. Ca²⁺ is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
3. Ca²⁺ binds troponin → tropomyosin shifts → myosin-binding sites on actin are exposed.
4. Myosin head (with ADP + Pi) binds actin → cross-bridge.
5. Head pivots, pulling actin toward the M-line (power stroke); ADP + Pi released.
6. A new ATP binds the head → cross-bridge breaks; ATP hydrolysis re-cocks the head.
7. Cycle repeats; Z-lines come closer = contraction. When Ca²⁺ is pumped back, the muscle relaxes.

Remember: A-band length stays same; I-band and H-zone shorten. Red fibres (slow, myoglobin-rich, many mitochondria, aerobic) vs white fibres (fast, little myoglobin, anaerobic). Fatigue = lactic acid build-up from anaerobic breakdown.

6. Disorders

Myasthenia gravis: autoimmune; antibodies block acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction → fatigue, weakness, paralysis.
Muscular dystrophy: genetic, progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle (e.g. Duchenne, X-linked).
Tetany: low Ca²⁺ → rapid spasms.
Arthritis: joint inflammation; osteoarthritis (cartilage wear), rheumatoid (autoimmune), gout (uric acid crystals).
Osteoporosis: reduced bone mass, fragile bones; common in old age, women after menopause (low oestrogen).

7. Solved Questions

Q1. Define sarcomere.

The functional unit of a myofibril, lying between two adjacent Z-lines; it has one A-band and half an I-band on each side.

Q2. Why does the A-band not shorten during contraction?

Myosin filaments keep their length; only actin slides over them. So A-band stays constant while I-band and H-zone shrink.

Q3. Role of Ca²⁺ in contraction?

It binds troponin, which moves tropomyosin and exposes myosin-binding sites on actin.

Q4. Differentiate red and white muscle fibres.

Red: much myoglobin, many mitochondria, aerobic, slow, fatigue-resistant. White: little myoglobin, few mitochondria, anaerobic, fast, tire quickly.
