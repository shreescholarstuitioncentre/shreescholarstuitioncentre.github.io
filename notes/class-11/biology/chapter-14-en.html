🫁 Class 11 Biology – Breathing and Exchange of Gases

1. Respiratory System

Path of air: external nostrils → nasal chamber → pharynx → larynx (sound box; epiglottis covers glottis during swallowing) → trachea → primary bronchi → secondary → tertiary bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli. Trachea is a straight tube supported by C-shaped cartilage rings (keep it open); it divides at the 5th thoracic vertebra into right and left bronchi.

Lungs: two, in the thoracic cavity (air-tight chamber: dorsally vertebral column, ventrally sternum, laterally ribs, below diaphragm). Covered by double-layered pleura with pleural fluid to reduce friction. Right lung 3 lobes, left lung 2 lobes. Conducting part: nostrils to terminal bronchioles (clears, warms, humidifies air). Exchange part: alveoli (thin, irregular, bag-like, highly vascularised) and their ducts. Diffusion membrane (< 1 µm): thin squamous epithelium of alveoli + endothelium of capillary + basement substance between.

2. Mechanism of Breathing

Inspiration (active): diaphragm contracts (flattens) and external intercostal muscles lift ribs and sternum → thoracic volume increases → intra-pulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric → air rushes in (Boyle’s law: P ∝ 1/V). Expiration (usually passive): muscles relax, diaphragm becomes dome-shaped, volume decreases, pressure rises above atmospheric → air out. Extra muscles (abdominal, internal intercostals) work in forced breathing. Normal rate: 12–16/min.

3. Exchange of Gases

Takes place by simple diffusion in alveoli and tissues, based on partial pressure gradients, solubility and membrane thickness.

CO₂ is 20–25 times more soluble than O₂, so even a small gradient (5 mm Hg) moves enough CO₂. Exchange: alveolus ↔ blood, and blood ↔ tissue.

4. Transport of Gases

Oxygen: 97% bound to haemoglobin (oxyhaemoglobin; each Hb carries up to 4 O₂), 3% dissolved in plasma. 1 g Hb binds ~1.34 mL O₂. 100 mL oxygenated blood has ~19.4 mL O₂; tissues release ~5 mL per 100 mL (deoxygenated has ~14.4 mL).
Carbon dioxide: ~70% as bicarbonate: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (enzyme carbonic anhydrase in RBC, plasma has traces; forward in tissues, reverse in alveoli); 20–25% as carbamino-haemoglobin; ~7% dissolved. 100 mL deoxygenated blood delivers ~4 mL CO₂ to alveoli.

Oxygen dissociation curve

Plot of % saturation of Hb vs pO₂ is sigmoid (cooperative binding). High pO₂ (lungs) → loading; low pO₂ (tissues) → unloading. Right shift (more O₂ release) by ↑pCO₂, ↑H⁺ (lower pH), ↑temperature, ↑2,3-BPG – as in active tissues (Bohr effect); left shift by opposite conditions.

5. Regulation of Respiration

Respiratory rhythm centre in medulla sets the basic rhythm. Pneumotaxic centre in pons moderates it (shortens inspiration). A chemosensitive area near the rhythm centre is highly sensitive to CO₂ and H⁺ (↑ → stronger breathing). Receptors in aortic arch and carotid artery also sense CO₂ and H⁺ changes. Role of O₂ is quite insignificant; CO₂ is the main regulator.

6. Disorders

Asthma: difficulty in breathing, wheezing due to inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles (allergens, smoke). Emphysema: chronic disorder where alveolar walls are damaged, reducing respiratory surface; cigarette smoking is the main cause. Occupational respiratory disorders: long exposure to dust (grinding/stone-breaking, silica, asbestos, coal) causes inflammation and fibrosis → serious lung damage (silicosis, asbestosis, black-lung disease). Workers should wear protective masks.

7. Solved Examples

Ex.1 (Easy) TV 500, IRV 2500, ERV 1000. VC = 500 + 2500 + 1000 = 4000 mL.
Ex.2 (Easy) RV 1100 → TLC = 4000 + 1100 = 5100 mL.
Ex.3 (Medium) Minute ventilation at 12/min: 12 × 500 = 6000 mL = 6 L/min.
Ex.4 (Medium, to find) Alveolar ventilation, dead space 150 mL: (500 − 150) × 12 = 4200 mL/min.
Ex.5 (Hard) Max O₂ carried by 15 g Hb per 100 mL: 15 × 1.34 = 20.1 mL.
Ex.6 (Hard) Cardiac output 5 L/min, tissues take 5 mL O₂ per 100 mL: 5000/100 = 50 units × 5 = 250 mL O₂/min.
Ex.7 (Proof) Why CO₂ diffuses as readily as O₂: rate ∝ gradient × solubility. O₂: (104−40) = 64 × 1 = 64. CO₂: (45−40) = 5 × ~20 = 100 → CO₂ diffuses at least as fast.
Ex.8 (Proof, Boyle) Lung volume rises from 2.5 L to 2.6 L at 760 mm Hg: P₂ = (760 × 2.5)/2.6 = 730.8 mm Hg < 760, so air flows in.

8. Short & Long Q&A (tap to open)

Q1. Why does trachea have C-shaped cartilage rings?

To keep the tube open, so air passes freely.

Q2. What is the function of pleural fluid?

Reduces friction between the lung and thoracic wall.

Q3. Differentiate conducting and exchange parts.

Conducting: nostrils to terminal bronchioles (condition air). Exchange: alveoli and ducts, where diffusion occurs.

Q4. Name the layers of diffusion membrane.

Alveolar squamous epithelium, capillary endothelium and basement substance.

Q5. Explain inspiration.

Diaphragm and external intercostals contract; thoracic volume rises; pressure drops below atmospheric and air enters.

Q6. Is expiration active or passive?

Usually passive; forced expiration is active with abdominal and internal intercostal muscles.

Q7. Define TV, IRV, ERV, RV.

500 mL normal breath; extra air inhaled by force; extra air exhaled by force; air left in lungs after forced expiration.

Q8. What is vital capacity?

Maximum air exhaled after forced inspiration: ERV + TV + IRV (3500–4500 mL).

Q9. Why is CO₂ transport more efficient by diffusion?

CO₂ is 20–25 times more soluble than O₂.

Q10. How is O₂ transported?

97% as oxyhaemoglobin, 3% dissolved in plasma.

Q11. Describe CO₂ transport.

70% bicarbonate via carbonic anhydrase, 20–25% carbamino-haemoglobin, 7% dissolved.

Q12. What is Bohr effect?

Right shift of the ODC with ↑pCO₂ / ↓pH, releasing more O₂ to tissues.

Q13. Which factors shift ODC to the right?

High pCO₂, high H⁺, high temperature, high 2,3-BPG.

Q14. Where is respiratory rhythm centre?

Medulla oblongata; moderated by pneumotaxic centre in pons.

Q15. What is asthma?

Inflammation of bronchi/bronchioles causing wheezing and difficult breathing.

Q16. What is emphysema?

Damage to alveolar walls reducing respiratory surface; mainly due to smoking.
