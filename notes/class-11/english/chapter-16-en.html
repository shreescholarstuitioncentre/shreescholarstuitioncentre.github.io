✎ Ranga’s Marriage — Class 11 Snapshots, Ch 3

Author: Masti Venkatesha Iyengar (Masti)  |  Genre: Short story (humorous, social)  |  Setting: Hosahalli, a village in Karnataka

1. Introduction

A light-hearted story about tradition vs modern ideas. Ranga, a young man educated in Bangalore, returns to his village Hosahalli. The villagers expect him to behave like a “modern” man; in fact, by clever planning he wins the bride he wanted, while others think they arranged the match. It satirises child marriage, village gossip and matchmaking customs.

2. Characters

  • Ranga: Young, educated in the city, witty and clever; plans his own marriage while seeming to follow custom.
  • The Narrator: A village man who tells the story and unknowingly helps Ranga's plan; a good-natured, slightly gullible fellow.
  • Shama Shastri: Respected village elder; follows tradition and horoscopes; father of Ratna.
  • Ratna: The girl Ranga marries; modest and obedient.
  • Villagers: Gossipy, curious, traditional; they believe in early marriage and horoscopes.

3. Plot (Summary)

  1. Ranga returns to Hosahalli after studies in Bangalore. The villagers wonder what “town-bred” ideas he has brought.
  2. Ranga tells the narrator that he wishes to marry, and prefers a girl he has seen, unlike the old custom of marrying unseen girls. The narrator is amused.
  3. He slowly builds a situation in which the narrator and Shama Shastri themselves come to suggest Ratna as bride. Horoscopes and elders' approval are used cleverly.
  4. Meanwhile gossip spreads through the village, and the narrator proudly thinks he is arranging a match.
  5. The marriage is fixed and celebrated in traditional style; the narrator finally realises that Ranga's cleverness had directed it all. It ends with gentle humour.

4. Themes

  • Tradition vs modernity: Old customs (child marriage, horoscopes) meet new ideas.
  • Humour and irony: The matchmaker is himself led.
  • Social satire: The story laughs at gossip and rigid customs.
  • Cleverness: Intelligence and tact win without open rebellion.

5. Literary Points

Humour · Irony (the matchmaker is outwitted) · First-person narration · Regional flavour (Karnataka village life) · Satire.

6. Difficult Words

Horoscope – chart used to match couples · Shastri – learned Brahmin priest · Gossip – idle talk · Tradition – long-held custom · Satire – mocking society's faults.

7. Short Q&A

Q1. Who wrote “Ranga's Marriage”?Masti Venkatesha Iyengar.
Q2. Where is the story set?In Hosahalli, a village in Karnataka.
Q3. Who is Ranga?A young man educated in Bangalore who returns to his village and cleverly plans his own marriage.
Q4. What did villagers expect from Ranga?They expected him, as a city-educated man, to bring new habits and perhaps to break village customs.
Q5. Who is Shama Shastri?A respected village elder, devoted to tradition, and Ratna's father.
Q6. Whom did Ranga marry?Ratna, daughter of Shama Shastri.
Q7. Why is the story humorous?Because the narrator thinks he is arranging the match, while Ranga has quietly directed everything.
Q8. What social custom does the story satirise?The custom of arranging marriages by horoscopes and gossip, often at a very young age.

8. Long Q&A

L1. Sketch the character of Ranga. (150 words)Ranga is educated, clever and tactful. Though returning from the city with modern ideas, he does not openly defy village customs. Instead he uses wit and planning so that elders themselves propose his chosen bride. He is humorous, patient and respectful, showing that change can come gently. His marriage ends successfully and everyone is happy, which proves his shrewdness.
L2. How does the story show conflict between tradition and modernity?The village follows horoscopes, elders' decisions and early marriage. Ranga's city education has given him freer ideas. Rather than fight tradition, he works inside it, using its rituals for his purpose. The story thus shows both sides gently and with humour.
L3. Discuss the role of the narrator.The narrator tells the tale in a chatty, proud tone and believes he is the real matchmaker. His innocence creates irony: the reader sees that Ranga is guiding him. The narrator reflects the villagers' attitudes and helps create humour.
