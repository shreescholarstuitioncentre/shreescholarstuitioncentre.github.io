1. About the Author

  • John Updike (1932-2009) was an American novelist, short-story writer, poet and critic. He is known for writing about ordinary middle-class American life, marriage and family.
  • His best-known works are the "Rabbit" novels (Rabbit, Run and its sequels). He won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction twice.
  • His style is precise, observant and full of small domestic details; he shows deep feelings through simple everyday situations.
  • "Should Wizard Hit Mommy?" is one of his well-known short stories, from around 1959. It looks like a simple bedtime story but quietly explores marriage, parenthood, tiredness and the loss of childhood innocence.

2. Background, Setting & Narration

Setting

  • Place: the family's home. Upstairs: Jo's bedroom, where the story is told. Downstairs: the living room, where Clare is moving furniture and painting woodwork.
  • Time: bedtime in the evening, an ordinary night that turns important.
  • Atmosphere: warm and quiet on the surface, but tired, tense and slightly trapped underneath.

Narration

  • Third-person narration, mainly through Jack's thoughts and feelings (close point of view).
  • The story uses two layers: the frame story (Jack, Jo, Clare at home) and the inner story (Roger Skunk, the owl and the wizard).
Understanding the two layers is the key to this chapter: the inner story is a mirror of the outer family situation.

3. Characters

(a) Jack - the father and storyteller

  • Husband of Clare and father of Jo and Bobby; he is tired of his nightly storytelling routine.
  • Has been asked to help paint, and feels pressed by family duties; he is exhausted and a little resentful.
  • Uses stories to escape from reality and to feel in control. Out of old memories of being bullied, he makes Roger a skunk.
  • When Jo's questions interrupt him, he becomes irritated; when she calls Roger's mother stupid, he reacts with unexpected force.
  • Qualities: weary, moody, imaginative, affectionate yet impatient, inwardly conflicted.

(b) Jo - the four-year-old daughter

  • Bright, curious, interrupting and questioning; she is passing through a "reality phase".
  • Asks if magic is real, if the wizard will die, and worries what happens to Roger.
  • Her last demand, that the wizard should hit Roger's mother, shows how a child's sense of justice can be harsh and direct.
  • Qualities: inquisitive, emotional, outspoken, innocent but beginning to question adult authority.

(c) Clare - the wife and mother

  • Six months pregnant with their third child, she is repainting furniture downstairs.
  • She does not appear much but her presence is strong: she stands for the duties of home and family that make Jack feel trapped.

(d) Bobby

Jo's two-year-old brother, already asleep. He shows the family is growing and that Jack's evening duties are repeated daily.

(e) Characters of the inner story

  • Roger Skunk: a little skunk whose smell drives friends away. He is lonely and bullied.
  • The wise owl: advises Roger to go to the wizard.
  • The wizard: an old magician who takes four pennies and gives the skunk the smell of roses.
  • Roger's mother: rejects the new smell and sends him back to be changed into a skunk again. She is happy to kiss him goodnight when he smells like a skunk.

4. The Story-within-a-Story (Roger Skunk)

  1. Jack's usual tale is about an animal with a problem who visits a wise owl, then a wizard who solves it, then plays with friends and reaches home before dinner.
  2. Out of ideas, Jack follows Jo's request for a story about a skunk. Roger's smell makes the other animals avoid him.
  3. The owl sends him to the wizard. Jo keeps urging Jack to hurry and hits him with questions about the wizard.
  4. The wizard accepts four pennies and changes Roger's smell to roses.
  5. The animals now welcome him and play with him.
  6. At home, Roger's mother does not like the new smell and sends him back to be a skunk again. She kisses him goodnight once he smells as before.
  7. Jo worries the animals will avoid him again; Jack says they got used to it.
Think: Is the inner story's message "be yourself", or "give in to your family's expectations"? Updike leaves it open. That open ending is the story's strength.

5. Summary - part by part

Part 1: The bedtime routine

Jack is putting Jo to bed. Bobby is already asleep, and Clare is downstairs painting woodwork and has asked Jack for help. Jack has told the same kind of story every night: an animal with a problem, a wise owl, a wizard who solves it and a happy return home. He is tired and has run out of ideas.

Part 2: Jo asks for a skunk

Jo asks for a story about Roger Skunk, which gives Jack a new idea. He gives Roger a smell problem that keeps all his friends away. As he builds the story, Jack remembers how he himself was bullied as a boy, and a bit of his own feelings enters the tale.

Part 3: Interruptions

Jo interrupts again and again. She mixes up details, urges Jack to take Roger to the wizard quickly, asks whether magic is real, and wants to know how old the wizard is and whether he will die. Jack feels that she is the one controlling the story, not he.

Part 4: The wizard and the roses

The wizard takes four pennies and gives Roger the scent of roses. The animals accept him happily.

Part 5: Jack changes the plot

Roger goes home. Jack now changes the story in an unexpected way: Roger's mother dislikes the new smell and sends him back to be a skunk again. Once the wizard restores the smell, she happily kisses Roger goodnight.

Part 6: Jo's judgement and Jack's reaction

Jo worries that the animals will run away again, and Jack says they got used to it. She calls the mother stupid, and Jack reacts sharply, defending her. Jo then demands a story in which the wizard hits Roger's mother on the head. Jack refuses to give a clear answer.

Part 7: Downstairs

Jack goes downstairs, too worn out to help. Clare remarks how long the story took. He sits and watches her paint, feeling that the household woodwork is like a cage closing around him.

One-line summary: A tired father's bedtime story to his four-year-old daughter reveals his own feelings about family duty, conformity and loss of freedom.

6. Themes & Message

ThemeExplanation
Conformity vs. individualityRoger is accepted only when he changes, yet his mother wants him as he was. Fitting in and being yourself pull in opposite directions.
Duty and entrapment in marriageJack feels the weight of fatherhood and household duties, symbolised by the woodwork "cage".
Loss of childhood innocenceJo moves from believing to questioning magic, and judges adults harshly.
Storytelling and controlJack uses the story to escape and to rule, but Jo challenges his control.
Parent-child relationshipParents and children don't always understand each other; each teaches the other something.
Bullying and acceptanceRoger's loneliness echoes Jack's own childhood experience.
Message: Even a small bedtime story can reveal big truths about family, freedom and the pressure to conform. Adults and children both struggle with who they are and what is expected of them.

7. Title, Irony, Symbols & Style

The title

  • The title is the question Jo asks at the end: should the wizard hit Roger's mother?
  • It shows a child's blunt idea of justice and also hints at violence, anger and resentment hidden in family life.
  • Jack avoids a clear answer, which is why the question stays with the reader.

Irony

  • A story meant to calm a child instead stirs up the father's own frustrations.
  • The skunk is accepted when he smells of roses, yet his mother rejects him for it: the "better" Roger is less welcome at home.
  • The narrator wants to control the tale, but the little listener ends up controlling him.

Symbols

  • The smell: identity, what makes us different.
  • The wizard: a magical fix, the wish to escape problems; also an authority figure.
  • The woodwork / cage: the confining routine of family life.
  • Four pennies: the small price of a big change.

Style

Simple conversational language, short dialogue, realistic domestic detail, humour mixed with sadness, and an open ending.

10. Solved Examples (step by step)

(This is an English literature chapter, so the "examples" are worked analyses with step-by-step solutions.)

Example 1 (Easy). Why was Jack tired of the bedtime story?
Step 1: He tells a story every night. Step 2: The plot has become repetitive. Step 3: He is also needed downstairs to help paint. Result: He is exhausted by routine and duty.
Example 2 (Intermediate). Show that the inner story reflects Jack's feelings.
To prove: the tale mirrors Jack.
Evidence 1: Roger is bullied, and Jack remembers being bullied. Evidence 2: Roger is accepted only by changing; Jack feels forced to fit family roles. Evidence 3: The mother's rejection of change matches Jack's own mixed feelings about expectation. Conclusion: The inner story is an outlet for Jack's emotions.
Example 3 (Hard). Explain why Jack refuses to answer whether the wizard should hit Mommy.
Step 1: Jo's question about hitting a mother touches Jack's own anger at women/duty. Step 2: If he says yes, he approves violence; if no, he must defend authority. Step 3: Earlier he sharply defended Roger's mother, which surprised him. Step 4: Not answering protects him from facing his feelings. Conclusion: The silence shows his inner conflict and gives the story an open ending.
