✎ Memories of Childhood

Class 12 English (Flamingo, Prose) • Part I: Zitkala-Sa • Part II: Bama

Overall Theme: Both authors recall childhood experiences of cultural humiliation and discrimination. Zitkala-Sa, a Native American girl, loses her tradition when her hair is cut at a white-run boarding school. Bama, a Dalit girl in India, faces untouchability. Both finally resist and show strength.

PART I — The Cutting of My Long Hair

1. About the Author

Zitkala-Sa (Red Bird), also known as Gertrude Simmons Bonnin (1876–1938), belonged to the Native American Yankton Dakota Sioux tribe. She was a writer, musician and activist who fought for Native American rights. This is from her autobiographical writing about her days at a boarding school run by missionaries.

2. Background

White missionaries took Native American children to boarding schools to "civilise" them, i.e. to make them give up their tribal culture, dress, language and customs. Their long hair, a sign of pride, was cut.

3. Summary (Step by Step)

Step 1: The first day at school is the third day in the land of apples. The writer is overwhelmed by the bell, the noise, the strange clothes and the rules; she is nervous at the dining hall.

Step 2: At the first breakfast she is confused by the ringing bells, the chairs, and the strange customs (like bowing heads, knives and forks). She feels shy and awkward and is confused when a boy's prayer begins.

Step 3: She is miserable, and her friend Judewin, who understands some English, warns her that they are going to cut their hair. Judewin says that only unruly girls have shingled hair, yet it will be done to them.

Step 4: The writer is full of fear and decides that she will resist: "I will not submit! I will struggle first!" She slips out and hides under a bed in a large room upstairs.

Step 5: She is found, dragged out kicking and scratching, and tied to a chair. She cries and resists, but is helpless.

Step 6: Her long hair is cut. She feels she has lost her spirit and is like a small animal driven by a herder. She is among strangers and cannot share her pain.

4. Diagram — Events (Animated)

5. Themes & Points

Loss of identity and culture: In her tribe only mourners and cowards had short hair, so hair was dignity. Cruelty of "civilising": the school forces uniformity. Resistance: hiding and fighting show spirit even when it fails. Helplessness: a child alone among strangers. Language: "shingled hair", "the land of apples" and "herder" convey her feelings.

PART II — We Too Are Human Beings

1. About the Author

Bama (Faustina Mary Fatima Rani, b. 1958) is a Tamil Dalit writer and teacher. Her autobiography Karukku is a landmark of Dalit literature. This extract describes caste discrimination as she saw it in her childhood.

2. Summary (Step by Step)

Step 1: In the third class, Bama walks home slowly from school, enjoying the shops, the street scenes, the performances, the fruit-sellers and the festival sights. A 10-minute walk takes her up to an hour.

Step 2: One day she sees an elderly man from her community walking along, carrying a packet of food (vadai) by its string, without touching it. The landlord's man is seated nearby.

Step 3: She finds it comical, as the man holds the packet by a string, bending, and gives it to the upper-caste person without touching it.

Step 4: At home her elder brother (Annan) explains that the elder is carrying it that way because "we are untouchables", and the upper castes believe that if they touch it, it will be polluted.

Step 5: Bama feels sad and angry and wants to touch the parcel and snatch it. She is outraged that people so respectable are demeaned just because of their birth.

Step 6: Annan advises her to study hard and make progress, since education brings respect and dignity. Bama takes it to heart, studies hard and stands first in her class. Many people become friendly with her.

3. Diagram — Events (Animated)

4. Themes & Points

Untouchability and caste: humiliation by birth. Education as empowerment: the way to dignity and self-respect. Child's perspective: innocence turning into awareness and anger. Resistance: Bama chooses hard work over despair.

Q&A

Q1. Why was Zitkala-Sa upset at the thought of hair-cutting?

In her culture only cowards and mourners had short hair, so it meant loss of dignity and tradition.

Q2. Who was Judewin?

A friend of the writer who knew English and warned her of the hair-cutting.

Q3. Where did she hide?

Under a bed in a large upstairs room.

Q4. How does she describe herself after the hair was cut?

As one of many little animals driven by a herder, having lost her spirit.

Q5. Why did the old man carry the packet by a string?

Because of untouchability; he was not allowed to touch what the upper-caste landlord would eat.

Q6. What did Annan advise Bama?

To study hard and make progress, because education would bring respect.

Q7. How did Bama respond to the advice?

She studied hard, stood first in class and many people became friendly with her.

Q8 (Long). Discuss the title "Memories of Childhood".

Both extracts are autobiographical memories of childhood, in which children suddenly meet prejudice. The memories shaped the writers' adult voices as activists. The title links two cultures and two kinds of injustice.

Q9 (Long). How do the two authors react to discrimination?

Zitkala-Sa fights with all her might but is overpowered, and feels broken. Bama is first amused, then angry, but turns her anger into determination to study. Both show the child's courage.
