1. About the Author

  • Pearl Sydenstricker Buck (1892-1973) was an American novelist. She was born in West Virginia, USA, but grew up in China because her parents were missionaries.
  • She understood Asian life and culture very well, so many of her books are set in Asia. Her famous novel is The Good Earth (it won the Pulitzer Prize).
  • She won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1938.
  • Her main concern as a writer: humanity, kindness, and understanding between different races and nations.
  • "The Enemy" shows exactly this: a Japanese doctor treats an American enemy soldier, proving that human compassion is greater than national hatred.

2. Background, Setting & Time

Time

The story is set during World War II, when Japan and America were enemies. Hatred against the opposite nation was very high.

Place

  • A quiet house on the coast of Japan, built on rocks that rise from the sea.
  • A small private beach in front of the house. It is the place where the wounded man is found.
  • Inside: the operating room in the house, the kitchen, and the bedroom where the patient is kept.
  • Nearby: the city where General Takima lives, and the hospital where Sadao works.

Mood

Tense, suspenseful, and finally hopeful. Fear of the government and neighbours always hangs over the characters.

The war setting is NOT just a background. It creates the central conflict: patriotism (duty to country) versus humanity (duty as a doctor and as a human being).

3. Characters

(a) Dr. Sadao Hoki - the protagonist

  • A famous Japanese surgeon and scientist, trained in America.
  • He was working on an important discovery to help the healing of wounds, so he was not sent to the war front.
  • He also served as the personal physician of the old and powerful General Takima.
  • Qualities: skilful, intelligent, duty-bound, kind-hearted but cautious, honest, practical.
  • Inner conflict: as a Japanese citizen he should hand the man over; as a doctor he cannot let a wounded man die. His doctor's instinct wins.
  • He does not want to appear a hero; he keeps saying he is only doing what a doctor must do.

(b) Hana - Sadao's wife

  • Loyal, loving, brave and sensitive; she is also educated in America.
  • She first shows a natural feeling of fear and hatred towards the enemy, but when she sees the man's helplessness her kindness appears.
  • She supports her husband and helps in the operation, even when the servants run away.
  • Qualities: dutiful, devoted, courageous, compassionate, practical in a crisis.

(c) The American prisoner (Tom)

  • A young American soldier who has escaped from a prison camp and is wounded (shot in the back).
  • He is washed up on the beach near Sadao's home. He is weak, frightened and thankful.
  • He represents "the enemy" in the title, yet he is only a human in pain.

(d) General Takima

  • An old, powerful and ill Japanese General. He depends on Sadao's medical care.
  • He is stern, but he proves forgetful of the threat he himself made. This forgetfulness lets Sadao off safely.

(e) The servants

  • The old gatekeeper, the cook and the maid Yumi (the household staff).
  • They are full of hatred and prejudice against the enemy and refuse to serve the wounded man, and finally leave the house.
  • They represent the common people poisoned by war propaganda.

4. Story Summary (part by part)

Part 1: The discovery on the beach

Dr. Sadao Hoki stands at the window of his house looking at the sea. He sees something dark moving on the rocks below, and when he goes down with Hana, they find a wounded white man lying face-down on the beach. He is bleeding from a bullet wound in the back. Sadao realises he is an American, an enemy. He faces a dilemma: if he reports it, the man will be taken away and probably killed; if he saves him, he may be called a traitor.

Part 2: The dilemma

Sadao is a doctor and cannot leave a person to die. He is also a loyal Japanese citizen who is afraid of the government. After thinking, he decides to take the man to the house. They carry him in with great effort. Hana helps but is also afraid. The man says he is a prisoner who escaped.

Part 3: The operation

Sadao decides to operate. He asks the servants for help, but they are shocked and unwilling to serve a white enemy. Hana brings water and helps. Sadao performs the surgery and removes the bullet successfully. The man is kept in the house while he recovers.

Part 4: Servants desert

The servants leave in disgust, because they cannot bear the idea of caring for an enemy. Hana has to do all household work as well as nurse the patient. The difficulty tests the couple's resolve.

Part 5: Telling the General

Sadao is afraid of being found out and so he decides to tell General Takima himself. The General listens and promises to take care of the matter quietly by sending private assassins to kill the prisoner at night. This makes Sadao's situation worse: he saved the man, and now the man will be murdered.

Part 6: Waiting in fear

The couple wait for the assassins every night. The nights pass; nobody comes. Days go by and the man grows stronger. Sadao is puzzled and tense. Later it becomes clear that the General had simply forgotten about the matter.

Part 7: The escape

Because the prisoner could not stay hidden forever, Sadao and Hana decide to help him escape. Sadao gives him food and a small boat and tells him how to reach a nearby island, where he can be picked up by a passing ship. A signal light is arranged to guide him. The man leaves, and Sadao watches the dark sea.

Part 8: The ending

After the escape Sadao feels a mixed relief and uncertainty. The General, when Sadao speaks to him later, shows he has forgotten the whole affair. Sadao is saved, and so is the prisoner. The story ends on a quiet thought that the man is gone but humanity has won.

One-line summary: A Japanese doctor saves an enemy American soldier at great personal risk, and in the end humanity triumphs over hatred and war.

5. Themes & Message

ThemeExplanation
Humanity above nationalismSadao treats a wounded man as a patient, not as an enemy. Compassion crosses borders.
Duty vs. conscienceLoyalty to country conflicts with the doctor's oath to save life; conscience wins.
Prejudice and hatredThe servants' behaviour shows how war creates blind racial hatred.
CourageSadao and Hana risk their reputation, safety and lives.
The futility of warOrdinary people suffer; enemy soldiers are young men like any others.
Professional ethicsA doctor's first duty is to heal, irrespective of religion or nationality.
Message: Kindness and humanity are above nations, races and wars. Every person, even an enemy, deserves help when in need.

6. Title, Irony & Literary Devices

Why "The Enemy"?

  • The American is called "the enemy" by the servants, the General and the society.
  • But in the end the title becomes ironic: the so-called enemy is helpless and harmless, while the real enemies are hatred, prejudice and war.
  • So the title works on two levels: literal (enemy soldier) and symbolic (narrow-mindedness).

Literary devices

  • Irony: the General, who threatens the prisoner, forgets him; a Japanese doctor saves an American.
  • Suspense: the nights spent waiting for assassins.
  • Symbolism: the sea stands for the barrier of war and also for hope and escape; the light signal stands for hope.
  • Contrast: Sadao's humanity vs. servants' hatred; duty vs. conscience.
  • Simple, direct narration: third-person narrator, clean language, vivid description.

9. Solved Examples

(Note: this is an English literature chapter, so the "examples" are given as worked-out analyses with step-by-step solutions.)

Example 1 (Easy). Character: Why is Sadao not sent to war?
Step 1: He is a famous surgeon and scientist. Step 2: He is working on a valuable discovery related to wounds. Step 3: He is also the personal doctor of General Takima. Result: The country values him more at home than on the battlefield.
Example 2 (Intermediate). Show that Sadao is a man of conflict.
To prove: inner conflict.
Evidence 1: He wants to save the man as a doctor. Evidence 2: He fears being called a traitor. Evidence 3: He tells the General but is afraid of the outcome. Evidence 4: He risks everything again by helping the man escape.
Conclusion: His struggle between duty and humanity runs through the whole story; humanity wins.
Example 3 (Hard). Justify that the title is ironic.
Step 1 (literal): The American is called the enemy. Step 2 (reality): He is helpless, wounded and young. Step 3 (contrast): The people who act harshly (servants, assassin plan) are driven by hatred. Step 4: The true enemy is prejudice and war itself. Conclusion: The title carries layered irony.
