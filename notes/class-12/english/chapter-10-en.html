An Elementary School Classroom in a Slum – Stephen Spender (Notes)

Poem: An Elementary School Classroom in a Slum  |  Poet: Stephen Spender (1909–1995)  |  Book: Flamingo, Class 12 English (Poetry, Ch.2)

1. About the Poet

Stephen Spender was an English poet, essayist and critic, one of the 1930s Auden group of socially conscious poets. He wrote about poverty, injustice and the need for change. Famous works: Poems, The Still Centre, World Within World (autobiography). He served as Poet Laureate Consultant in Poetry to the Library of Congress (1965).

2. Central Idea (≈150 words)

The poem is a powerful protest against poverty and an unjust education system. Spender describes a gloomy classroom in a slum where thin, sick and hopeless children sit with bent heads, inheriting disease and misery. Pictures of Shakespeare, a world map and a beautiful valley hang on the walls, but they belong to a rich world far from the children’s lives, so they become a cruel temptation instead of inspiration. One boy dreams of a squirrel playing in the trees, showing that hope for freedom still lives. In the last stanza, the poet speaks to the governor, inspector and visitor, urging them to act. He wants the map to become a window to the world, so that children can leave the slum, see green fields, sunshine and history. The poem calls for real education, social reform and equal opportunity for every child.

3. Stanza-wise Explanation

Stanza 1 – The children

The poet describes the faces of the children: stunted, tired, hopeless. They are like rootless weeds, a tall girl bends her head in shame, a thin boy looks like paper with frightened eyes, another inherits his father’s disease, and a dreamy boy thinks of a squirrel’s game outside.

Stanza 2 – The classroom walls

The walls show Shakespeare’s portrait, a map and a beautiful valley. The poet ironically calls them wicked and bad, because they show a bright world these children cannot reach, so they tempt them to dream of what is impossible.

Stanza 3 – Map and the world

The map gives the world to others but, to these children, their ‘world’ is only a dark slum with narrow streets. The sky, sun and sea outside their classroom are far away from their reality.

Stanza 4 – The appeal

The poet appeals to the governor, inspector and visitor: let the map become the children’s window; free them from the slum; give them green fields, sun and a chance to make history.

4. Animated Diagram (Classroom)

Explanation: The bright window and sun (glowing) mean hope and a better world. The bent-head child (slowly swaying) shows weariness. The map and the Shakespeare picture show a rich culture far from their lives. The poem asks to connect the classroom to the sun.

7. Theme, Tone, Message

Themes: poverty, injustice, education, hope. Tone: sad, ironic, then urgent. Message: Real education must free the poor; the powerful must act to change society.

8. Short and Long Q&A (click to open)

Q1. Where is the poem set?

In a gloomy elementary school classroom in a slum, filled with poor, undernourished children.

Q2. Why does the poet call the children 'unnatural'?

Because poverty and hunger have made them look weak and unlike healthy, lively children; they seem like faded weeds without roots.

Q3. What do 'rootless weeds' suggest?

Weeds without roots have no support or nourishment; the slum children lack family, food, education, security and a future.

Q4. Describe the boy with the 'tall girl’s' weighed-down head.

A tall girl sits with her head bent low, heavy with shame, tiredness and hopelessness, showing a spirit crushed by poverty.

Q5. Who is the 'paper-seeming boy with rat’s eyes'?

A thin, pale boy whose body looks like paper, and whose small, frightened eyes show fear and hunger like a rat’s.

Q6. What does the stunted boy inherit?

A twisted, weak body and a father’s disease: his poverty and illness are handed down as his legacy.

Q7. What is the boy at the back dreaming of?

A little boy sits at the back, lost in thoughts of a squirrel’s game in the trees; he longs for freedom and a happy life outside.

Q8. Why is his dreaming important?

It shows that even in suffering, a child’s mind seeks freedom, joy and nature; hope hasn’t died.

Q9. What is on the classroom wall?

A portrait of Shakespeare, a map of the world, and a picture of a Tyrolese valley, all symbols of a rich culture, far from the children’s lives.

Q10. Why does the poet say Shakespeare is 'wicked' and the map a 'bad example'?

Ironically: these show beauty and opportunity that the slum children can never reach, so they tempt them to dream of what they cannot have.

Q11. What is the irony in the poem?

Education that should free them only reminds them of what they lack, because it is disconnected from their life.

Q12. What does the map stand for?

The world of opportunity, travel, knowledge and freedom; the children’s 'world' is just the slum.

Q13. What is the meaning of 'sun' in the poem?

Sun symbolises light, hope, warmth, knowledge and a better life, which are missing in the dark classroom.

Q14. What do the windows signify in the last stanza?

The poet wants the map to become a window through which children see the world; education should open their eyes.

Q15. Whom does the poet address in the last stanza?

The governor, inspector and visitor: people of power and authority who can change the system.

Q16. What does the poet demand from them?

To free the children from slums by giving them real education, nutrition and opportunities, so that they enjoy green fields, sun and history.

Q17. What does 'History is theirs whose language is the sun' mean?

Those who have light, knowledge and freedom make history; the poor must be given these to take their rightful place.

Q18. What is the central theme of the poem?

Social injustice, the poverty of slum children, and the need for meaningful education and reform.

Q19. What type of poem is it?

A free-verse poem of protest and social reform, in four stanzas, rich in imagery and irony.

Q20. Name the poetic devices.

Simile, metaphor, imagery, irony, symbolism, personification, alliteration, contrast, apostrophe.

Q21. What is the tone of the poem?

Sad and critical at first, and then urgent and hopeful, with a call for action.

Q22. Who is Stephen Spender?

An English poet (1909–1995), associated with the 1930s Left-wing poets; he wrote about social injustice and the plight of the poor.

Q23. What does the title show?

The poem exposes a school meant to teach, but set in a slum with deprived, hopeless surroundings.

Q24. What message does the poem give?

Education should connect with children’s lives; society must remove poverty to give every child dignity and equal chances.

9. Long Answer Questions

Q1. Describe the condition of the children in the slum classroom (120 words).

The children are weak, stunted and underfed, like rootless weeds. A tall girl bends her head with shame; a paper-thin boy has rat-like frightened eyes; another boy inherits his father’s disease. Only one boy dreams of a squirrel’s game, showing a hidden wish for freedom. Their faces show hunger, fear and hopelessness, the result of poverty and neglect.

Q2. Why does the poet call Shakespeare wicked and the map a bad example?

It is irony. Shakespeare and the map symbolise culture, knowledge and the wide world. But they have no connection with the children’s lives, so instead of helping, they remind the poor of what they lack and tempt them to dream of the impossible.

Q3. Explain the last stanza of the poem.

The poet appeals to authority to make the map a window for the children, free them from the slum, and give them sun, green fields and education. He believes history belongs to those with light and knowledge, so the poor must get them too.
