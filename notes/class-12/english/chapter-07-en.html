The Interview

Part I: Christopher Silvester | Part II: An Interview with Umberto Eco - Class 12 English (Flamingo, Prose Ch.7)

Chapter Summary

Part I by Christopher Silvester explains that the interview is a major source of information and a literary art form. It began with Horace Greeley's 1859 interview of Brigham Young. Some writers like Kipling, Wells, Bellow and Naipaul disliked interviews, while journalists value them. Part II is Mukund Padmanabhan's interview with Umberto Eco, a scholar and novelist who uses 'interstices' (empty gaps of time) to write, and who describes his work and The Name of the Rose.

Short Answer Q&A (tap to open)

Q1. Who wrote Part I of 'The Interview'?

Christopher Silvester, from his work The Penguin Book of Interviews.

Q2. Why is the interview important in journalism?

It is a vital source of information and opinion and is the most commonly used form of communication in the media. It has become a major art form and is also a way to learn about people and the world.

Q3. Who gave the first interview in journalism?

Horace Greeley of the New York Tribune interviewed Brigham Young, the Mormon leader, in 1859, and it is often called the first interview.

Q4. What did V. S. Naipaul say about interviews?

He said that interviews hurt the subject, and that some people are wounded by them and 'something' is taken away.

Q5. Why did Rudyard Kipling hate interviews?

He thought they were an offence, a crime and an immoral act, like assault, and he refused to grant interviews.

Q6. Who is Umberto Eco?

An Italian scholar, professor of semiotics at Bologna University and a famous novelist, best known for The Name of the Rose.

Q7. Who interviewed Eco?

Mukund Padmanabhan of The Hindu, in the interview section of the chapter.

Q8. What are 'interstices' in Eco's life?

Empty spaces between daily activities, such as waiting, which Eco uses for writing and ideas, and which let him do creative work.

Long Answer Q&A

Q9. Why does Silvester say the interview is a major art form?

The interview is a major route to information and opinions from celebrities and the common people. Over time its form changed from simple Q&A to thoughtful conversation that tells about both people. Good interviews bring out a person's mind. Readers enjoy them, and writers use them to enter the lives of important people. They are a popular form of writing in print, radio and television.

Q10. What are the views for and against interviews in Part I?

Some celebrated writers disliked interviews. Kipling thought them an offence. H. G. Wells found them an ordeal, Saul Bellow compared them to having his pocket picked and Naipaul felt they wound the subject. Others like Lewis Carroll avoided them. But journalists find interviews important in giving a human face to news, and many writers accepted them as useful. Thus there are two sides: art and intrusion.

Q11. How does Eco manage to be both scholar and novelist?

Eco says he has a day job as a professor, and uses empty spaces or interstices in his daily life to write. He explains that one can use the time spent waiting or travelling. He did not plan to be a novelist; he wrote The Name of the Rose later in life. He believes his scholarly and fictional works are connected and that one reaches for pleasure and for work in both.

Given: Eco speaks of 'interstices'. To find: What it shows. Solution: Step 1: He has a busy life as professor. Step 2: He uses gaps to write. Step 3: Small spare moments add up. Step 4: It shows discipline and creativity are possible by managing time.
