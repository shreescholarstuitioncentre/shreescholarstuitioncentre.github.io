My Mother at Sixty-six – Kamala Das (Notes)

Poem: My Mother at Sixty-six  |  Poet: Kamala Das (1934–2009)  |  Book: Flamingo, Class 12 English (Poetry, Ch.1)

1. About the Poet

Kamala Das (pen name Madhavikutty) was a bilingual Indian writer, Malayalam and English. She wrote honest, confessional poetry about love, women’s feelings and ageing. Famous works: Summer in Calcutta, The Descendants, My Story (autobiography). She won the Sahitya Akademi award for her Malayalam work.

2. Central Idea (≈150 words)

The poem expresses a daughter’s deep love and fear as she watches her ageing mother. While driving to Cochin airport, the poet sees her sixty-six-year-old mother sleeping beside her, pale and lifeless like a corpse. This sight stirs an old childhood fear of losing her. To escape the painful thought, she looks outside, where young trees seem to race past and cheerful children come out of their homes. The sharp contrast between youth and old age highlights the inevitability of ageing and death. At the airport, after the security check, she looks again at her mother, who appears pale and faded like the winter moon, and the fear returns. Yet the poet hides her anxiety, smiles, and says 'see you soon, Amma', hoping to reassure her mother and herself. The poem shows the bond of love, the pain of separation, and a child’s helplessness before time.

3. Stanza-wise Explanation

Part 1 – In the car (opening lines)

The poet drives out of her parents’ house. Her mother, sixty-six, dozes with her mouth open; her face is ashen like a corpse. The poet is hurt by the sight.

Part 2 – Looking outside

She turns her eyes to the world outside. Young trees seem to run backwards; children burst out of their houses. This shows life, speed and energy. The contrast makes the mother’s decline more painful.

Part 3 – At the airport

After the security check, she looks at her mother again: pale, withered like a late winter’s moon. Her old fear of losing her mother returns.

Part 4 – Parting

She puts on a smile and says 'see you soon, Amma'. The repeated smiles show courage and a mask over her pain.

4. Animated Diagram (car journey and contrast)

Explanation: The red car moves; trees move past (youth, speed, life). Inside, the mother’s pale face (age, stillness). Moon = fading, cold life. This contrast is the soul of the poem.

7. Theme, Tone, Message

Themes: ageing, mortality, mother–daughter love, fear of separation. Tone: anxious, tender, sad. Message: Time is unstoppable; cherish loved ones while they are with us.

8. Short and Long Q&A (click to open)

Q1. Where was the poet going and with whom was she driving?

Kamala Das was driving to Cochin (Kochi) airport with her mother, after visiting her parents’ home, to catch a flight back.

Q2. How did the poet’s mother look?

She sat beside the poet, dozing, her mouth open. Her face was pale and ashen, like a corpse, as lifeless as the late-winter moon.

Q3. Why does the poet compare her mother’s face to a corpse?

To show how old, drained and frail she looked; it also exposes the poet’s deep fear that she may lose her soon.

Q4. What is the effect of the simile 'late winter’s moon'?

Winter moon is cold, dim and faint. It suggests colourless, fading life in old age, a life near its end.

Q5. What did the poet do when the painful thought struck her?

She looked away from her mother, out of the window, to push the thought out of her mind.

Q6. What did she see outside?

Young trees seeming to sprint past the car, and happy children spilling out of their homes.

Q7. What is the contrast in the poem?

Outside: youth, speed, joy, life. Inside the car: age, stillness, pain, approaching death.

Q8. Which figure of speech is 'young trees sprinting'?

Personification (trees given a human action); also a visual image of the car’s speed.

Q9. What does 'spilling out' suggest about the children?

Their joyful, uncontrolled energy, like water overflowing a vessel.

Q10. What happened at the airport?

After the security check, the poet looked at her mother again and saw her pale, faded and wan, like a late winter’s moon.

Q11. Why did the poet look at her mother again after the check?

She wanted a final look, and the same fear returned strongly.

Q12. What did the poet say and do while parting?

She smiled and said 'see you soon, Amma', hiding her fear, trying to cheer her mother.

Q13. Why does she repeat 'smiling'?

Repetition shows an effort to convince herself and her mother that all is well, though she is anxious.

Q14. What is the theme of the poem?

Fear of losing an ageing parent, the pain of watching old age, and the love between mother and daughter.

Q15. Name the poetic devices used.

Simile, personification, imagery, contrast, repetition, enjambment, free verse, colloquial diction.

Q16. What is the tone of the poem?

Sad, anxious, tender, and finally quietly hopeful but troubled.

Q17. Which stanza/part shows a child’s fear?

The memory of 'my childhood fear' of losing mother; the poet suddenly feels like a child afraid of separation.

Q18. Why is the poem called 'My Mother at Sixty-six'?

It focuses on her mother at age 66, an age of physical decline, and the poet’s feelings about it.

Q19. What is the poem’s form?

A free-verse, confessional poem of 15 lines with no regular rhyme, in simple everyday language.

Q20. Who is Amma?

'Amma' means mother in Malayalam and other Indian languages; the poet is addressing her mother.

9. Long Answer Questions

Q1. Describe the poet’s feelings in 'My Mother at Sixty-six' (120–150 words).

The poet feels deep love, anxiety and helplessness. In the car, she is shocked by her mother’s pale, corpse-like face and recalls her childhood fear of losing her. To avoid pain, she looks outside and sees lively trees and children, which sharpens the contrast with her mother’s age. At the airport, the same fear returns when she sees her mother like a faded winter moon. Still, she smiles and says 'see you soon, Amma' to hide her pain and comfort her mother. Thus, the poem shows love mixed with fear of death.

Q2. Explain the contrast between the inside and outside of the car.

Outside: sprinting young trees and joyful children symbolise youth, energy and life. Inside: the dozing, ashen-faced mother symbolises old age, weakness and approaching death. The poet uses this contrast to deepen sadness and show life’s cycle.

Q3. Why does the poet smile at the end? What does it show?

The smile is a mask to hide her fear and to reassure her mother. It shows her courage, tenderness and emotional control, though inside she is anxious.
