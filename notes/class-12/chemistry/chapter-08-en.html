d- and f-Block Elements – Class 12 Chemistry, Ch. 8

8.1 The Transition Elements (d-Block)

Definition: elements which have an incompletely filled d-subshell in their atoms or in any of their common oxidation states. Four series: 3d (Sc–Zn), 4d (Y–Cd), 5d (La, Hf–Hg), 6d (Ac, Rf–Cn). Zn, Cd, Hg have d¹⁰ in atom and in +2 state, so strictly they are not transition elements (but studied with d-block).

Electronic configuration

General: (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns¹⁻². Exceptions: Cr = 3d⁵4s¹ and Cu = 3d¹⁰4s¹ (extra stability of half-filled and fully-filled d-subshell). Ions are formed by loss of 4s electrons first, then 3d.

General properties

1. Metallic character & physical: hard, lustrous, good conductors, high mp/bp (except Zn, Cd, Hg) because of strong metallic bonding by both ns and unpaired (n−1)d electrons. Melting point rises to a maximum near the middle (Cr, and tungsten W 3683 K highest) and then falls as d⁵ electrons pair. Enthalpy of atomisation is high (high energy to form free atoms).
2. Atomic radii: decrease from Sc to Cr, remain nearly constant (Mn to Cu) because added d-electrons shield the nuclear charge, and increase slightly at Zn due to repulsion. 4d and 5d radii are almost same (Zr 160, Hf 159 pm) due to lanthanoid contraction.
3. Ionisation enthalpy: increases slowly across the series (low variation) as (n−1)d electrons shield. Irregularities: high IE₂ for Cr and Cu (stable d⁵, d¹⁰ after losing one electron).
4. Oxidation states: variable because (n−1)d and ns energies are close; successive states differ by one unit (Mn: +2 to +7) unlike p-block (differ by two). Common +2 (loss of 4s²); highest at Mn (+7, all 4s and 3d electrons) in oxides and oxoanions; OsO₄ shows +8. Higher states in fluorides and oxides (strongly electronegative), lower states with ligands like CO.
5. Electrode potentials: E°(M²⁺/M) mostly negative except Cu (+0.34 V) as its high atomisation enthalpy is not offset by hydration enthalpy. Cr²⁺ is reducing (Cr³⁺/Cr²⁺ = −0.41 V); Mn³⁺ and Co³⁺ are strong oxidisers (+1.57, +1.97 V); Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ = +0.77 V.
6. Magnetic behaviour: unpaired electrons give paramagnetism; spin-only magnetic moment μ = √[n(n+2)] BM (n = unpaired electrons). Diamagnetic if all paired.
7. Coloured ions: due to d–d transition: electron absorbs a part of visible light to jump to a higher d level in the field of ligands, and the complementary colour is seen. Sc³⁺, Ti⁴⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu⁺ are colourless (d⁰ or d¹⁰). Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ green, Mn²⁺ pink, Ni²⁺ green, Co²⁺ pink.
8. Complex formation: small size, high charge, vacant d-orbitals. 9. Catalytic property: variable oxidation states and surface adsorption (Fe in Haber, V₂O₅ in contact, Ni in hydrogenation, MnO₂ in KClO₃ decomposition). 10. Interstitial compounds: small atoms (H, C, N) occupy voids (TiC, Fe₃H, Mn₄N): hard, high mp, chemically inert, conduct electricity. 11. Alloys: similar radii allow mixing: brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn), steel. 12. Non-stoichiometric compounds (Fe₀.₉₄O).

Oxides of manganese

MnO (basic), Mn₃O₄, Mn₂O₃, MnO₂ (amphoteric), Mn₂O₇ (acidic). Acidic nature increases with the oxidation state. Similarly CrO (basic) < Cr₂O₃ (amphoteric) < CrO₃ (acidic).

Potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇)

Preparation: from chromite ore FeCr₂O₄: 4FeCr₂O₄ + 8Na₂CO₃ + 7O₂ → 8Na₂CrO₄ + 2Fe₂O₃ + 8CO₂ (fusion with Na₂CO₃ in excess air). Acidify: 2Na₂CrO₄ + 2H⁺ → Na₂Cr₂O₇ + 2Na⁺ + H₂O. Then Na₂Cr₂O₇ + 2KCl → K₂Cr₂O₇ + 2NaCl (orange crystals separate as K₂Cr₂O₇ is less soluble).
Chromate–dichromate equilibrium: 2CrO₄²⁻ + 2H⁺ ⇌ Cr₂O₇²⁻ + H₂O. Yellow in alkaline, orange in acidic solution; the change is pH dependent (not redox).

Structure: chromate ion is tetrahedral; dichromate has two tetrahedra sharing one corner O (Cr–O–Cr angle 126°).
Oxidising nature: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O (E° = 1.33 V). Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6I⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 3I₂ + 7H₂O; Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6Fe²⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 6Fe³⁺ + 7H₂O; Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 3H₂S + 8H⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 3S + 7H₂O; Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 3Sn²⁺ + 14H⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 3Sn⁴⁺ + 7H₂O. Colour change orange → green (Cr³⁺). Uses: primary standard in volumetric analysis, leather tanning, dyes, photography, chromyl chloride test.

Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄)

Preparation: from pyrolusite MnO₂: 2MnO₂ + 4KOH + O₂ → 2K₂MnO₄ + 2H₂O (green manganate on fusion). Oxidation: 3MnO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ → 2MnO₄⁻ + MnO₂ + 2H₂O (disproportionation in acid); commercially by electrolytic oxidation of manganate in alkaline solution.
Properties: dark purple crystals; heating at 513 K: 2KMnO₄ → K₂MnO₄ + MnO₂ + O₂. MnO₄⁻ is tetrahedral and diamagnetic; MnO₄²⁻ is green and paramagnetic (d¹).
Oxidising nature depends on medium: acidic: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O (colourless Mn²⁺); neutral/weakly alkaline: MnO₄⁻ + 2H₂O + 3e⁻ → MnO₂ + 4OH⁻ (brown); strongly alkaline: MnO₄⁻ + e⁻ → MnO₄²⁻.

Reactions in acid medium: 5Fe²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → 5Fe³⁺ + Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O; 2MnO₄⁻ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O (warm, ~333 K); 2MnO₄⁻ + 10I⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5I₂ + 8H₂O; 2MnO₄⁻ + 5H₂S + 6H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5S + 8H₂O. In neutral medium: 2MnO₄⁻ + H₂O + I⁻ → 2MnO₂ + 2OH⁻ + IO₃⁻. Why H₂SO₄ is used for acidification? HCl would be oxidised to Cl₂ and HNO₃ itself is an oxidant. Uses: titrations (self-indicator), disinfectant (Condy's fluid), bleaching, Baeyer's reagent (cold dilute alkaline KMnO₄ for unsaturation test).

8.2 The Inner Transition Elements (f-Block)

Lanthanoids (Ce to Lu, 4f series)

Configuration: [Xe] 4f¹⁻¹⁴ 5d⁰⁻¹ 6s². Oxidation states: +3 most common (stable); +2 (Eu, Yb: to attain f⁷, f¹⁴) and +4 (Ce, Pr, Tb: to attain f⁰, f⁷). Ce⁴⁺ is a strong oxidiser (becomes Ce³⁺); Eu²⁺ is a strong reducer (becomes Eu³⁺).

Lanthanoid contraction: steady decrease in atomic and ionic radii from La (La³⁺ = 106 pm) to Lu (Lu³⁺ = 85 pm). Cause: 4f electrons shield poorly (diffuse shape), so effective nuclear charge increases with atomic number.

Consequences: (1) 5d elements have radii similar to 4d elements (Zr = Hf), so their chemistry is alike and they are hard to separate. (2) Separation of lanthanoids is difficult (ion exchange used). (3) Basic strength of hydroxides decreases from La(OH)₃ to Lu(OH)₃. (4) Variation in complex formation tendency and hydration enthalpy.

Properties: silvery-white soft metals, tarnish in air, react with dilute acids to give H₂, burn in O₂ to give oxides, react with halogens and N₂. Many ions are coloured (f–f transitions); paramagnetic. Mischmetall (Ce ~40%, La, Nd, Fe ~5%, traces of S, C, Ca, Al) is used in Mg-alloys and lighter flints; lanthanoid oxides in phosphors for television screens, lasers, glass polishing.

Actinoids (Th to Lr, 5f series)

All are radioactive. Configuration: [Rn] 5f¹⁻¹⁴ 6d⁰⁻¹ 7s². Show a wider range of oxidation states (+3 to +7; U +3 to +6, Np +7) because 5f, 6d and 7s energies are close; +3 is common. The actinoid contraction is greater (poorer shielding by 5f). They are more reactive, many are coloured, and they form complexes more readily than lanthanoids. Thorium and uranium are used in nuclear energy; U-235 in reactors.

Questions & Answers (short, long, MCQ)

Q1. Why are Zn, Cd and Hg not regarded as transition elements?

They have a completely filled d¹⁰ configuration in atoms and in common +2 ions, so there is no incomplete d-subshell.

Q2. Why do Cr and Cu have exceptional configurations?

Half-filled (d⁵) and fully-filled (d¹⁰) subshells are extra stable: Cr = 3d⁵4s¹, Cu = 3d¹⁰4s¹.

Q3. Why do transition metals have high melting points?

Both ns and unpaired (n−1)d electrons participate in strong metallic bonding; the more unpaired electrons, the higher the mp (maximum at middle).

Q4. Why does Cu not liberate H₂ from dilute acids?

E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V is positive because its enthalpy of atomisation is high and hydration enthalpy cannot compensate.

Q5. Why do transition elements show variable oxidation states?

(n−1)d and ns electrons have nearly the same energy, so both can take part in bonding, giving states differing by one unit.

Q6. Why does Mn show the highest oxidation state in the 3d series?

It has five unpaired d electrons plus two 4s electrons, so up to +7 in MnO₄⁻ and Mn₂O₇.

Q7. Why is Sc³⁺ colourless but Ti³⁺ purple?

Sc³⁺ is d⁰, no d–d transition. Ti³⁺ is d¹, so it absorbs visible light for d–d transition and shows the complementary colour.

Q8. Calculate spin-only magnetic moment of Fe³⁺ (Z = 26).

Fe³⁺ = 3d⁵, n = 5; μ = √(5×7) = √35 = 5.92 BM.

Q9. Why are transition metals good catalysts?

They have variable oxidation states giving intermediates and a large surface with free valencies for adsorption of reactants.

Q10. What are interstitial compounds? Properties.

Compounds formed when small atoms (H, C, N, B) occupy voids of the metal lattice, e.g. TiC. They are hard, have high mp, are chemically inert and conduct electricity.

Q11. What is lanthanoid contraction? Give causes and consequences.

Steady decrease in size from La³⁺ to Lu³⁺ because poor shielding by 4f electrons. Consequences: Zr–Hf similarity, difficult separation, decreasing basicity of hydroxides.

Q12. Why is Ce⁴⁺ a good oxidising agent although +4 is expected to be stable (f⁰)?

Ce⁴⁺ readily takes an electron to form Ce³⁺, which is the more common and stable state in aqueous solution.

Q13. Why is acidified KMnO₄ not used with HCl?

Cl⁻ is oxidised to Cl₂ by KMnO₄, so extra KMnO₄ is consumed and the result is wrong. H₂SO₄ is used.

Q14. How does yellow chromate become orange dichromate?

On adding acid: 2CrO₄²⁻ + 2H⁺ → Cr₂O₇²⁻ + H₂O; adding alkali reverses the change. No change in oxidation state of Cr (+6).

Q15. MCQ: Which of the following is colourless? (a) Cu²⁺ (b) Zn²⁺ (c) Fe²⁺ (d) Mn²⁺

(b) Zn²⁺ (d¹⁰, no d–d transition).

Numericals – step by step (Easy → Hard)

Easy: Calculate the spin-only magnetic moment of Mn²⁺ (Z = 25).

Step 1: Mn = 3d⁵4s²; Mn²⁺ = 3d⁵. Step 2: unpaired electrons n = 5. Step 3: μ = √[n(n+2)] = √35. Answer: 5.92 BM.

Intermediate: 25 mL of 0.1 M FeSO₄ is titrated with 0.02 M KMnO₄ in acid. Find the volume of KMnO₄.

Reaction: 5Fe²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → 5Fe³⁺ + Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. Step 1: moles Fe²⁺ = 0.1 × 0.025 = 0.0025. Step 2: moles MnO₄⁻ = 0.0025/5 = 0.0005. Step 3: V = 0.0005/0.02 = 0.025 L = 25 mL.

Hard: What mass of K₂Cr₂O₇ (M = 294 g/mol) is needed in acid to completely oxidise 0.1 mol of Fe²⁺?

Reaction: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6Fe²⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 6Fe³⁺ + 7H₂O. Step 1: 6 mol Fe²⁺ need 1 mol Cr₂O₇²⁻. Step 2: moles K₂Cr₂O₇ = 0.1/6 = 0.01667. Step 3: mass = 0.01667 × 294 = 4.9 g.
