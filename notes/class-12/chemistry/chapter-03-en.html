3.3 Variation of Molar Conductivity with Concentration

Conductivity κ decreases on dilution (fewer ions per unit volume). Molar conductivity Λm increases on dilution because volume containing 1 mole grows faster than κ falls.

Strong electrolytes: Λm rises slowly; ion–ion attraction decreases. Debye–Hückel–Onsager:

Λm = Λ°m − A√c
Extrapolating the straight line to c = 0 gives limiting molar conductivity Λ°m (intercept). Weak electrolytes: rise is very steep as degree of dissociation α increases on dilution; Λ°m cannot be found by extrapolation (use Kohlrausch law).

α = Λm/Λ°m ; Ka = cα²/(1−α) = cΛm²/[Λ°m(Λ°m−Λm)]

3.4 Kohlrausch's Law

Law of independent migration of ions: at infinite dilution each ion contributes a definite share to Λ°m, irrespective of the other ion.
Λ°m = ν₊λ°₊ + ν₋λ°₋

Example: Λ°m(Al₂(SO₄)₃) = 2λ°(Al³⁺) + 3λ°(SO₄²⁻). Applications: (i) Λ°m of weak electrolytes: Λ°(CH₃COOH) = Λ°(CH₃COONa) + Λ°(HCl) − Λ°(NaCl). (ii) Degree of dissociation α and Ka. (iii) Solubility of sparingly soluble salts (AgCl, BaSO₄): Λm ≈ Λ°m, so S = κ×1000/Λ°m.

3.5 Electrochemical Cells (Galvanic Cells or Voltaic Cells)

A galvanic cell converts chemical energy → electrical energy using a spontaneous redox reaction. Daniell cell: Zn | ZnSO₄ ‖ CuSO₄ | Cu.

Anode (−): oxidation, Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. Cathode (+): reduction, Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. Overall: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu. Electrons flow anode → cathode in the external wire; current flows opposite.

Salt bridge (U-tube with KCl, KNO₃ or NH₄NO₃ in agar-agar; ions of nearly equal mobility): (i) completes the inner circuit, (ii) keeps both solutions electrically neutral, (iii) removes liquid-junction potential. Cell notation: anode on the left, cathode on the right, ‖ = salt bridge, | = phase boundary.

3.6 Electrode Potential

Electrode potential is the tendency of an electrode to lose or gain electrons. Standard electrode potential E° is measured at 1 M, 1 bar, 298 K. SHE: Pt foil coated with platinum black, H₂ at 1 bar, 1 M H⁺; E° = 0 V (by convention). Reduction potentials are tabulated.

Higher (more +ve) E° = stronger oxidising agent; lower E° = stronger reducing agent. A metal displaces from solution any metal below it in the series.

Nernst: E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q = E° − (0.0591/n) log Q (298 K)

3.7 EMF of a Galvanic Cell (Cell Potential)

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

For Zn–Cu: 0.34 − (−0.76) = 1.10 V. Positive E°cell means a spontaneous reaction. Nernst for Daniell cell: E = 1.10 − (0.0591/2) log([Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺]).

ΔG° = −nFE° ; ΔG° = −2.303RT log K ; log K = nE°/0.0591

At equilibrium E = 0 and the cell is "dead". F = 96500 C mol⁻¹.

3.8 Electrolysis and Criteria for Product Formation

Electrolysis: passing electric current through an electrolyte to bring about chemical change. It converts electrical → chemical energy. Cathode (−): reduction; anode (+): oxidation (opposite polarity to galvanic cell).
  • Molten NaCl: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (cathode); 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (anode).
  • Aqueous NaCl: cathode gives H₂ (water reduces more easily than Na⁺); anode gives Cl₂ (not O₂) due to overpotential of O₂. NaOH remains in solution.
  • Aqueous CuSO₄: with Pt electrodes – Cu at cathode, O₂ at anode; with Cu electrodes – Cu dissolves at anode and deposits at cathode (purification).

Criteria: at cathode the species with higher E° (easier to reduce) is discharged; at anode the species with lower E° (easier to oxidise) is discharged; also consider overpotential and electrode nature (inert or active).

3.9 Quantitative Aspect of Electrolysis

Faraday's 1st law: W ∝ Q, so W = ZIt, where Z = electrochemical equivalent = E/96500. 2nd law: for the same charge, masses deposited ∝ equivalent masses: W₁/W₂ = E₁/E₂.

W = (M/nF) × I × t ; 1 Faraday = 96500 C = charge of 1 mole of electrons

Charge needed: Ag⁺ 1 F per mol, Cu²⁺ 2 F, Al³⁺ 3 F. Q = It.

3.10 Some Commercial Cells (Batteries)

Primary (non-rechargeable): Dry (Leclanché) cell – anode Zn, cathode graphite with MnO₂ + carbon, electrolyte NH₄Cl + ZnCl₂ paste, 1.5 V. Mercury cell – Zn–Hg amalgam anode, HgO + C cathode, KOH + ZnO electrolyte, constant 1.35 V (hearing aids, watches).

Secondary (rechargeable): Lead storage battery – anode Pb, cathode PbO₂ on Pb grid, 38% H₂SO₄, ~12 V (6 cells × 2 V). Discharge: Pb + PbO₂ + 2H₂SO₄ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O. Charging reverses it. Ni–Cd cell – 1.4 V, longer life.

Fuel cell (H₂–O₂): anode: 2H₂ + 4OH⁻ → 4H₂O + 4e⁻; cathode: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻; overall 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Efficiency ~70%, pollution free, used in space (Apollo).

3.11 Corrosion

Corrosion is slow destruction of metal by reaction with environment (rusting of iron). It is an electrochemical process: at the anodic spot Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ (E° = −0.44 V); at the cathodic spot O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O (E° = +1.23 V). Fe²⁺ is further oxidised to Fe³⁺ forming rust, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O. Moisture, O₂, CO₂/acid, impurities and salts speed it.

Prevention: paint/oil/grease coating; galvanisation (Zn coating); electroplating; sacrificial anodes of Mg or Zn; cathodic protection of pipelines; alloying (stainless steel).

3.12 The Hydrogen Economy

Using hydrogen as the main fuel. Advantages: very high energy per gram (≈150 kJ/g), burns to water only (no CO₂), can be made by electrolysis of water using solar energy, and used in fuel cells. Problems: safe storage and transport (high pressure cylinders, metal hydrides) and cost.

Numerical Problems (click to open)

Example 1: A cell with 0.1 M KCl (κ = 1.29 S m⁻¹) has R = 100 Ω. The same cell with 0.02 M KCl gives R = 520 Ω. Find κ and Λm of 0.02 M KCl.

To find: κ, Λm

G* = κR = 1.29×100 = 129 m⁻¹.
κ = G*/R = 129/520 = 0.248 S m⁻¹.
c = 0.02 M = 20 mol m⁻³; Λm = κ/c = 0.248/20 = 0.0124 S m² mol⁻¹ = 124 S cm² mol⁻¹.

Example 2: Λ°m: NaCl 126.5, HCl 426.2, CH₃COONa 91.0 S cm² mol⁻¹. Find Λ°m of CH₃COOH.

To find: Λ°m

Λ°(CH₃COOH) = Λ°(CH₃COONa)+Λ°(HCl)−Λ°(NaCl) = 91.0+426.2−126.5 = 390.7 S cm² mol⁻¹.

Example 3: 0.001 M acetic acid has Λm = 39.07 S cm² mol⁻¹; Λ°m = 390.7. Find α and Ka.

To find: α, Ka

α = 39.07/390.7 = 0.1.
Ka = cα²/(1−α) = 0.001×0.01/0.9 = 1.11×10⁻⁵.

Example 4: Find E of Zn | Zn²⁺(0.1 M) ‖ Cu²⁺(0.01 M) | Cu. E°cell = 1.10 V.

To find: E

n = 2; Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺] = 0.1/0.01 = 10.
E = 1.10 − (0.0591/2) log 10 = 1.10 − 0.02955 = 1.0705 V.

Example 5: For the Daniell cell (E° = 1.10 V) find ΔG° and K.

To find: ΔG°, K

ΔG° = −nFE° = −2×96500×1.10 = −212300 J = −212.3 kJ mol⁻¹.
log K = nE°/0.0591 = 2.20/0.0591 = 37.22; K = antilog = 1.66×10³⁷.

Example 6: Mass of Cu deposited by 2 A passed for 30 min (Cu = 63.5).

To find: W

Q = It = 2×1800 = 3600 C. Equivalent mass = 63.5/2 = 31.75.
W = (31.75×3600)/96500 = 1.18 g.

Example 7: Time for 4 A to deposit 5.4 g Al (Al = 27).

To find: t

E = 27/3 = 9. W = E I t/96500 → t = 5.4×96500/(9×4) = 521100/36 = 14475 s = 4.02 hours.

Example 8: Electrode potential of hydrogen electrode at pH = 4 (H₂ 1 bar).

To find: E

2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂; E = 0 − (0.0591/2) log(1/[H⁺]²) = −0.0591 log(1/[H⁺]) ... = −0.0591×pH = −0.0591×4 = −0.236 V.

Example 9: Prove E = E° − (0.0591/n) log Q at 298 K.

To find: proof

ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q. Substituting ΔG = −nFE and ΔG° = −nFE°: −nFE = −nFE° + RT ln Q.
E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q. At 298 K: R = 8.314, F = 96500, so RT/F = 0.0257 V; ×2.303 = 0.0591.
Hence E = E° − (0.0591/n) log Q.

Short & Long Questions

Q1. Differentiate metallic and electrolytic conductance.

See table 3.1: metallic by electrons, no chemical change, decreases with T; electrolytic by ions, chemical change, increases with T.

Q2. Why is AC used to measure conductance of solutions?

DC would cause electrolysis, changing concentration and polarising the electrodes; AC prevents this.

Q3. Define cell constant.

G* = l/A, the ratio of distance between electrodes to their area; κ = G*/R.

Q4. Why does Λm increase with dilution?

Volume holding 1 mol rises more than κ falls; for weak electrolytes α also increases.

Q5. State Kohlrausch's law and give an application.

Λ°m = ν₊λ°₊+ν₋λ°₋. Used to get Λ°m of weak electrolytes like acetic acid.

Q6. What is the role of the salt bridge?

Completes the circuit, maintains electrical neutrality, and minimises liquid-junction potential.

Q7. Why can't Zn spoon store CuSO₄ solution?

Zn (E° −0.76) is more reactive than Cu (+0.34); it displaces Cu and dissolves.

Q8. What is SHE? Give its use.

Pt electrode in 1 M H⁺ with H₂ at 1 bar; E° = 0. It is the reference for measuring other electrode potentials.

Q9. Why is Cl₂, not O₂, obtained at the anode in aqueous NaCl?

Oxidation of water has high overpotential, so Cl⁻ is oxidised preferentially.

Q10. Write reactions in lead storage battery on discharge.

Anode: Pb + SO₄²⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2e⁻. Cathode: PbO₂ + SO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2H₂O.

Q11. Explain rusting as an electrochemical process.

Anode: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻; cathode: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O; Fe²⁺ oxidised to Fe₂O₃·xH₂O. Water and O₂ are needed.

Q12. What are fuel cells? Advantage?

Cells converting chemical energy of fuel directly into electricity; efficiency about 70%, no pollution.
