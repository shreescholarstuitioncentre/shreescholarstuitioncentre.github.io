13.1 Cyanides and Isocyanides

Nomenclature and Structure

CompoundName
CH₃–C≡NEthanenitrile (methyl cyanide, acetonitrile)
C₆H₅–C≡NBenzonitrile
CH₃–N≡CMethyl isocyanide (isocyanomethane), carbylamine

Cyanide (nitrile): R–C≡N, attached through carbon. Isocyanide (isonitrile): R–N≡C, attached through nitrogen; resonance R–N⁺≡C⁻ ↔ R–N=C:. Both are functional isomers.

Preparation

  • Cyanides: (1) R–X + alc. KCN → R–CN (KCN is ionic, so C attacks). (2) Dehydration of amides: RCONH₂ + P₂O₅ → RCN. (3) Dehydration of aldoximes. (4) Sandmeyer: ArN₂⁺ + CuCN → ArCN.
  • Isocyanides: (1) R–X + AgCN (covalent, N attacks) → R–NC. (2) Carbylamine reaction: R–NH₂ (1° only) + CHCl₃ + 3KOH (alc.) → R–NC + 3KCl + 3H₂O. A foul smell indicates a primary amine (aliphatic or aromatic).

Properties and Reactions

  • Cyanides are polar, with b.p. higher than the corresponding hydrocarbons; lower members are soluble in water. Isocyanides have a very unpleasant smell and are toxic.
  • Hydrolysis of cyanide: RCN + H₂O → RCONH₂ → RCOOH (acid or alkali, heat).
  • Reduction of cyanide: LiAlH₄ or Na/C₂H₅OH (Mendius reduction) → R–CH₂NH₂ (1° amine). Stephen reduction (SnCl₂/HCl) → aldehyde. With Grignard reagent → ketone.
  • Isocyanide hydrolysis: dilute acid → R–NH₂ + HCOOH; it is stable to alkali.
  • Isocyanide reduction (H₂/Ni) → secondary amine R–NH–CH₃. Heating at 523 K isomerises R–NC to R–CN. Isocyanides add O and S.

13.2 Nitro Compounds

Preparation

  1. Nitroalkanes: R–X + AgNO₂ → R–NO₂ (covalent AgNO₂; KNO₂ would give R–ONO nitrite); vapour-phase nitration of alkanes (HNO₃, 673 K).
  2. Nitrobenzene: benzene + conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ at 330 K. The electrophile is the nitronium ion: HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻. Further nitration (fuming HNO₃, 373 K) gives 1,3-dinitrobenzene (meta).

Properties

Nitrobenzene is a pale yellow oily liquid with a smell of bitter almonds (oil of mirbane), insoluble in water, poisonous. Nitroalkanes show tautomerism (nitro ⇌ aci-nitro form) through α-H.

Reactions

  1. Reduction of nitrobenzene depends on the medium:
    • Acidic (Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl, H₂/Pd): aniline.
    • Neutral (Zn/NH₄Cl): N-phenylhydroxylamine.
    • Alkaline (Zn/NaOH): azoxybenzene → azobenzene → hydrazobenzene (successively).
  2. Electrophilic substitution: –NO₂ is deactivating and meta-directing (nitration, halogenation, sulphonation give meta products). Nitrobenzene does not undergo Friedel–Crafts reaction.
  3. Nucleophilic substitution is easier when –NO₂ is at o/p to a halogen (chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene reacts readily with OH⁻).
  4. Victor Meyer test (nitroalkanes): 1° → nitrolic acid (red colour with alkali); 2° → pseudonitrole (blue); 3° → no reaction.

13.3 Amines

Classification and Nomenclature

Replacement of H of NH₃ by one, two, three alkyl/aryl groups gives 1°, 2°, 3° amines. Four groups give a quaternary ammonium salt. Common: alkylamine; IUPAC: alkanamine.

CompoundIUPAC
CH₃CH₂NH₂Ethanamine (1°)
CH₃NHCH₂CH₃N-Methylethanamine (2°)
(CH₃)₃NN,N-Dimethylmethanamine (3°)
C₆H₅NH₂Benzenamine (aniline)
C₆H₅N(CH₃)₂N,N-Dimethylbenzenamine

Structure: N is sp³, the lone pair occupies one orbital, so the shape is pyramidal; C–N–H angle is about 108°.

Preparation

  1. Reduction of nitro compounds: Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl or H₂/Pd (aniline from nitrobenzene; fastest, Fe/HCl gives only a little HCl needed).
  2. Reduction of nitriles and amides: LiAlH₄ → 1° amine (RCN → RCH₂NH₂; RCONH₂ → RCH₂NH₂).
  3. Ammonolysis of alkyl halides: R–X + NH₃ → RNH₂ → R₂NH → R₃N → R₄N⁺X⁻ (mixture; use large excess NH₃ for 1°).
  4. Gabriel phthalimide synthesis: phthalimide + KOH → potassium phthalimide; + R–X → N-alkylphthalimide; hydrolyse with NaOH(aq) → RNH₂ + sodium phthalate. Gives pure 1° aliphatic amines only. Aromatic amines fail because aryl halides do not undergo nucleophilic substitution.
  5. Hofmann bromamide degradation: RCONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → RNH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O. The amine has one carbon less than the amide (benzamide → aniline). Intermediate is an isocyanate (R–N=C=O).

Physical Properties

  • Lower aliphatic amines are gases with a fishy smell; aniline is a colourless liquid that darkens in air.
  • 1° and 2° amines form intermolecular H-bonds, so b.p. order: 1° > 2° > 3° (isomers); amine b.p. is higher than alkanes but lower than alcohols (N is less electronegative than O).
  • Lower amines dissolve in water (H-bonding), solubility decreases as the hydrophobic alkyl portion grows (above about 6 C). Aniline is only slightly soluble.

Basicity

R–NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ R–NH₃⁺ + OH⁻; amines are basic because of the lone pair on N. pKb: NH₃ 4.75; CH₃NH₂ 3.38; (CH₃)₂NH 3.27; (CH₃)₃N 4.22; C₆H₅NH₂ 9.38.

  • Aliphatic amines are stronger bases than NH₃ (+I effect pushes electrons onto N and stabilises the cation).
  • In aqueous solution the order is not simple, because solvation (H-bonding) of R₃NH⁺ is less with more alkyl groups, and steric hindrance: (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N > NH₃ and (C₂H₅)₂NH > (C₂H₅)₃N > C₂H₅NH₂ > NH₃. In the gas phase it is 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃.
  • Aniline is a weaker base than NH₃: the lone pair is delocalised into the ring (resonance), so it is less available. Anilinium ion has fewer resonance structures than aniline, hence is less stable.
  • Electron-releasing groups (–CH₃, –OCH₃) increase aryl amine basicity; electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂, –Cl) decrease it (p-nitroaniline is much weaker).

Chemical Reactions of Amines

  1. Alkylation: with R–X gives higher amines and finally quaternary ammonium salts.
  2. Acylation: 1° and 2° amines + RCOCl or (RCO)₂O (base such as pyridine to remove HCl) → amides (N-substituted). 3° do not react (no N–H). Acetylation protects –NH₂.
  3. Carbylamine reaction (1° only): see 13.1.
  4. With nitrous acid (NaNO₂ + HCl): 1° aliphatic → alcohol + N₂↑ (via unstable diazonium); 1° aromatic → diazonium salt (273–278 K); 2° → N-nitrosoamine (yellow oil; Liebermann’s test); 3° aliphatic → ammonium nitrite salt; 3° aromatic → C-nitroso (p-nitroso) compound.
  5. Hinsberg’s test (C₆H₅SO₂Cl): 1° gives sulphonamide with an acidic N–H, which dissolves in alkali; 2° gives a sulphonamide insoluble in alkali; 3° does not react (remains insoluble but the amine dissolves in acid).
  6. Aniline: electrophilic substitution (–NH₂ strongly activating, o/p-directing):
    • Bromination: Br₂ water → 2,4,6-tribromoaniline (white precipitate, cannot stop at mono). To get mono-bromo, first acetylate to acetanilide (reduces activation), then brominate and hydrolyse.
    • Nitration: direct nitration with HNO₃/H₂SO₄ gives o-, p- and also m-nitroaniline (about 47% m) because in strong acid it forms anilinium ion (–NH₃⁺, meta-directing), plus oxidation. Protect as acetanilide to get mainly p-nitroaniline.
    • Sulphonation: aniline + conc. H₂SO₄, heat at 453–473 K → sulphanilic acid (exists as a dipolar zwitterion, p-aminobenzenesulphonic acid).
    • Friedel–Crafts fails because –NH₂ is a Lewis base and bonds to AlCl₃ forming a salt that strongly deactivates the ring.

13.4 Diazonium Salts

General formula ArN₂⁺X⁻ (X = Cl⁻, HSO₄⁻, BF₄⁻). Aliphatic diazonium salts are unstable; aromatic ones are stabilised by resonance of the ring with the diazo group.

Preparation (diazotisation)

C₆H₅NH₂ + NaNO₂ + 2HCl → C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + NaCl + 2H₂O at 273–278 K. Benzenediazonium chloride is stable only in cold aqueous solution; it is explosive when dry. Benzenediazonium fluoroborate is stable at room temperature.

Reactions

B. Coupling reactions (N₂ retained): ArN₂⁺ + Ar′–OH or Ar′–NH₂ → azo compound Ar–N=N–Ar′. Phenol couples at the para position in slightly alkaline medium (pH 9–10) to give orange p-hydroxyazobenzene; aniline couples in slightly acidic medium (pH 4–5) to give yellow p-aminoazobenzene. These are azo dyes.

Importance: a route to many substituted aromatics not obtainable by direct substitution, e.g. fluoro, iodo, cyano, hydroxy compounds, and azo dyes.

13.5 Some Commercially Important Compounds

  • Nitrobenzene: intermediate for aniline, dyes, explosives; solvent for cheap soaps and shoe polish.
  • Aniline: dyes, drugs (sulpha drugs), rubber chemicals, azo dyes.
  • TNT (2,4,6-trinitrotoluene): explosive.
  • Urea (NH₂CONH₂): first organic compound made in the laboratory (Wöhler, 1828: heating NH₄CNO). Industrial: CO₂ + 2NH₃ → ammonium carbamate → urea + H₂O (about 413–453 K, 140–200 atm). Colourless crystals, m.p. 405 K, soluble in water. Hydrolysis with alkali gives NH₃ + Na₂CO₃; with HNO₂ gives N₂ + CO₂. Heating gives biuret, which with NaOH + dilute CuSO₄ gives a violet colour (biuret test). Uses: nitrogen fertiliser (46% N), urea–formaldehyde resin, barbiturates.

Solved Numericals (step by step)

Easy: Mass of aniline from 12.3 g nitrobenzene (100% yield)

C₆H₅NO₂ (123 g/mol) → C₆H₅NH₂ (93 g/mol).
Step 1: moles = 12.3/123 = 0.1. Step 2: 1:1 ratio so aniline = 0.1 mol. Step 3: mass = 0.1 × 93 = 9.3 g.

Intermediate: Hofmann bromamide — 5.9 g acetamide gives methylamine in 80% yield. Find the mass.

CH₃CONH₂ = 59 g/mol; CH₃NH₂ = 31 g/mol.
Moles = 5.9/59 = 0.1. Theoretical = 0.1 × 31 = 3.1 g. Actual = 3.1 × 80/100 = 2.48 g.

Hard: pKb of aniline = 9.38. Find the pH of a 0.1 M solution.

Kb = 10^(−9.38) = 4.17 × 10⁻¹⁰.
For a weak base [OH⁻] = √(Kb × C) = √(4.17 × 10⁻¹¹) = 6.46 × 10⁻⁶ M.
pOH = −log(6.46 × 10⁻⁶) = 5.19. pH = 14 − 5.19 = 8.81.

Kjeldahl: 0.5 g of an organic compound releases ammonia that neutralises 20 mL of 0.5 N H₂SO₄. Find % nitrogen.

% N = (1.4 × N × V)/W = (1.4 × 0.5 × 20)/0.5 = 28%.
Check: 20 mL × 0.5 N = 10 meq NH₃ = 10 × 14 mg = 140 mg N; 0.14/0.5 × 100 = 28%.

Q & A and MCQs

Q1. Why is aniline a weaker base than methylamine?

In aniline the N lone pair is delocalised into the ring by resonance, so it is less available for protonation; in methylamine +I of CH₃ increases electron density on N.

Q2. Why can’t aromatic primary amines be made by Gabriel phthalimide synthesis?

Aryl halides do not undergo nucleophilic substitution with the phthalimide anion.

Q3. Why is aniline acetylated before nitration?

To avoid oxidation and formation of anilinium ion (m-product); the acetamido group still directs o/p, giving mostly p-nitroaniline.

Q4. Why does aniline not undergo Friedel–Crafts reaction?

It forms a salt with AlCl₃ (Lewis acid), putting a positive charge on N and deactivating the ring.

Q5. Why must diazotisation be done at 273–278 K?

The diazonium salt decomposes above about 283 K to give phenol and nitrogen.

Q6. Why does benzenediazonium chloride dissolve in water while the amine does not?

It is an ionic salt, so it is soluble in water.

Q7. How do you distinguish 1°, 2°, 3° amines?

Hinsberg’s test, or nitrous acid test: 1° gives N₂ gas (aliphatic), 2° gives yellow nitrosoamine oil, 3° gives a salt.

Q8. Why are amines soluble in dilute HCl?

They form water-soluble ammonium chloride salts.

Q9. Why is a mixture formed in ammonolysis of alkyl halides?

The product amine is itself a nucleophile and reacts again with the halide, giving 2°, 3° amines and quaternary salt.

Q10. Write the main product when nitrobenzene is reduced in alkaline medium with Zn.

Azoxybenzene, then azobenzene and hydrazobenzene with continued reduction.

MCQ1. Carbylamine reaction is given by: (a) 1° amine (b) 2° amine (c) 3° amine (d) quaternary salt

Answer: (a).

MCQ2. Hofmann bromamide reaction of benzamide gives: (a) benzylamine (b) aniline (c) benzoic acid (d) benzonitrile

Answer: (b), one carbon less.

MCQ3. Strongest base in aqueous solution: (a) NH₃ (b) CH₃NH₂ (c) (CH₃)₂NH (d) C₆H₅NH₂

Answer: (c).

MCQ4. Benzenediazonium chloride + H₃PO₂ gives: (a) phenol (b) chlorobenzene (c) benzene (d) aniline

Answer: (c).

MCQ5. Iodobenzene is prepared from diazonium salt using: (a) CuI (b) KI (c) HI (d) I₂

Answer: (b), no copper catalyst needed.
