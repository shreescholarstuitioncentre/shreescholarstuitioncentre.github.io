Chapter 16: CHEMISTRY IN EVERYDAY LIFE

16.1 Drugs and Drug-Target Interaction

Drugs are low molecular mass (≈100–500 u) chemicals that interact with macromolecular targets and produce a biological response. When used for treating diseases they are medicines; the use of chemicals for therapeutic effect is chemotherapy.

Drug–target interaction

Targets are biomolecules (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids). Proteins act as enzymes and receptors.

(a) Enzymes as drug targets: The substrate binds to the enzyme's active site through H-bonds, ionic or van der Waals forces and is converted to product. Drugs inhibit enzymes in two ways:

  • Competitive inhibitors: resemble the substrate, compete for the active site and block it.
  • Allosteric inhibitors: bind at a different (allosteric) site, changing the shape of the active site so that the substrate cannot bind.

(b) Receptors as drug targets: Receptors are proteins in cell membranes that receive chemical messengers (e.g. acetylcholine). Agonists mimic the natural messenger and activate the receptor; antagonists bind but block the receptor so no response occurs.

16.2 Therapeutic Action of Different Classes of Drugs

1. Antacids

Excess HCl in stomach causes acidity. Weak bases NaHCO₃, Mg(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃ neutralise it (but excess NaHCO₃ causes ulcers and metal hydroxides are a better choice; Al/Mg hydroxide is insoluble so pH does not become too basic). Cimetidine (Tagamet) and ranitidine (Zantac) prevent interaction of histamine with H₂-receptors of the stomach, reducing acid release.

2. Antihistamines

Histamine causes allergic reactions and nasal congestion. Brompheniramine (Dimetapp), terfenadine (Seldane) block H₁-receptors; they do not affect acid secretion because antiallergic and antacid drugs work on different receptors.

3. Neurologically active drugs

  • Tranquilizers (for stress, anxiety): Equanil, iproniazid and phenelzine (antidepressants), chlordiazepoxide, diazepam, veronal, luminal, seconal (barbiturates, hypnotic sleep-inducing). Act by inhibiting noradrenaline degradation.
  • Analgesics (pain killers): Non-narcotic – aspirin (also antipyretic, anti-blood-clotting, prevents heart attack), paracetamol, ibuprofen. Narcotic – morphine, codeine, heroin; used in post-operative pain, cancer, childbirth; addictive.

4. Antimicrobials

  • Antibiotics: from micro-organisms. Bactericidal (kill bacteria): penicillin, ofloxacin, aminoglycosides. Bacteriostatic (inhibit growth): erythromycin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol. Narrow-spectrum: penicillin G. Broad-spectrum: ampicillin, amoxycillin, chloramphenicol. Chloramphenicol is used for typhoid, pneumonia; ofloxacin for urinary infections.
  • Antiseptics (applied on living tissue): 0.2% phenol solution, dettol (chloroxylenol + terpineol), bithionol (soaps), iodine tincture (2–3% in alcohol–water), boric acid (eye wash), furacin.
  • Disinfectants (non-living objects): 1% phenol, Cl₂ (0.2–0.4 ppm in water), SO₂ in low concentration.
Remember: 0.2% phenol = antiseptic, 1% phenol = disinfectant.

5. Antifertility drugs

Birth control pills contain synthetic progesterone–oestrogen derivatives, e.g. norethindrone and ethynylestradiol (novestrol).

16.3 Chemicals in Food

  • Preservatives: sodium benzoate, salts of sorbic acid and propanoic acid; they prevent microbial spoilage.
  • Artificial sweeteners: Saccharin (≈550 × sweeter than sucrose, excreted unchanged, zero calories), Aspartame (≈100×, used in cold foods only because unstable at cooking temperature), Alitame (≈2000×, control of use difficult), Sucralose (≈600×, trichloro derivative of sucrose, stable at cooking temperature).
  • Antioxidants: BHT (butylated hydroxytoluene), BHA (butylated hydroxyanisole) – added to fats and oils; they are more reactive to oxygen than the food. SO₂ and sulphite also used in wine.
  • Edible colours, flavours, nutritional supplements (vitamins, minerals, amino acids) improve appeal and nutrition.

16.4 Cleansing Agents

Soaps

Sodium or potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids (C₁₂–C₁₈), e.g. sodium stearate C₁₇H₃₅COONa, sodium palmitate, sodium oleate. Prepared by saponification: oil/fat + NaOH → soap + glycerol; soap is precipitated by adding NaCl (salting out).

(C₁₇H₃₅COO)₃C₃H₅ + 3NaOH → 3C₁₇H₃₅COONa + C₃H₅(OH)₃

Types: toilet soaps (excess fat, glycerol, colour), laundry soaps (fillers: sodium rosinate, borax, trisodium phosphate), transparent soaps (glycerol/alcohol), medicated soaps (antiseptic), shaving soaps (potassium soap, glycerol).

Cleansing action (micelle formation)

A soap molecule has a hydrophilic polar head (–COO⁻Na⁺) and a hydrophobic nonpolar hydrocarbon tail. In water the tails point into the grease/oil droplet and the heads face water, forming a micelle (colloidal, around the oil), which is washed away.

Hard water problem

Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ in hard water form insoluble scum: 2C₁₇H₃₅COONa + CaCl₂ → (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₂Ca↓ + 2NaCl. So soaps are useless in hard water; also sea water.

Solved Numericals

Easy: Find molar mass of sodium stearate C₁₇H₃₅COONa.
C = 18×12 = 216; H = 35×1 = 35; O₂ = 32; Na = 23.
Total = 306 g/mol.
Intermediate: How many g of NaOH are needed to saponify 89 g tristearin (M = 890)?
Step 1: moles = 89/890 = 0.1 mol.
Step 2: 1 mol fat needs 3 mol NaOH → 0.3 mol.
Step 3: mass = 0.3×40 = 12 g.
Hard: Soap mass obtained from 89 g tristearin if yield 80%?
Step 1: 0.1 mol fat → 0.3 mol sodium stearate (theoretical).
Step 2: theoretical mass = 0.3×306 = 91.8 g.
Step 3: actual = 91.8×0.80 = 73.44 g.

Q & A

Q1. What are drugs and medicines?
Drugs are chemicals of low molecular mass that interact with biomolecules to give a biological response; those useful in curing disease are medicines.
Q2. What is the difference between competitive and allosteric inhibitors?
Competitive inhibitors compete with the substrate for the active site; allosteric inhibitors bind elsewhere and change active-site shape.
Q3. Why are cimetidine and ranitidine better than NaHCO₃?
They prevent histamine from stimulating acid release instead of only neutralising acid; NaHCO₃ can cause over-alkalinity and ulcer.
Q4. Differentiate antiseptics and disinfectants.
Antiseptics are applied on living tissues (dettol); disinfectants on non-living objects (1% phenol).
Q5. Why is aspartame used only in cold foods?
It decomposes at cooking temperature.
Q6. What are narcotic analgesics? Give two examples.
Pain killers that cause sleep and addiction in excess; morphine, codeine.
Q7. Why do soaps not work in hard water?
Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ form insoluble calcium/magnesium soaps (scum) with soap anions.
Q8. Why are branched-chain detergents harmful?
Microbes cannot degrade them; they persist in water causing foam and pollution.
