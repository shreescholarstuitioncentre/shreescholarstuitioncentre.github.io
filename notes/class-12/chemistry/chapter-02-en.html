2.2 Intensive, Extensive and Colligative Properties

Extensive: depend on amount (mass, volume, energy). Intensive: independent of amount (density, temperature, refractive index). Colligative: depend only on the number of solute particles, not on their nature: (i) relative lowering of vapour pressure, (ii) elevation of boiling point, (iii) depression of freezing point, (iv) osmotic pressure. Valid for dilute solutions.

2.3 Solutes: Non-electrolyte, Electrolyte, Dissociation and Association

Non-volatile non-electrolyte (glucose, urea): does not ionise; number of particles = number of molecules. Electrolyte (NaCl, BaCl₂): dissociates into ions, so particles increase and colligative effect is larger. Some solutes associate (acetic acid or benzoic acid dimerise in benzene by H-bonding), so particles decrease and the effect is smaller. This is corrected using van't Hoff factor i (see 2.10).

2.4 Solutions of Gases in Liquids

Henry's law: p = KH · x

The partial pressure of a gas over a solution is proportional to its mole fraction in the solution. A higher KH means lower solubility. KH rises with temperature, so gas solubility decreases on heating.

Applications: soda bottles are sealed under high CO₂ pressure; bends in scuba divers (N₂ dissolves at depth and forms bubbles on quick ascent; divers use He–O₂ mixtures); anoxia at high altitude (low partial pressure of O₂ in blood).

Factors: nature of gas and solvent, pressure (↑ solubility), temperature (↓ solubility).

2.5 Solid Solutions

Solute and solvent both solid. Substitutional: solute atoms replace solvent atoms (similar size; brass Cu–Zn, bronze Cu–Sn). Interstitial: small solute atoms fit in voids (steel: C in Fe). Alloys are harder and more corrosion resistant than pure metals.

2.6 Solutions of Solids in Liquids

"Like dissolves like": polar solutes in polar solvents (NaCl in water), non-polar in non-polar (naphthalene in benzene). Saturated solution: dissolved and undissolved solute in dynamic equilibrium. Solubility = amount per 100 g solvent in saturated solution. By Le Chatelier's principle, if dissolution is endothermic, solubility rises with temperature (KNO₃); if exothermic, it falls (Ce₂(SO₄)₃). Pressure has negligible effect.

2.7 Vapour Pressure of Solutions and Raoult's Law

Vapour pressure: pressure of vapour in equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature; it rises with T and falls with stronger intermolecular forces.
Raoult's law (volatile liquids): pA = xAp°A; ptotal = pA + pB

Vapour phase composition: yA = pA/ptotal (Dalton). For a non-volatile solute: p = x1p°, hence

(p° − p)/p° = x2 = n2/(n1+n2)
called relative lowering of vapour pressure. For dilute solutions: (p°−p)/p° ≈ w₂M₁/(M₂w₁).

2.9 Colligative Properties

(i) Relative lowering of vapour pressure

(p°−p)/p° = x₂ ; M₂ = w₂M₁p°/(w₁(p°−p)).

(ii) Elevation of boiling point

ΔTb = Kb·m ; M₂ = 1000 Kb w₂ /(ΔTb w₁)

Boiling occurs when vapour pressure = atmospheric pressure; the solute lowers vapour pressure so more heat is needed. Kb (K kg/mol): water 0.52, benzene 2.53.

(iii) Depression of freezing point

ΔTf = Kf·m ; M₂ = 1000 Kf w₂ /(ΔTf w₁)

Kf: water 1.86, benzene 5.12. Used in antifreeze (ethylene glycol) and salting icy roads.

(iv) Osmotic pressure

π = CRT = (n/V)RT ; M₂ = w₂RT/(πV)

Osmosis: flow of solvent through a semipermeable membrane. π: extra pressure needed to stop it. Isotonic solutions have equal π (0.9% NaCl with blood); hypertonic cells shrink (plasmolysis); hypotonic cells swell. Reverse osmosis: pressure > π forces solvent out of solution – desalination of sea water. Osmotic pressure suits macromolecules (proteins, polymers) as measured at room temperature and gives a large measurable value.

2.10 Abnormal Molecular Masses (van't Hoff Factor)

i = normal molar mass / observed molar mass = observed colligative property / calculated colligative property = total moles after / before

Modified: ΔTb = i Kb m; ΔTf = i Kf m; π = i CRT; (p°−p)/p° = i x₂.

Dissociation (A → n ions): i = 1+(n−1)α, so i > 1 (KCl ≈ 2, BaCl₂ ≈ 3). Association (n molecules → 1): i = 1 − α(1 − 1/n), so i < 1 (acetic acid in benzene ≈ 0.5).

Example 1: 18 g glucose (M = 180) in 100 g water. Find molality and mole fraction of glucose.

To find: m, x

n(glucose) = 18/180 = 0.1 mol. Molality = 0.1/0.1 kg = 1 m.
n(water) = 100/18 = 5.556 mol. x = 0.1/(0.1+5.556) = 0.0177.

Example 2: 4 g NaOH in 250 mL solution. Find molarity.

To find: M

n = 4/40 = 0.1 mol; V = 0.25 L; M = 0.1/0.25 = 0.4 M.

Example 3: 36 g glucose in 90 g water at 25°C; p° of water = 23.8 mmHg. Find vapour pressure and relative lowering.

To find: p

n₂ = 36/180 = 0.2; n₁ = 90/18 = 5. x₁ = 5/5.2 = 0.9615.
p = x₁p° = 0.9615×23.8 = 22.88 mmHg.
RLVP = x₂ = 0.2/5.2 = 0.0385.

Example 4: 5 g urea (M = 60) in 100 g water. Kb = 0.52. Find boiling point.

To find: Tb

m = (5/60)/0.1 = 0.833. ΔTb = 0.52×0.833 = 0.433 K. Tb = 100 + 0.43 = 100.43°C.

Example 5: 1.0 g of solute in 50 g benzene lowers freezing point by 0.40 K (Kf = 5.12). Find molar mass.

To find: M₂

M₂ = 1000 Kf w₂/(ΔTf w₁) = 1000×5.12×1/(0.40×50) = 5120/20 = 256 g/mol.

Example 6: Find osmotic pressure of 0.1 M glucose at 27°C (R = 0.0821 L atm/mol K).

To find: π

T = 300 K. π = CRT = 0.1×0.0821×300 = 2.463 atm.

Example 7: Calculate ΔTf of 0.1 m NaCl (Kf = 1.86), assuming complete dissociation.

To find: ΔTf

NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻, so i = 2. ΔTf = i Kf m = 2×1.86×0.1 = 0.372 K.

Example 8: Prove i = 1 + (n−1)α for dissociation.

To find: proof

Take 1 mol solute: A → nB. Dissociated = α, left = 1−α, ions formed = nα.
Total after = 1−α+nα = 1+(n−1)α. Before = 1.
i = total after/before = 1+(n−1)α. Hence α = (i−1)/(n−1).

Example 9: N₂ at 0.8 bar over water; KH = 76480 bar. Find mole fraction of N₂.

To find: x

x = p/KH = 0.8/76480 = 1.05×10⁻⁵.

Short & Long Questions

Q1. State Raoult's law.

For a solution of volatile liquids the partial vapour pressure of each component equals its mole fraction times its vapour pressure when pure. For non-volatile solute, relative lowering = mole fraction of solute.

Q2. State Henry's law and give two applications.

p = K_H·x. Used in sealing soda bottles under pressure and explaining bends in divers.

Q3. Why do soft drinks fizz on opening?

Pressure drops to atmospheric, CO₂ solubility falls, gas escapes as bubbles.

Q4. What are colligative properties?

Properties of dilute solutions depending only on the number of solute particles: RLVP, ΔTb, ΔTf, π.

Q5. Why is molality preferred over molarity?

Molality uses masses, so it is independent of temperature; molarity changes with volume.

Q6. What is an azeotrope? Types?

Constant-boiling liquid mixture of fixed composition. Minimum boiling (+ve deviation) and maximum boiling (−ve deviation).

Q7. Why does ethanol–acetone show +ve deviation?

Acetone–ethanol interactions are weaker than ethanol–ethanol H-bonds (acetone breaks them), so escaping tendency rises and ΔH, ΔV are positive.

Q8. Why is ΔTf of 0.1 m NaCl nearly double that of 0.1 m glucose?

NaCl gives two ions per formula unit (i≈2) while glucose does not ionise.

Q9. Define osmosis and reverse osmosis.

Osmosis: solvent flow via SPM to the more concentrated side. Reverse osmosis: pressure greater than π drives solvent out of solution, used for desalination.

Q10. Why is osmotic pressure best for molar mass of polymers?

It is measured at room temperature (no decomposition), and gives measurable values even at very low concentration.

Q11. Why does salt melt ice on roads?

Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so ice melts at lower temperatures.

Q12. What is van't Hoff factor? Give values.

i = observed/calculated colligative property. NaCl 2, K₂SO₄ 3, glucose 1, acetic acid in benzene ≈ 0.5.
