p-Block Elements – Class 12 Chemistry, Ch. 7

7.1 Group 16 Elements: Oxygen Family (Chalcogens)

Members: Oxygen (O), Sulphur (S), Selenium (Se), Tellurium (Te), Polonium (Po, radioactive). Chalcogens = ore-formers (most metal ores are oxides/sulphides). Valence shell configuration: ns²np⁴. Oxygen is the most abundant element in the earth's crust (~46.6% by mass); air has ~21% O₂ by volume.

Periodic trends

Atomic and ionic radii increase down the group. Ionisation enthalpy decreases down the group (O 1314 > S 1000 > Se 941 > Te 869 kJ/mol); it is lower than group 15 because of the half-filled stable p³ configuration of group 15. Electron gain enthalpy: sulphur is the most negative (O is less negative due to small size and interelectronic repulsion). Electronegativity decreases from O (3.5) downwards. Oxidation states: −2 mainly; +2, +4, +6 for S, Se, Te; oxygen shows −2 (−1 in peroxides, +2 in OF₂). Anomalous behaviour of oxygen: small size, high electronegativity, absence of d-orbitals, so its covalency is limited to 2 (4 in some) and it forms hydrogen bonds (H₂O is a liquid; H₂S is a gas).

Physical: melting and boiling points increase down the group; O₂ is a gas (diatomic), S, Se, Te are solids; S forms S₈ rings, showing catenation.

Chemical properties

Hydrides (H₂E): bond angle H₂O 104.5° > H₂S 92° > H₂Se 91° > H₂Te 90°. Acidic character: H₂O < H₂S < H₂Se < H₂Te. Thermal stability: H₂O > H₂S > H₂Se > H₂Te. Reducing power increases down. H₂O has the highest boiling point due to hydrogen bonding.
Oxides: SO₂, SO₃, SeO₂ acidic; TeO₂ amphoteric.
Halides: SF₆ (sp³d², octahedral, very inert), SF₄ (see-saw), SCl₂, S₂Cl₂, OF₂, O₂F₂.

Dioxygen

Preparation: 2KClO₃ →(MnO₂, heat) 2KCl + 3O₂; 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂; 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂; electrolysis of water.
Properties: colourless, odourless, paramagnetic gas; supports combustion; 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO; 4NH₃ + 5O₂ →(Pt) 4NO + 6H₂O; 2SO₂ + O₂ →(V₂O₅) 2SO₃. Uses: respiration, oxy-acetylene welding, steel making, rocket fuel, hospitals.

Ozone (O₃)

3O₂ ⇌ 2O₃, ΔH = +142 kJ/mol, so it is endothermic and unstable. Preparation: passing silent electric discharge through pure dry oxygen (~10% ozonised oxygen). Structure: angular (bent), O–O bond length 128 pm, angle ~117°, a resonance hybrid of two forms. Properties: pale blue gas, strong oxidising agent as it gives nascent oxygen: O₃ → O₂ + [O]. PbS + 4O₃ → PbSO₄ + 4O₂; 2KI + H₂O + O₃ → 2KOH + I₂ + O₂ (used to estimate O₃; moist starch-iodide paper turns blue); mercury loses meniscus (tailing). Uses: germicide, disinfectant, bleaching, ozonolysis. Ozone layer depletion: CFCs (freons) under UV give Cl• radicals: Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂; ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂; one Cl• destroys many O₃ molecules (chain reaction). NO from aircraft also depletes O₃.

Sulphur allotropes

Rhombic (α) sulphur is stable below 369 K; monoclinic (β) is stable above 369 K (transition temperature). Both contain puckered crown-shaped S₈ rings, differing in arrangement. At 1000 K, S₂ forms, paramagnetic like O₂.

Sulphur dioxide (SO₂)

Preparation: S + O₂ → SO₂; roasting sulphides: 4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂; lab: SO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → SO₂ + H₂O (sulphite + dilute acid). Properties: colourless, pungent, easily liquefied, very soluble in water (H₂SO₃). Reducing and temporary bleaching (moist SO₂ + water gives nascent H; colour returns on standing in air). 2Fe³⁺ + SO₂ + 2H₂O → 2Fe²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺; 5SO₂ + 2MnO₄⁻ + 2H₂O → 5SO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ + 2Mn²⁺ (purple decolourised); SO₂ + Cl₂ → SO₂Cl₂. Structure: V-shaped, S sp² hybridised, resonance, S–O 143 pm, angle ~119.5°. Uses: bleaching wool, silk, straw; preservative; refining petroleum and sugar; making H₂SO₄.

Oxoacids of sulphur

Sulphurous H₂SO₃, sulphuric H₂SO₄, thiosulphuric H₂S₂O₃, pyrosulphuric (oleum) H₂S₂O₇, peroxomonosulphuric (Caro's) H₂SO₅, peroxodisulphuric (Marshall's) H₂S₂O₈.

Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) – Contact process

Steps: (i) burn S or roast pyrites to SO₂; (ii) purify gas; (iii) oxidise: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (exothermic, volume decreases), so high pressure and low temperature favour it, but for good rate use V₂O₅ catalyst, 720 K, 2 bar (~96–98% conversion); (iv) absorb SO₃ in 98% H₂SO₄ to give oleum (H₂S₂O₇), because SO₃ in water forms a mist; (v) dilute oleum to get 98% acid.

Properties: colourless, dense, oily liquid (98%, density 1.84 g/mL); very high affinity for water (strongly exothermic; always add acid to water). Dehydrating agent: C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ → 12C + 11H₂O (sugar chars). Oxidising agent: Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O; C + 2H₂SO₄ → CO₂ + 2SO₂ + 2H₂O; S + 2H₂SO₄ → 3SO₂ + 2H₂O. Strong dibasic acid: forms two series of salts (bisulphates and sulphates); NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl. Uses: fertilisers (superphosphate, (NH₄)₂SO₄), petroleum refining, detergents, lead storage batteries, explosives, paints and pigments, metallurgy.

7.2 Group 17 Elements: Halogens

Members: F, Cl, Br, I, At (radioactive). Halogens = salt-formers. Configuration ns²np⁵. F occurs as fluorspar CaF₂, cryolite Na₃AlF₆; Cl as NaCl (sea water, rock salt); Br in sea water as bromides; I in seaweeds and Chile saltpetre.

Trends

Atomic radii increase; ionisation enthalpy decreases (very high, halogens do not lose electrons easily). Electron gain enthalpy: very negative; Cl > F > Br > I (F smaller due to compact 2p, interelectronic repulsion). Electronegativity: F (4.0) highest. Oxidation states: −1 for all; F only −1; Cl, Br, I show +1, +3, +5, +7 (vacant d-orbitals) in oxoacids and interhalogens. Physical: F₂ pale yellow gas, Cl₂ greenish-yellow gas, Br₂ red-brown liquid, I₂ violet solid; colour arises from absorption of visible light and excitation of outer electrons. Bond dissociation enthalpy: Cl₂ (242.6) > Br₂ (192.8) > F₂ (158.8) > I₂ (151.1 kJ/mol); F₂ is low due to repulsion of lone pairs on small F atoms.

Chemical properties

Oxidising power: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂; a higher halogen displaces a lower halide from its salt solution (Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂). F₂ is the strongest oxidising agent and oxidises water: 2F₂ + 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4F⁻ + O₂. Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HOCl.

Hydrogen halides: HF is a liquid due to hydrogen bonding (bp 293 K). Acid strength: HF < HCl < HBr < HI. Thermal stability: HF > HCl > HBr > HI. Reducing power: HI > HBr > HCl > HF.

Oxides: OF₂, O₂F₂ (fluorides of oxygen); Cl₂O, ClO₂ (explosive), Cl₂O₆, Cl₂O₇; I₂O₅ (oxidises CO to CO₂: used for CO estimation). Oxoacids: HOX (hypohalous), HOXO (halous), HOXO₂ (halic), HOXO₃ (perhalic). Acid strength: HClO < HClO₂ < HClO₃ < HClO₄ (more oxygen stabilises the anion).

Interhalogen compounds

Types XX′ (ClF, BrF, ICl), XX′₃ (ClF₃, BrF₃), XX′₅ (BrF₅, IF₅), XX′₇ (IF₇). The larger halogen is the central atom. Structures: ClF₃ bent T-shaped, IF₅ square pyramidal, IF₇ pentagonal bipyramidal. They are more reactive than halogens (except F₂) as X–X′ bonds are weaker. Hydrolysis: XX′ + H₂O → HX′ + HOX. Used as fluorinating agents; ClF₃, BrF₃ in nuclear fuel (UF₆) production.

Chlorine (Cl₂)

Preparation: MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + Cl₂ + 2H₂O; 2KMnO₄ + 16HCl → 2KCl + 2MnCl₂ + 8H₂O + 5Cl₂; 4NaCl + MnO₂ + 4H₂SO₄ → MnCl₂ + 4NaHSO₄ + 2H₂O + Cl₂. Deacon's process: 4HCl + O₂ →(CuCl₂, 723 K) 2Cl₂ + 2H₂O. Commercial: electrolysis of brine.

Properties: greenish-yellow, pungent, 2.5 times heavier than air, soluble in water. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl; 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃; H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl; 8NH₃ + 3Cl₂ → 6NH₄Cl + N₂ (excess NH₃); NH₃ + 3Cl₂ → NCl₃ + 3HCl (excess Cl₂). With cold dilute NaOH: 2NaOH + Cl₂ → NaCl + NaOCl + H₂O; with hot conc. NaOH: 6NaOH + 3Cl₂ → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O; with slaked lime: 2Ca(OH)₂ + 2Cl₂ → CaCl₂ + Ca(OCl)₂ + 2H₂O (bleaching powder). Substitution: CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl; addition: C₂H₄ + Cl₂ → C₂H₄Cl₂. Bleaching: Cl₂ + H₂O → 2HCl + [O]; permanent, oxidising bleach for vegetable matter. Uses: bleaching paper and cotton, water purification, PVC, pesticides, poison gases.

Hydrogen chloride (HCl)

NaCl + H₂SO₄ →(420 K) NaHSO₄ + HCl; NaHSO₄ + NaCl →(823 K) Na₂SO₄ + HCl. Colourless pungent gas, very soluble. Moist HCl + NH₃ gives white fumes of NH₄Cl. Aqua regia (3 parts conc. HCl : 1 part conc. HNO₃) dissolves Au and Pt: Au + 4H⁺ + NO₃⁻ + 4Cl⁻ → AuCl₄⁻ + NO + 2H₂O.

7.3 Group 18 Elements: Noble Gases

Members: He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn (radioactive). Configuration ns²np⁶ (He 1s²) — complete octet. Argon is ~0.93% of dry air; He from natural gas and minerals (monazite); Rn from radioactive decay of radium.

Properties: monoatomic, colourless, odourless. Atomic (van der Waals) radii are large. Very high ionisation enthalpy, positive electron gain enthalpy, so they are chemically inert. Weak dispersion forces give very low melting/boiling points; He has the lowest bp (4.2 K) and shows superfluidity (does not solidify at 1 atm).

Compounds of xenon

Bartlett (1962): O₂ + PtF₆ → O₂⁺[PtF₆]⁻; since the IE of Xe (1170 kJ/mol) is close to that of O₂ (1175), he obtained Xe⁺[PtF₆]⁻. Fluorides: Xe + F₂ →(673 K, 1 bar, excess Xe) XeF₂; Xe + 2F₂ →(873 K, 7 bar) XeF₄; Xe + 3F₂ →(573 K, 60–70 bar) XeF₆. Hydrolysis: XeF₆ + 3H₂O → XeO₃ + 6HF; XeF₆ + H₂O → XeOF₄ + 2HF; 6XeF₄ + 12H₂O → 4Xe + 2XeO₃ + 24HF + 3O₂.

Uses

He: balloons (non-inflammable), deep-sea diving mixtures (He–O₂ reduces nitrogen solubility), cryogenics, MRI. Ne: discharge tubes, advertising lights (red-orange). Ar: inert atmosphere in welding, bulbs. Kr, Xe: lamps, photographic flash. Rn: radiotherapy.

Questions & Answers (short, long, MCQ)

Q1. Why is oxygen a gas but sulphur a solid?

O forms stable O=O (pπ–pπ) diatomic molecules with weak intermolecular forces; S forms large S₈ rings with stronger van der Waals forces.

Q2. Why is the first ionisation enthalpy of oxygen less than nitrogen?

N has a stable half-filled p³ configuration; O (p⁴) loses an electron to attain p³.

Q3. Why is H₂O a liquid but H₂S a gas?

H₂O forms strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds because O is highly electronegative and small; H₂S cannot.

Q4. Arrange hydrides of group 16 in order of acidic strength and explain.

H₂O < H₂S < H₂Se < H₂Te. Bond length rises and H–E bond enthalpy falls down the group, so H⁺ is released more easily.

Q5. Why does ozone act as a powerful oxidising agent?

It is endothermic and unstable, readily giving nascent oxygen: O₃ → O₂ + [O].

Q6. How is ozone layer depleted by freons?

UV breaks CFCs to give Cl•; Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂; ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂. The chain regenerates Cl•, so one radical destroys many O₃ molecules.

Q7. Why does SO₂ act as a bleaching agent? Is it permanent?

Moist SO₂ reduces coloured matter (SO₂ + 2H₂O → H₂SO₄ + 2[H]). It is temporary; air re-oxidises and colour returns.

Q8. Explain contact process with conditions.

S/pyrites → SO₂; purify; 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (V₂O₅, 720 K, 2 bar); SO₃ absorbed in 98% H₂SO₄ to form oleum; oleum diluted to give H₂SO₄. SO₃ is not absorbed in water directly because it forms an acid mist.

Q9. Why is concentrated H₂SO₄ a dehydrating agent? Give example.

It has great affinity for water. Sugar chars: C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ → 12C + 11H₂O.

Q10. Why is fluorine the strongest oxidising agent?

Low F–F bond enthalpy, high hydration enthalpy of F⁻, and high electronegativity.

Q11. Why is electron gain enthalpy of F less negative than Cl?

F is very small, so added electron faces strong repulsion from the compact 2p electrons.

Q12. Why HF is a weaker acid than HCl in water?

H–F bond is very strong and F⁻ is hydrogen-bonded, so HF ionises less (H–F bond enthalpy 574 kJ/mol).

Q13. What is aqua regia? Write its use.

Mixture of conc. HCl and conc. HNO₃ in 3 : 1 ratio; dissolves noble metals Au and Pt by forming chloro-complexes.

Q14. Why are noble gases inert? Why did Bartlett prepare XePtF₆?

Complete octet, high IE and positive electron gain enthalpy. Xe has IE close to O₂, so Xe could be oxidised by PtF₆ just as O₂ is.

Q15. MCQ: The shape of XeF₄ is (a) tetrahedral (b) square planar (c) see-saw (d) octahedral

(b) square planar (sp³d², 4 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs).

Numericals – step by step (Easy → Hard)

Easy: Find the oxidation state of Cl in HClO₄.

Step 1: Let Cl = x. H = +1, O = −2. Step 2: +1 + x + 4(−2) = 0. Step 3: x − 7 = 0, so x = +7.

Intermediate: Volume of Cl₂ at STP obtained from 8.7 g MnO₂ with excess HCl (Mn = 55, O = 16).

Reaction: MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + Cl₂ + 2H₂O. Step 1: Molar mass MnO₂ = 87 g/mol. Step 2: moles = 8.7/87 = 0.1. Step 3: 1 mol MnO₂ gives 1 mol Cl₂, so 0.1 mol Cl₂. Step 4: V = 0.1 × 22.4 = 2.24 L.

Hard: Mass of H₂SO₄ made from 32 g sulphur by contact process (S = 32, H₂SO₄ = 98), 100% yield.

Step 1: S + O₂ → SO₂. Step 2: 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃. Step 3: SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇; H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄. Step 4: 1 mol S (32 g) gives 1 mol SO₂ → 1 mol SO₃ → 1 mol H₂SO₄. Answer: 98 g.
