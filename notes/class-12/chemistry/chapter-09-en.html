Coordination Compounds – Class 12 Chemistry, Ch. 9

9.1 Coordination Compounds and Important Terms

Coordination compound: a compound in which a central metal atom/ion is bonded to a fixed number of ions or molecules (ligands) by coordinate (dative) bonds. A double salt (e.g. Mohr's salt FeSO₄·(NH₄)₂SO₄·6H₂O, potash alum) loses its identity in water and gives all its simple ions, whereas a complex (e.g. K₄[Fe(CN)₆]) keeps the complex ion intact.

Werner's theory (1898)

(1) A metal shows two types of valence: primary (ionisable, non-directional, equals oxidation state, satisfied by negative ions) and secondary (non-ionisable, directional, equals coordination number, satisfied by ligands or negative ions). (2) Every metal tends to satisfy both. (3) Secondary valences have fixed spatial arrangement: octahedral, tetrahedral, square planar.
Evidence (CoCl₃ + NH₃): CoCl₃·6NH₃ gives 3 AgCl with AgNO₃ ([Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃, yellow); CoCl₃·5NH₃ gives 2 AgCl ([Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂, purple); CoCl₃·4NH₃ gives 1 AgCl ([Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]Cl, green); CoCl₃·3NH₃ gives none ([Co(NH₃)₃Cl₃]). Co has coordination number 6 in all.

Important terms

Central atom/ion: the metal to which ligands bind. Ligand: ion/molecule donating a lone pair. Types: unidentate (H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻), didentate (ethane-1,2-diamine "en", oxalate), polydentate (EDTA⁴⁻ is hexadentate), ambidentate (two donor atoms: NO₂⁻ as N or O; SCN⁻ as S or N). Chelate: ring formed by didentate/polydentate ligand (more stable). Coordination number: number of ligand donor atoms directly bonded to metal (in [Cu(en)₂]²⁺ it is 4, not 2). Coordination sphere: central atom + ligands inside [ ]; counter ions are outside. Coordination polyhedron: spatial arrangement. Oxidation number: charge of the central atom if all ligands were removed with their pairs. Homoleptic: only one kind of ligand ([Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺); heteroleptic: different ligands ([Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺).

9.2 Nomenclature of Coordination Compounds

IUPAC rules: (1) Cation is named first, then anion. (2) Inside the coordination sphere, ligands are named first in alphabetical order (ignoring prefixes di-, tri-), then the metal. (3) Prefixes di, tri, tetra for simple ligands; bis, tris, tetrakis for complex ligands (like en). (4) Oxidation state of metal in Roman numerals in brackets. (5) If the complex is an anion, metal name ends in -ate (ferrate, cuprate, argentate, aurate, stannate, plumbate use Latin names). (6) Anionic ligands end in -o (chlorido, cyanido, hydroxido, oxalato, sulphato, nitrito-N / nitrito-O, thiocyanato-S); neutral ligands keep names, except aqua (H₂O), ammine (NH₃), carbonyl (CO), nitrosyl (NO).

9.3 Isomerism in Coordination Compounds

A. Structural isomerism

Linkage: ambidentate ligand binds through different atoms: [Co(NH₃)₅(NO₂)]Cl₂ (nitrito-N, yellow) and [Co(NH₃)₅(ONO)]Cl₂ (nitrito-O, red). Coordination: interchange of ligands between cation and anion: [Co(NH₃)₆][Cr(CN)₆] and [Cr(NH₃)₆][Co(CN)₆]. Ionisation: counter ion and ligand exchange: [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br (gives Br⁻, AgBr ppt) and [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ (gives SO₄²⁻, BaSO₄ ppt). Solvate (hydrate): water inside vs outside the sphere: [Cr(H₂O)₆]Cl₃ (violet), [Cr(H₂O)₅Cl]Cl₂·H₂O (grey-green), [Cr(H₂O)₄Cl₂]Cl·2H₂O (dark green).

B. Stereoisomerism

Geometrical (cis–trans): in square planar [MA₂B₂] and octahedral [MA₄B₂]; [MA₃B₃] has fac and mer forms. Tetrahedral complexes cannot show geometrical isomerism. [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]: cis is polar and the anticancer drug cisplatin; trans is non-polar.

Optical isomerism: complexes with no plane or centre of symmetry give non-superimposable mirror images (enantiomers, dextro and laevo rotating plane-polarised light): [Co(en)₃]³⁺ and cis-[CoCl₂(en)₂]⁺ (trans form is optically inactive). Square planar complexes are optically inactive.

9.4 Bonding in Coordination Compounds

Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

Ligand lone pairs are donated into vacant hybrid orbitals of the metal. CN 4: sp³ tetrahedral ([NiCl₄]²⁻, Ni²⁺ d⁸ with 2 unpaired e⁻, paramagnetic); dsp² square planar ([Ni(CN)₄]²⁻, strong CN⁻ pairs the d⁸ electrons, diamagnetic). CN 6: inner orbital d²sp³ using (n−1)d, low spin ([Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ diamagnetic, [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ with 1 unpaired e⁻); outer orbital sp³d² using nd, high spin ([CoF₆]³⁻ paramagnetic with 4 unpaired e⁻). Limitations: cannot explain colour, quantitative magnetic data, or the relative energies of orbitals.

Crystal Field Theory (CFT)

Ligands are point negative charges; metal–ligand interaction is purely electrostatic. In a free ion the five d-orbitals are degenerate; in an octahedral field they split into t₂g (dxy, dyz, dzx; lower, −0.4Δo each) and e_g (dx²−y², dz²; higher, +0.6Δo each). In tetrahedral field the order reverses (e below t₂) and Δt = (4/9)Δo (smaller, so mostly high spin).

Spectrochemical series (weak → strong field): I⁻ < Br⁻ < SCN⁻ < Cl⁻ < S²⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ < EDTA⁴⁻ < NH₃ < en < bipy < phen < NO₂⁻ < CN⁻ < CO. d⁴–d⁷: if Δo < P (pairing energy): weak field, electrons enter e_g, high spin; if Δo > P: strong field, low spin. CFSE (octahedral) = (−0.4 n(t₂g) + 0.6 n(e_g)) Δo + pairing energy. Example: low spin d⁶ (Co³⁺ in [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺): t₂g⁶: CFSE = −2.4Δo.

Colour: due to d–d transition: the complex absorbs light of energy equal to Δo and shows the complementary colour. [Ti(H₂O)₆]³⁺ (d¹) absorbs ~500 nm (green-yellow) and looks purple. Colour depends on ligand (Δo) and metal. d⁰ and d¹⁰ ions (Sc³⁺, Zn²⁺) are colourless. Limitations of CFT: ligands as point charges only, ignores covalency, cannot explain the position of anions (Cl⁻) vs neutral ligands (NH₃) in the spectrochemical series.

9.5 Stability of Coordination Compounds in Solution

Formation: M + 4L ⇌ ML₄. Stability constant (formation constant) K = [ML₄]/([M][L]⁴); stepwise constants K₁, K₂,… and overall β₄ = K₁K₂K₃K₄. Higher K means more stable complex. Factors: higher charge and smaller size of metal ion; stronger basic ligand; chelate effect (chelates, e.g. [Ni(en)₃]²⁺, are far more stable than complexes of unidentate ligands because of entropy gain). Instability constant = 1/β. Labile complexes exchange ligands quickly; inert slowly (kinetic term, different from thermodynamic stability).

9.6 Applications of Coordination Compounds

Qualitative analysis: Cu²⁺ + NH₃ → deep blue [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺; Ni²⁺ + dimethylglyoxime (DMG) → red ppt; Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ → blood-red [Fe(SCN)]²⁺. Hardness of water: EDTA titration forms stable Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ complexes. Metallurgy: extraction of Ag/Au as cyanide complexes, Ni by Mond process. Biological: haemoglobin (Fe²⁺ complex, O₂ carrier), chlorophyll (Mg), vitamin B₁₂ (Co). Medicine: cisplatin in cancer, EDTA in lead/mercury poisoning (chelation therapy). Photography: hypo dissolves unreacted AgBr: AgBr + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → [Ag(S₂O₃)₂]³⁻ + Br⁻. Electroplating: [Ag(CN)₂]⁻ gives uniform smooth deposit. Catalysis: Wilkinson's catalyst [(Ph₃P)₃RhCl] for hydrogenation; Ziegler–Natta catalyst for polymerisation.

9.7 Bonding in Metal Carbonyls

Homoleptic carbonyls contain only CO ligands: Ni(CO)₄ (tetrahedral), Fe(CO)₅ (trigonal bipyramidal), Cr(CO)₆ (octahedral), Mn₂(CO)₁₀ and Co₂(CO)₈ (contain M–M bond). The metal is in oxidation state zero. Bonding is synergic: (i) a σ-bond formed by donation of the lone pair on carbon of CO into a vacant metal orbital; (ii) a π-bond formed by back donation of electrons from a filled metal d-orbital into the vacant antibonding π* orbital of CO. This strengthens M–C and weakens C–O bond (C–O length increases, IR stretching frequency lowers). Effective atomic number (EAN) = Z − oxidation number + 2 × CN; in Ni(CO)₄: 28 − 0 + 8 = 36 (krypton); Fe(CO)₅: 26 + 10 = 36; Cr(CO)₆: 24 + 12 = 36.

Questions & Answers (short, long, MCQ)

Q1. State the postulates of Werner's theory.

Metals have primary valence (ionisable, equals oxidation state) and secondary valence (non-ionisable, equals coordination number, directional); both are satisfied; secondary valences have fixed geometry.

Q2. Distinguish a double salt from a complex.

A double salt dissociates completely into all constituent ions in water (Mohr's salt); a complex retains the complex ion (K₄[Fe(CN)₆] gives K⁺ and [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻).

Q3. What are ambidentate ligands? Give examples.

Ligands with two different donor atoms, either of which can bind to the metal: NO₂⁻ (N or O), SCN⁻ (S or N).

Q4. Why does CoCl₃·5NH₃ give only 2 moles of AgCl?

Its formula is [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂; one Cl⁻ is inside the coordination sphere and only 2 Cl⁻ are ionisable.

Q5. Write the IUPAC name of K₃[Fe(CN)₆].

Potassium hexacyanidoferrate(III): Fe oxidation state = +3 (3 + x − 6 = 0).

Q6. Write the formula for tetraamminedichloridoplatinum(IV) chloride.

[Pt(NH₃)₄Cl₂]Cl₂

Q7. What is linkage isomerism? Example.

Isomerism from ambidentate ligands bonded through different atoms: [Co(NH₃)₅(NO₂)]²⁺ and [Co(NH₃)₅(ONO)]²⁺.

Q8. Does [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] show geometrical isomerism? Explain.

Yes, as square planar [MA₂B₂]: cis (identical ligands adjacent) and trans (opposite).

Q9. Why are tetrahedral complexes not optically/geometrically isomeric in general?

Four positions are all adjacent to each other, so no cis–trans arrangement exists; optical isomerism occurs only if four different ligands.

Q10. Why is [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻ diamagnetic but [NiCl₄]²⁻ paramagnetic?

CN⁻ is a strong field ligand and pairs the d⁸ electrons giving dsp² square planar (0 unpaired). Cl⁻ is weak, so sp³ tetrahedral with 2 unpaired electrons.

Q11. What is crystal field splitting? Why Δt = 4/9 Δo?

Splitting of d-orbitals by ligand field. In a tetrahedral field only four ligands and none points directly at orbitals, so splitting is smaller: Δt = (4/9)Δo.

Q12. Why is [Ti(H₂O)₆]³⁺ coloured but [Sc(H₂O)₆]³⁺ colourless?

Ti³⁺ is d¹, so the electron undergoes d–d transition absorbing visible light; Sc³⁺ is d⁰, so no d–d transition.

Q13. What is chelate effect? Why are chelates more stable?

Ring formation by didentate/polydentate ligands gives a large entropy increase (more particles released), so the complex is more stable.

Q14. Explain synergic bonding in metal carbonyls.

σ donation from CO carbon to metal and π back-donation from metal d to CO π*. Each strengthens the other, so M–C is strengthened and C–O weakened.

Q15. MCQ: The coordination number of Cu in [Cu(en)₂]²⁺ is (a) 2 (b) 4 (c) 6 (d) 3

(b) 4, because each en is didentate (2 donor N atoms).

Numericals – step by step (Easy → Hard)

Easy: Find the oxidation state of Co in [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂.

Step 1: Two Cl⁻ are outside, so the complex ion is [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]²⁺. Step 2: NH₃ = 0, Cl⁻ = −1. Step 3: x + 0 + (−1) = +2, so x = +3.

Intermediate: Calculate the spin-only magnetic moment of [CoF₆]³⁻ (F⁻ weak field, Z of Co = 27).

Step 1: Co³⁺ = 3d⁶. Step 2: F⁻ is weak field, so high spin: t₂g⁴ e_g² → unpaired electrons n = 4. Step 3: μ = √[n(n+2)] = √24. Answer: 4.90 BM (paramagnetic, sp³d² outer orbital).

Hard: 0.1 mol of CoCl₃·5NH₃ gives 28.7 g AgCl with excess AgNO₃ (AgCl = 143.5 g/mol). Find the formula of the complex.

Step 1: moles AgCl = 28.7/143.5 = 0.2. Step 2: moles Cl⁻ precipitated per mole complex = 0.2/0.1 = 2. Step 3: So 2 Cl⁻ are outside, 1 Cl⁻ is inside the sphere. Step 4: Co has CN 6 = 5 NH₃ + 1 Cl. Answer: [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂.
