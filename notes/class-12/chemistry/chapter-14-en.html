14.1 The Cell, Cellular Energetics and Energy Cycle

Biomolecules are the organic molecules (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, vitamins, enzymes, hormones) that build and run living organisms. The cell is the structural and functional unit of life.

The Cell

  • Prokaryotic cells (bacteria): no true nucleus. Eukaryotic cells (plants, animals): membrane-bound nucleus and organelles.
  • Cell membrane: controls entry and exit of substances. Nucleus: holds DNA, controls cell activities. Mitochondria: “powerhouse”, site of aerobic respiration and ATP production. Ribosomes: protein synthesis. Endoplasmic reticulum: transport and synthesis. Golgi body: packing and secretion. Lysosomes: digestion (“suicide bags”). Chloroplasts (plants): photosynthesis. Cell wall (plants): cellulose.

Cellular Energetics

Metabolism = anabolism (building up, uses energy) + catabolism (breaking down, releases energy). The energy currency is ATP (adenosine triphosphate): adenine + ribose + three phosphate groups. Hydrolysis of the terminal phosphate (ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ) releases about 30.5 kJ/mol (the phosphoanhydride bonds are called “high-energy” bonds). ADP is converted back to ATP using energy from food oxidation.

Breaking down glucose (cellular respiration)

  1. Glycolysis (cytoplasm, no O₂ needed): glucose (6C) → 2 pyruvate (3C); net 2 ATP and 2 NADH.
  2. Krebs (citric acid) cycle (mitochondrial matrix): acetyl-CoA is oxidised to CO₂; produces NADH, FADH₂, a little ATP.
  3. Electron transport chain / oxidative phosphorylation (inner mitochondrial membrane): NADH and FADH₂ pass electrons to O₂, forming H₂O and a large amount of ATP. Total about 30–32 ATP per glucose (older books say 38).
  4. Without O₂ (anaerobic): pyruvate → lactic acid (muscles) or ethanol + CO₂ (yeast), giving only 2 ATP.

14.2 Carbohydrates

Optically active polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds that give them on hydrolysis. General formula often Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ. Called “sugars” if sweet.

Classification

  • Monosaccharides: cannot be hydrolysed further (glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose). Aldoses (–CHO) and ketoses (C=O). By carbon count: triose, tetrose, pentose, hexose.
  • Oligosaccharides: 2–10 units. Disaccharides (sucrose, maltose, lactose) give two monosaccharides.
  • Polysaccharides: many units (starch, cellulose, glycogen); not sweet.
  • Reducing sugars have a free aldehyde/hemiacetal group and reduce Tollens’ and Fehling’s (glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose). Non-reducing: sucrose (both anomeric carbons are used in the glycosidic bond), starch, cellulose.

Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆, dextrose, grape sugar)

Preparation: (1) Sucrose + H₂O → glucose + fructose (dil. HCl or H₂SO₄ in alcohol). (2) Starch hydrolysis (dil. H₂SO₄, 393 K, under pressure) → glucose (commercial).

Open-chain structure and evidence

CHO–CHOH–CHOH–CHOH–CHOH–CH₂OH (D-glucose, (+)).

  • Molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆ (analysis).
  • HI + red P, heat → n-hexane: six carbons in a straight chain.
  • Reaction with NH₂OH → oxime and HCN → cyanohydrin: a carbonyl group (–CHO since oxidation keeps the 6 carbons).
  • Bromine water → gluconic acid: confirms –CHO (a mild oxidant keeps C6).
  • Acetic anhydride → pentaacetate: five –OH groups.
  • Conc. HNO₃ → saccharic (glucaric) acid, a dicarboxylic acid: a primary –OH is present at the other end.

Facts the open chain cannot explain

  • No Schiff’s test and no NaHSO₃ addition; glucose pentaacetate does not react with NH₂OH.
  • Mutarotation: freshly prepared α-D-glucose (+111°) or β-D-glucose (+19.2°) in water slowly changes rotation to +52.5° (equilibrium).
  • These facts are explained by the cyclic hemiacetal structure: C5–OH adds to the C1 aldehyde, producing a six-membered pyranose ring with two forms, α and β (anomers; they differ at C1, the anomeric carbon).
  • Haworth structure: α-form has the C1 –OH below the plane (trans to CH₂OH), β-form above.
  • D/L refers to configuration at the highest-numbered chiral carbon relative to glyceraldehyde (–OH on the right in Fischer = D). (+)/(−) or d/l refers to the direction of optical rotation; they are unrelated.
  • Epimers: differ in configuration at only one carbon other than C1 (glucose and galactose at C4; glucose and mannose at C2). Anomers: differ only at C1.

Fructose (C₆H₁₂O₆, fruit sugar)

A ketohexose with C=O at C2; D-(−)-fructose, [α] = −92.4°, sweetest sugar. Forms a five-membered furanose ring in combined form (as in sucrose). Reduces Tollens’ and Fehling’s even though it is a ketose, because it isomerises to glucose in alkali. Glucose and fructose give the same osazone with excess phenylhydrazine.

Sucrose is dextrorotatory (+66.5°). On hydrolysis it gives glucose (+52.5°) and fructose (−92.4°); the mixture is laevorotatory (net about −20°) and is called invert sugar (inversion of rotation).

Polysaccharides

  • Starch (plant storage): about 20% amylose (unbranched α-1,4 chain of α-D-glucose, water-soluble, helical, blue colour with iodine) and 80% amylopectin (branched; α-1,4 chain with α-1,6 branches, insoluble).
  • Cellulose: unbranched β-1,4 chain of β-D-glucose; structural material of plant cell walls; humans lack the enzyme to digest it, but cattle can.
  • Glycogen: “animal starch”, highly branched like amylopectin, stored in liver and muscles.

Importance

Major food energy source (about 17 kJ/g); cellulose for paper and cloth; ethanol by fermentation; storage and structural roles; part of nucleic acids (ribose).

14.3 Proteins

Polymers of α-amino acids (NH₂–CHR–COOH) joined by peptide bonds (–CO–NH–). The name comes from Greek proteios, “of prime importance”.

Amino acids

  • Twenty standard α-amino acids; R is the side chain (glycine R = H, alanine R = CH₃).
  • Essential amino acids cannot be made by the body and must come from food: valine, leucine, isoleucine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, methionine, lysine, threonine, histidine (arginine in children). Non-essential ones are made in the body (glycine, alanine, glutamic acid…).
  • Zwitterion: in water the –COOH gives H⁺ to –NH₂, forming ⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻ (high m.p., soluble in water, crystalline salt-like). Amino acids are amphoteric.
  • Isoelectric point (pI): the pH at which the amino acid has zero net charge; it does not move in an electric field and its solubility is least.

Peptide bond

–COOH of one amino acid + –NH₂ of another → –CO–NH– + H₂O. Dipeptide has two amino acids and one peptide bond; a tripeptide has two bonds. Glycylalanine: H₂N–CH₂–CO–NH–CH(CH₃)–COOH. Written with the N-terminal on the left.

Classification

  • By shape: fibrous (keratin in hair, myosin in muscle; long thread-like, insoluble in water) and globular (insulin, albumin, haemoglobin; spherical, soluble).
  • By composition: simple (only amino acids) and conjugated (with a non-protein part, e.g. nucleoproteins, glycoproteins).

Structure of proteins

LevelDescription
PrimarySequence of amino acids (peptide bonds); change in one amino acid can change function (e.g. sickle-cell anaemia)
SecondaryRegular folding held by H-bonds between C=O and N–H: α-helix (right-handed, 3.6 residues per turn) and β-pleated sheet (chains side by side)
TertiaryOverall 3D folding of a chain: H-bonds, disulphide (–S–S–) bridges, ionic, van der Waals and hydrophobic interactions
QuaternaryArrangement of two or more polypeptide subunits (haemoglobin has four)

Denaturation

Heat, pH change, UV, heavy metals, alcohol break the weak bonds, destroying secondary and tertiary structure (primary is intact) and biological activity; usually irreversible. Examples: boiling an egg (albumin coagulates), curdling of milk.

Tests

  • Biuret test: NaOH + dilute CuSO₄ → violet colour (peptide bonds; at least two).
  • Ninhydrin test: blue-violet with α-amino acids and proteins (proline gives yellow).
  • Xanthoproteic test: conc. HNO₃ → yellow (aromatic amino acids).
  • Millon’s test: red colour (tyrosine).

Functions

Enzymes, hormones (insulin), transport (haemoglobin), structure (keratin, collagen), defence (antibodies), contraction (myosin).

14.4 Enzymes

Biological catalysts, nearly all of them proteins, that speed up reactions in living cells without being consumed.

Characteristics

  • Highly specific for a substrate (and reaction).
  • Very efficient: small amount of enzyme converts a large amount of substrate.
  • Optimum temperature about 298–310 K; denatured at high temperature, inactive at low.
  • Optimum pH: pepsin 1.5–2; salivary amylase about 6.8; trypsin about 8.
  • Activity changes with activators (co-enzymes, often derived from vitamins; metal ions) and inhibitors (competitive or non-competitive).

Mechanism

Lock-and-key model (Fischer): the substrate fits the enzyme’s active site like a key in a lock. Induced-fit model (Koshland): the active site changes shape to fit the substrate. Steps: E + S ⇌ ES complex → E + P. The enzyme lowers the activation energy.

Examples: amylase (starch → maltose), maltase (maltose → glucose), invertase (sucrose → glucose + fructose), zymase (glucose → ethanol + CO₂), urease (urea → NH₃ + CO₂), pepsin and trypsin (proteins → peptides), lipase (fats → glycerol + fatty acids), lactase (lactose → glucose + galactose).

Uses: fermentation, detergents, medicines, diagnosis (blood enzymes); lack of an enzyme causes metabolic disorders.

14.5 Hormones

Chemical messengers secreted in tiny amounts by endocrine (ductless) glands directly into blood, acting on target organs to coordinate growth, metabolism and reproduction.

TypeExamples
SteroidsTestosterone, estradiol, progesterone (sex hormones); cortisol, aldosterone (adrenal cortex)
Polypeptides / proteinsInsulin (pancreas, 51 amino acids in two chains A = 21, B = 30), glucagon, oxytocin (9 amino acids), vasopressin, growth hormone
Amino-acid derivativesThyroxine (thyroid), adrenaline/epinephrine (adrenal medulla)
  • Insulin lowers blood glucose; deficiency causes diabetes mellitus. Glucagon raises it.
  • Thyroxine controls metabolic rate; iodine deficiency gives goitre; excess gives hyperthyroidism.
  • Adrenaline: “fight or flight” (raises heart rate and glucose).
  • Growth hormone (pituitary): deficiency causes dwarfism, excess causes gigantism.
  • Testosterone: male characteristics; estradiol/progesterone: female characteristics and pregnancy.

14.6 Nucleic Acids

Long-chain polymers of nucleotides: DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) stores genetic information; RNA (ribonucleic acid) helps in protein synthesis. First isolated by Miescher (1869) from the cell nucleus.

Components

  • Pentose sugar: 2-deoxy-D-ribose in DNA; D-ribose in RNA.
  • Phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄).
  • Nitrogenous bases: purines adenine (A) and guanine (G); pyrimidines cytosine (C), thymine (T, DNA only) and uracil (U, RNA only).
  • Nucleoside = sugar + base (base at C1′); nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphate (at C5′). Nucleotides join through phosphodiester bonds between C3′ of one sugar and C5′ of the next.

Structure of DNA (Watson and Crick, 1953)

  • Two right-handed, antiparallel polynucleotide strands wound into a double helix, sugar–phosphate backbone outside, bases inside.
  • Base pairing by H-bonds: A = T (two H-bonds), G ≡ C (three H-bonds). Chargaff: [A] = [T], [G] = [C].
  • Diameter about 2 nm; one turn (pitch) about 3.4 nm with 10 base pairs (0.34 nm per pair).
  • Replication is semi-conservative: each new DNA has one old and one new strand.

RNA

Single-stranded; uracil replaces thymine; ribose sugar. Three types: mRNA (carries the genetic message), rRNA (part of ribosomes), tRNA (brings amino acids).

Biological functions

DNA is the chemical basis of heredity and carries replication. Protein synthesis: transcription (DNA → mRNA) then translation (mRNA → protein). A group of three bases forms a codon for one amino acid (64 codons: 61 code amino acids, 3 are stop codons). The genetic code is universal, degenerate and non-overlapping. A change in the base sequence is a mutation. DNA fingerprinting identifies individuals.

14.7 Vitamins

Organic compounds required in small amounts for normal growth and health; they cannot be synthesised by the body (except a few) and are supplied in the diet. Their absence causes deficiency diseases. Named by letters (Hopkins and Funk, 1912).

  • Fat-soluble: A, D, E, K — stored in liver and fatty tissue.
  • Water-soluble: B complex and C — excreted in urine, so needed regularly (B₁₂ is stored in the liver).

Solved Numericals (step by step)

Easy: Mass of glucose from complete hydrolysis of 171 g sucrose

C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (342 g/mol) + H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose) + C₆H₁₂O₆ (fructose).
Step 1: moles sucrose = 171/342 = 0.5. Step 2: 1 mol gives 1 mol glucose, so 0.5 mol. Step 3: mass = 0.5 × 180 = 90 g (and 90 g of fructose).

Easy: A DNA sample has 30% adenine. Find the other bases.

Chargaff: A = T, so T = 30%. A + T = 60%, remaining 40% is G + C, equal in amount, so G = C = 20% each.

Intermediate: Specific rotation of invert sugar (equimolar glucose and fructose)

Glucose +52.5°, fructose −92.4°. For equal amounts, average = (52.5 − 92.4)/2 = −39.9/2 = −19.95° ≈ −20°, so the mixture is laevorotatory.

Hard: At equilibrium [α] = +52.5°. Find % α and β anomers (α = +111°, β = +19.2°).

Let fraction of α be x. Then 111x + 19.2(1 − x) = 52.5.
111x + 19.2 − 19.2x = 52.5 → 91.8x = 33.3 → x = 0.363.
α = 36.3%, β = 100 − 36.3 = 63.7%.

Hard: Respiration of 1 mol glucose gives 2880 kJ. If 30 mol ATP form (30.5 kJ each), find the efficiency.

Energy stored = 30 × 30.5 = 915 kJ.
Efficiency = (915/2880) × 100 = 31.8%; the rest is lost as heat.

Q & A and MCQs

Q1. What are essential amino acids? Give two examples.

Amino acids the body cannot synthesise and which must come from the diet; e.g. valine and lysine.

Q2. Why is sucrose a non-reducing sugar?

The anomeric carbons of glucose (C1) and fructose (C2) are both used in the glycosidic bond, so no free aldehyde/ketone group is available.

Q3. What is mutarotation?

The spontaneous change in optical rotation of a freshly prepared sugar solution until equilibrium, due to interconversion of α and β anomers through the open chain.

Q4. Differentiate between amylose and amylopectin.

Amylose: linear α-1,4, soluble, about 20%. Amylopectin: branched (α-1,4 and α-1,6), insoluble, about 80%.

Q5. What are zwitterions and isoelectric point?

A zwitterion is a dipolar ion, ⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻. The isoelectric point is the pH at which its net charge is zero.

Q6. What happens to a protein on denaturation?

Secondary and tertiary structures are destroyed while the primary structure stays; the protein loses biological activity (e.g. egg albumin coagulates).

Q7. Why are enzymes specific?

Only a substrate with the right shape and groups fits the active site (lock-and-key / induced fit).

Q8. Name the base pairs of DNA and the bonds between them.

A–T (two hydrogen bonds), G–C (three hydrogen bonds).

Q9. Differentiate nucleoside and nucleotide.

Nucleoside = sugar + base; nucleotide = sugar + base + phosphate.

Q10. Why must water-soluble vitamins be supplied regularly?

They are excreted in urine and are not stored in the body (except B₁₂).

Q11. What is the difference between a fibrous and a globular protein?

Fibrous: thread-like, insoluble in water, e.g. keratin. Globular: spherical, soluble, e.g. insulin.

Q12. What is an anomer? Give an example.

Two cyclic forms that differ only in the configuration at the anomeric C1: α-D-glucose and β-D-glucose.
