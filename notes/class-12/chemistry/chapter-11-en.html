11.1 Classification of Hydroxy Derivatives

Alcohols have –OH on an sp³ carbon; phenols have –OH on an sp² carbon of a benzene ring; ethers have R–O–R′.

  • By number of –OH: monohydric (CH₃OH), dihydric (glycol), trihydric (glycerol), polyhydric.
  • Monohydric alcohols by carbon bearing –OH: primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°).
  • By hybridisation: C(sp³)–OH: allylic (CH₂=CH–CH₂OH), benzylic (C₆H₅CH₂OH). C(sp²)–OH: vinylic (CH₂=CH–OH, unstable) and phenols.
  • Ethers: simple/symmetrical (C₂H₅OC₂H₅), mixed/unsymmetrical (CH₃OC₂H₅).

Alcohol: replace “e” of alkane with “ol”, number the chain from the end nearer –OH. Ether: alkoxy-alkane (smaller R becomes alkoxy).

Structure: in alcohol the C–O–H angle is about 108.9° (less than tetrahedral 109.5° because of lone-pair repulsion); in ether C–O–C is about 111.7° (larger because of repulsion of bulky alkyl groups). O is sp³.

11.2 Alcohols

Preparation

  1. Hydration of alkenes: acid-catalysed, follows Markovnikov (propene → propan-2-ol). Hydroboration–oxidation (B₂H₆ then H₂O₂/OH⁻): anti-Markovnikov, gives 1° alcohol from a terminal alkene, syn addition.
  2. Reduction of carbonyls: aldehyde → 1° alcohol; ketone → 2° alcohol; carboxylic acid/ester → 1° alcohol. Reagents: H₂/Pd, NaBH₄ (aldehydes and ketones only), LiAlH₄ (all, including acids and esters; in dry ether).
  3. Grignard reagent: HCHO + RMgX → 1° alcohol; other RCHO → 2°; ketone → 3° (add water/H⁺ afterwards). Ethylene oxide + RMgX → 1° alcohol with two extra carbons.
  4. Hydrolysis of alkyl halides: R–X + aq. KOH → R–OH.

Physical Properties

  • Boiling points are much higher than those of alkanes, ethers, haloalkanes of similar mass because of intermolecular H-bonding. Among isomers: 1° > 2° > 3° (branching reduces surface area).
  • Lower alcohols are soluble in water (H-bonding with water); solubility falls as the alkyl part grows.

Acidity

R–OH ⇌ RO⁻ + H⁺. Alcohols are weaker acids than water (pKa ethanol ≈ 15.9). Order: CH₃OH > 1° > 2° > 3° because alkyl groups (+I) destabilise the alkoxide. Reaction with sodium: 2R–OH + 2Na → 2RONa + H₂↑.

Chemical Reactions

  1. Esterification: R–OH + R′COOH ⇌ R′COOR + H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄); with acid chloride/anhydride + pyridine, irreversible. Aspirin is made by acetylation of salicylic acid.
  2. Reaction with HX (Lucas test): conc. HCl + ZnCl₂. 3° alcohol: turbidity at once; 2°: in about 5 minutes; 1°: no turbidity at room temperature.
  3. Dehydration: conc. H₂SO₄ at 443 K gives alkene; ease 3° (358 K) > 2° (440 K) > 1° (443 K); at 413 K with excess alcohol gives ether.
  4. Oxidation: 1° → aldehyde (PCC, or Cu at 573 K) → carboxylic acid (KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, CrO₃). 2° → ketone. 3° resists mild oxidation; under strong conditions C–C breaks.
  5. Dehydrogenation (Cu, 573 K): 1° → aldehyde, 2° → ketone, 3° → alkene.

11.3 Phenols

Preparation

  1. From haloarenes: chlorobenzene + NaOH, 623 K, 320 atm, then HCl.
  2. From benzenesulphonic acid: fuse with NaOH, then acidify.
  3. From diazonium salts: warm with water (or dilute H₂SO₄).
  4. Cumene process (industrial): cumene + O₂ → cumene hydroperoxide; with dilute acid → phenol + acetone.

Properties

H-bonding gives high b.p.; phenol is sparingly soluble in water. It is more acidic than alcohols (pKa about 10) since the phenoxide ion is stabilised by resonance (negative charge delocalised on ortho and para carbons) whereas in an alkoxide it is localised on O. Electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂) at o/p increase acidity (p-nitrophenol > phenol); electron-releasing groups (–CH₃) decrease it. Phenol does not react with NaHCO₃ (weaker than carbonic acid), but reacts with NaOH.

Reactions

  1. Bromination: Br₂ water → 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white precipitate); Br₂ in CS₂ at low temperature → o- and p-bromophenol (mainly para).
  2. Nitration: dilute HNO₃ at 298 K → o- and p-nitrophenol (o- is steam volatile due to intramolecular H-bonding, p- is not; this separates them); conc. HNO₃ → 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid).
  3. Kolbe’s reaction: sodium phenoxide + CO₂ (400 K, 4–7 atm), then H⁺ → salicylic acid.
  4. Reimer–Tiemann: phenol + CHCl₃ + aq. NaOH (340 K), then H⁺ → salicylaldehyde (intermediate: dichlorocarbene).
  5. Reduction: Zn dust, heat → benzene. Oxidation: Na₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄ → benzoquinone.
  6. Test: neutral FeCl₃ gives violet colour.

11.4 Commercially Important Alcohols

Methanol (wood spirit)

CO + 2H₂ → CH₃OH (ZnO–Cr₂O₃, 573–673 K, 200–300 atm). Colourless, poisonous liquid; 10 mL can cause blindness and 30 mL is fatal. Used as solvent, in paints, and to make formaldehyde.

Ethanol

By fermentation: sucrose (invertase) → glucose + fructose; C₆H₁₂O₆ (zymase) → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂. Fermentation needs air excluded, 298–303 K, and stops at about 14% ethanol as yeast dies. Denatured alcohol: ethanol mixed with CuSO₄ (colour) and pyridine (smell) so it cannot be drunk. Absolute alcohol (100%) is obtained by azeotropic distillation of the 95.6% mixture with benzene.

11.5 Dihydric and Trihydric Alcohols

  • Ethylene glycol: from ethene + cold dilute alkaline KMnO₄ (Baeyer’s reagent), or hydrolysis of ethylene oxide. Sweet, viscous; high b.p. (H-bonding). Used as antifreeze and in making terylene (PET).
  • Glycerol: from saponification of fats and oils (by-product of soap). With conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄ gives nitroglycerine (explosive, used in dynamite). Used in cosmetics, medicines, as humectant. Dehydration with KHSO₄ gives acrolein (pungent smell, test for glycerol).

11.6 Ethers

Preparation

  1. Dehydration of alcohols: excess alcohol, conc. H₂SO₄, 413 K (works for 1° only).
  2. Williamson synthesis: R–ONa + R′–X → R–O–R′ (SN2). Use a 1° alkyl halide; 3° halide gives alkene by elimination. For tert-butyl methyl ether use sodium tert-butoxide + CH₃Br, not the reverse.

Properties

No H-bonding between ether molecules, so b.p. is close to alkanes and much lower than isomeric alcohols (ethoxyethane 308 K vs butan-1-ol 391 K). Ethers dissolve in water to a small extent (H-bonding with water).

Reactions

  1. Cleavage by HX: R–O–R′ + HX → R–X + R′OH; with excess HX both become halides. Order HI > HBr > HCl. For 1° alkyl groups (SN2) the halide attaches to the smaller group (CH₃OC₂H₅ + HI → CH₃I + C₂H₅OH). If one group is 3° the reaction is SN1, the 3° halide forms: (CH₃)₃C–O–CH₃ + HI → (CH₃)₃C–I + CH₃OH. Anisole + HI → phenol + CH₃I (O–aryl bond never cleaves, as C(sp²)–O has partial double-bond character).
  2. Electrophilic substitution of anisole: –OCH₃ is activating and o/p-directing. Bromination (Br₂, ethanoic acid) → p-bromoanisole (major); Friedel–Crafts alkylation/acylation (CH₃Cl or CH₃COCl, anhyd. AlCl₃) → o and p products (p- major); nitration (HNO₃/H₂SO₄) → o- and p-nitroanisole.

Solved Numericals (step by step)

Easy: Mass of ethanol from 90 g glucose by fermentation (100% yield)

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂. Molar mass: glucose 180, ethanol 46.
Step 1: moles glucose = 90/180 = 0.5. Step 2: 1 mol gives 2 mol ethanol, so 1.0 mol. Step 3: mass = 1.0 × 46 = 46 g.

Intermediate: 120 g cumene gives phenol with 75% yield. Find mass of phenol.

Cumene C₉H₁₂ = 120 g/mol; phenol C₆H₆O = 94 g/mol; 1 mol cumene → 1 mol phenol.
Theoretical = 94 g. Actual = 94 × 75/100 = 70.5 g. (Acetone formed too.)

Hard: Ka of phenol = 1.3 × 10⁻¹⁰. Find pH of 0.1 M solution.

PhOH ⇌ PhO⁻ + H⁺; Ka = x²/(0.1 − x) ≈ x²/0.1 (x is very small).
x² = 1.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 0.1 = 1.3 × 10⁻¹¹. x = 3.6 × 10⁻⁶ M.
pH = −log(3.6 × 10⁻⁶) = 6 − 0.556 = 5.44.

Q & A and MCQs

Q1. Why is the boiling point of ethanol higher than that of methoxymethane?

Ethanol has intermolecular H-bonds; methoxymethane (same mass, 46) has none.

Q2. Why is phenol more acidic than ethanol?

Phenoxide is resonance-stabilised (charge on ortho and para carbons); ethoxide has a localised charge, made worse by the +I effect of ethyl.

Q3. Why is o-nitrophenol more volatile than p-nitrophenol?

o-isomer has intramolecular H-bonding (no association), p-isomer has intermolecular H-bonding (association).

Q4. Why does Williamson synthesis fail with tertiary alkyl halide?

The alkoxide is a strong base, so E2 elimination dominates and gives an alkene.

Q5. Why is phenol brominated without a catalyst?

–OH is strongly activating (+R), so the ring is electron-rich at o/p positions.

Q6. How do you distinguish 1°, 2°, 3° alcohols?

Lucas test: 3° turbid immediately, 2° after about 5 min, 1° no turbidity at room temperature.

Q7. Why do ethers not react with sodium?

They have no acidic hydrogen on oxygen. This distinguishes them from alcohols.

Q8. Why does anisole + HI give phenol and not iodobenzene?

The C(sp²)–O bond is stronger (partial double bond by resonance); I⁻ attacks the methyl carbon by SN2.

Q9. Why is denatured alcohol undrinkable?

It contains poisonous additives (methanol, pyridine, CuSO₄) added to prevent misuse.

MCQ1. Reduction of acetone with NaBH₄ gives: (a) propan-1-ol (b) propan-2-ol (c) propanal (d) propanoic acid

Answer: (b), ketone → 2° alcohol.

MCQ2. Cumene process gives phenol and: (a) acetone (b) acetaldehyde (c) formaldehyde (d) benzaldehyde

Answer: (a).

MCQ3. Reimer–Tiemann reaction on phenol gives: (a) salicylic acid (b) salicylaldehyde (c) picric acid (d) benzoquinone

Answer: (b).

MCQ4. Most acidic: (a) phenol (b) p-cresol (c) p-nitrophenol (d) ethanol

Answer: (c), –NO₂ stabilises the phenoxide.

MCQ5. Reagent for converting ethanol to ethanal: (a) PCC (b) LiAlH₄ (c) conc. H₂SO₄ (d) Na

Answer: (a), PCC stops at the aldehyde.
