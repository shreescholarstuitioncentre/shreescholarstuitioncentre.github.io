4.1 Reaction Rate, its Measurement and its Dependence on Various Factors

Rate of reaction: change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. Unit: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (for gases atm s⁻¹).

Average rate = −Δ[R]/Δt = +Δ[P]/Δt. Instantaneous rate = rate at one instant = slope of tangent to the concentration–time curve = −d[R]/dt. For aA + bB → cC + dD:

Rate = −(1/a) d[A]/dt = −(1/b) d[B]/dt = +(1/c) d[C]/dt = +(1/d) d[D]/dt

Measurement: titration of aliquots, pressure change (gases), conductivity, colour (colorimeter), polarimetry (sucrose inversion), volume of gas evolved.

Factors: concentration (more collisions), temperature, catalyst, nature of reactants, surface area (finely divided = faster), radiation/light.

4.2 Dependence of Reaction Rate on Concentration of Reactants

Rate law: Rate = k [A]x [B]y ; Order n = x + y

k = rate constant (rate at unit concentrations). Order is found experimentally and may be zero, fractional or whole; it is not obtained from the balanced equation.

OrderUnit of k
0mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹
1s⁻¹
2L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹
n(mol L⁻¹)1−n s⁻¹

Pseudo first order: a higher-order reaction that follows first-order kinetics because one reactant is in large excess: hydrolysis of ethyl acetate (water in excess), inversion of cane sugar.

4.3 Zero Order Reactions

R → P ; Rate = k[R]⁰ = k (independent of concentration).

[R] = [R]₀ − kt ; t½ = [R]₀/2k

Derivation: −d[R]/dt = k → [R] = −kt + I; at t = 0, I = [R]₀. Plot of [R] vs t is a straight line of slope −k. Examples: decomposition of NH₃ on hot Pt, HI on gold; photochemical H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl.

4.4 First Order Reactions

Rate = k[R]. Integrated forms:

ln[R] = ln[R]₀ − kt ; k = (2.303/t) log([R]₀/[R]) ; [R] = [R]₀e−kt
t½ = 0.693/k (independent of initial concentration)

Plot of ln[R] vs t (or log[R] vs t) is a straight line, slope = −k (−k/2.303). For gas-phase A → B + C: k = (2.303/t) log[pi/(2pi − pt)]. Examples: decomposition of N₂O₅ and H₂O₂, radioactive decay, hydrolysis of esters (pseudo). Time for 99.9% completion = 10 t½ (approximately).

4.5 Experimental Determination of Rate Law, Rate Constant and Order

(i) Initial rate method: vary one concentration keeping others fixed and compare rates. (ii) Integrated rate law method: put data in zero/first/second order equations; the one that gives constant k is correct. (iii) Graphical: straight line of [R]–t (zero), ln[R]–t (first). (iv) Half-life method: t½ independent of conc → first order; t½ ∝ [R]₀ → zero order; t½ ∝ 1/[R]₀ → second order.

4.6 Dependence of Reaction Rate on Temperature

Temperature coefficient = k(T+10)/k(T) ≈ 2 to 3. Collision theory: reaction needs collisions that are effective: molecules must have energy ≥ threshold energy and proper orientation. Threshold energy = activation energy Ea + average energy of reactants. Raising T increases the fraction of molecules with E ≥ Ea (see Maxwell–Boltzmann graph).

Arrhenius: k = A e−Ea/RT ; log k = log A − Ea/(2.303RT)
log(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/2.303R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂) = (Ea/2.303R)(T₂ − T₁)/(T₁T₂)

Plot of log k vs 1/T is a straight line; slope = −Ea/2.303R, intercept = log A. Catalyst: gives an alternate path of lower Ea, increases rate, but does not change ΔH, ΔG or equilibrium constant.

4.7 Molecularity of a Reaction

Number of reacting species colliding in an elementary step: unimolecular (1; NH₄NO₂ → N₂ + 2H₂O), bimolecular (2; 2HI → H₂ + I₂), termolecular (3; 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ is rare). Molecularity is a whole number (1, 2, 3), never zero or fractional, and has no meaning for complex reactions.

OrderMolecularity
ExperimentalTheoretical
Zero, fractional or wholeOnly 1, 2, 3
For overall reactionFor each elementary step

4.8 Mechanism of a Reaction

A complex reaction occurs in a series of elementary steps. The slowest step is the rate determining step; overall rate depends only on it. Intermediates formed in one step are consumed in the next.

Example 1: H₂O₂ + 2I⁻ + 2H⁺ → 2H₂O + I₂. Step 1 (slow): H₂O₂ + I⁻ → H₂O + IO⁻. Step 2 (fast): H₂O₂ + IO⁻ → H₂O + O₂ + I⁻. Rate = k[H₂O₂][I⁻]; order 2.

Example 2: NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂ (below 500 K). Step 1 (slow): 2NO₂ → NO + NO₃. Step 2 (fast): NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂. Rate = k[NO₂]².

Example 1: [R] changes from 0.5 M to 0.4 M in 10 s. Find average rate.

To find: rate

Rate = −Δ[R]/Δt = (0.5 − 0.4)/10 = 0.01 mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.

Example 2: Exp1: [A]=0.1,[B]=0.1, rate=2.0×10⁻³; Exp2: [A]=0.2,[B]=0.1, rate=4.0×10⁻³; Exp3: [A]=0.1,[B]=0.2, rate=8.0×10⁻³. Find rate law, order and k.

To find: x, y, k

Exp2/Exp1: 2ˣ = 2 → x = 1. Exp3/Exp1: 2ʸ = 4 → y = 2.
Rate = k[A][B]², order = 3.
k = rate/([A][B]²) = 2.0×10⁻³/(0.1×0.01) = 2 L² mol⁻² s⁻¹.

Example 3: A first order reaction is 75% complete in 32 min. Find k and t½.

To find: k, t½

[R]₀ = 100, [R] = 25. k = (2.303/32) log 4 = (2.303×0.6021)/32 = 1.3866/32 = 0.0433 min⁻¹.
t½ = 0.693/0.0433 = 16 min (consistent: 75% = 2 half-lives).

Example 4: First order, k = 1.15×10⁻³ s⁻¹. Time for 90% completion.

To find: t

[R]₀/[R] = 100/10 = 10. t = (2.303/k) log 10 = 2.303/(1.15×10⁻³) = 2002 s ≈ 33.4 min.

Example 5: Zero order, [R]₀ = 0.5 M, k = 0.01 mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. Find t½ and time for completion.

To find: t½, t

t½ = [R]₀/2k = 0.5/0.02 = 25 s. Completion: [R] = 0 → t = [R]₀/k = 0.5/0.01 = 50 s.

Example 6: Rate doubles when T rises from 298 K to 308 K. Find Ea (R = 8.314).

To find: Ea

log 2 = (Ea/2.303R)(T₂−T₁)/(T₁T₂).
0.3010 = Ea×10/(19.147×91784) → Ea = 0.3010×19.147×91784/10 = ≈ 52.9 kJ mol⁻¹.

Example 7: Ea = 50 kJ mol⁻¹. Find k₂/k₁ for 300 K → 310 K.

To find: k₂/k₁

log(k₂/k₁) = (50000/19.147)×(10/(300×310)) = 2611.3×1.0753×10⁻⁴ = 0.2808.
k₂/k₁ = antilog(0.2808) = 1.91.

Example 8: Prove k = (2.303/t) log([R]₀/[R]) and t½ = 0.693/k for first order.

To find: proof

−d[R]/dt = k[R] → d[R]/[R] = −k dt. Integrate: ln[R] = −kt + I. At t = 0: I = ln[R]₀.
So ln([R]₀/[R]) = kt → k = (2.303/t) log([R]₀/[R]).
At t½, [R] = [R]₀/2: kt½ = ln 2 = 0.693 → t½ = 0.693/k.

Short & Long Questions

Q1. Define rate of reaction.

Change in concentration of reactant or product per unit time; unit mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.

Q2. Differentiate rate and rate constant.

Rate depends on concentrations and changes with time; k is the rate at unit concentrations and is constant at fixed temperature.

Q3. Define order and molecularity; differences.

See table 4.7: order is experimental, can be 0 or fractional; molecularity is theoretical, only 1, 2, 3.

Q4. What is a pseudo first order reaction? Example.

A reaction of higher order obeying first-order kinetics because a reactant is in large excess, e.g. hydrolysis of ethyl acetate.

Q5. Why is t½ independent of concentration for first order?

t½ = 0.693/k contains no concentration term.

Q6. What is the rate determining step?

The slowest elementary step of a mechanism; it controls the overall rate.

Q7. State Arrhenius equation and explain terms.

k = Ae^(−Ea/RT); A = frequency factor, Ea = activation energy, R = gas constant, T = temperature.

Q8. Why does rate double for 10 K rise?

Fraction of molecules with energy ≥ Ea roughly doubles, though average kinetic energy rises only ~3%.

Q9. What is activation energy?

Minimum extra energy that reactants must acquire to form the activated complex.

Q10. How does a catalyst increase rate?

It provides a new path with lower activation energy; ΔH and equilibrium are unaffected.

Q11. Can order of a reaction be zero? Explain.

Yes; the rate is independent of concentration, e.g. NH₃ on hot platinum.

Q12. Why do reactions with molecularity 3 rarely occur?

Simultaneous collision of three molecules with correct orientation and energy is extremely unlikely.
