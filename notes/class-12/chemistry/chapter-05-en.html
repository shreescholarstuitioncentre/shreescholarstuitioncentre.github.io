Surface Chemistry – Class 12 Chemistry, Ch. 5

5.1 Adsorption

Definition: The accumulation of molecules of a substance (adsorbate) on the surface of a solid or liquid (adsorbent) is called adsorption. Example: charcoal adsorbs NH₃, silica gel adsorbs moisture. It happens because surface atoms have unbalanced (residual) forces.

Adsorption vs Absorption: adsorption = surface phenomenon (concentration on surface is different from bulk); absorption = uniform throughout the bulk (e.g. water in sponge, NH₃ in water).

Other terms: Desorption = removal of adsorbed substance. Sorption = adsorption + absorption together. Occlusion = adsorption of gas on metal (H₂ on Pd).

Factors affecting adsorption of gas on solid

1) Nature of gas: easily liquefiable gases (high critical temperature) adsorb more (NH₃ > CO₂ > H₂). 2) Surface area of adsorbent: more area, more adsorption (finely divided, porous). 3) Pressure: increases with pressure. 4) Temperature: adsorption is exothermic, so by Le Chatelier it decreases with rise in temperature (physisorption).

Adsorption isotherm – Freundlich

x/m = k·P1/n (n > 1)
log(x/m) = log k + (1/n) log P — a graph of log(x/m) vs log P is a straight line with slope 1/n and intercept log k.
At low P: x/m ∝ P; at intermediate P: x/m ∝ P1/n; at high P: x/m is independent of P (saturation).

Applications

Gas masks (charcoal), removing colour from sugar, silica gel to control humidity, heterogeneous catalysis, froth flotation, chromatography, softening of water by ion-exchange resins.

5.2 Catalysis

Catalyst: a substance that alters the rate of a reaction while itself remaining chemically unchanged in mass and composition. Promoter: increases catalyst activity (Mo in Haber process with Fe). Poison: decreases activity (As on Pt in contact process).

Homogeneous: catalyst and reactants in same phase (NO in lead-chamber process; H⁺ in ester hydrolysis). Heterogeneous: different phases (Fe in Haber, V₂O₅ in contact process, Ni in hydrogenation of oil).

Adsorption theory of heterogeneous catalysis

Steps: (1) diffusion of reactants to surface, (2) adsorption on active sites, (3) chemical reaction forming intermediate, (4) desorption of products, (5) products leave, freeing surface for fresh reactants.

Features of solid catalysts

Activity (ability to speed up), selectivity (e.g. CO + H₂ gives CH₄ with Ni, CH₃OH with Cu/ZnO–Cr₂O₃, HCHO with Cu). A catalyst does not change ΔG or equilibrium position; it only reaches equilibrium faster.

Shape-selective catalysis & Enzymes

Zeolites (e.g. ZSM-5) are shape-selective; they convert alcohols to petrol. Enzymes are biological protein catalysts: highly specific (lock-and-key), work at optimum temperature (~298–310 K) and optimum pH, very efficient (work in tiny quantity), and are affected by inhibitors/poisons. Examples: invertase (sucrose→glucose+fructose), zymase (glucose→ethanol), urease (urea→NH₃+CO₂), pepsin, amylase.

5.3 The Colloidal State

True solution: particle size < 1 nm. Colloid: 1–1000 nm. Suspension: > 1000 nm. A colloid has a dispersed phase and a dispersion medium.

Classification

(a) By physical state: sol (solid in liquid), gel (liquid in solid), emulsion (liquid in liquid), foam, aerosol (fog, smoke), etc.
(b) By interaction: Lyophilic (solvent-loving; reversible; stable; gelatin, starch, gum) vs Lyophobic (solvent-hating; irreversible; need stabiliser; metal sols like Au, As₂S₃, Fe(OH)₃).
(c) By particle type: Multimolecular (aggregates of atoms/small molecules, e.g. sulphur sol, gold sol), Macromolecular (large molecules, e.g. starch, proteins, cellulose), Associated colloids (micelles: soaps/detergents above CMC and Kraft temperature).

Micelle formation

Soap RCOO⁻Na⁺ has a hydrophobic tail and a hydrophilic head. Above the critical micelle concentration (CMC) molecules gather with tails inward and heads outward in water; this traps grease and gives the cleansing action.

Preparation

Chemical methods: double decomposition (As₂S₃ sol), oxidation (S sol: H₂S + SO₂), reduction (Au sol), hydrolysis (Fe(OH)₃ from FeCl₃ + hot water). Bredig's arc method (dispersion + condensation) for Au, Ag, Pt. Peptization: freshly precipitated Fe(OH)₃ + small FeCl₃ gives sol.

Purification

Dialysis (through parchment/cellophane membrane; used in artificial kidney), electrodialysis, ultrafiltration.

Properties

1. Tyndall effect – scattering of light by colloidal particles makes the beam path visible (needs particle size close to light wavelength and a large refractive index difference).

2. Brownian motion – zig-zag random motion of particles caused by unequal bombardment by medium molecules; it keeps the sol stable against gravity.

3. Charge: sols carry charge: positive (Fe(OH)₃, Al(OH)₃, TiO₂) / negative (As₂S₃, Au, Ag, starch, clay). Charge arises by preferential adsorption of ions. Helmholtz electrical double layer: fixed layer + diffuse layer; potential difference = zeta potential. 4. Electrophoresis: movement of particles toward an electrode under electric field. Electro-osmosis: medium moves while particles are fixed.

Coagulation

Process of settling of colloidal particles. Methods: electrophoresis, mixing oppositely charged sols, boiling, persistent dialysis, adding electrolyte. Hardy–Schulze rule: the coagulating power of an ion is greater when its charge is higher. For positive sol: PO₄³⁻ > SO₄²⁻ > Cl⁻; for negative sol: Al³⁺ > Ba²⁺ > Na⁺. Flocculation value = minimum millimoles per litre of electrolyte to coagulate a sol in 2 hrs. Gold number = milligrams of protective colloid that just prevents coagulation of 10 mL gold sol on adding 1 mL of 10% NaCl. Lower gold number = better protective power (gelatin 0.005–0.01).

Colloids around us: blue sky, blood (coagulation by FeCl₃), delta formation, smoke precipitation by Cottrell precipitator, medicines (argyrol), rubber plating, tanning.

5.4 Emulsions

Liquid–liquid colloids. O/W (oil in water; milk, vanishing cream) – dilute with water, conducts electricity. W/O (water in oil; butter, cold cream) – dilute with oil, does not conduct. An emulsifying agent (soap, gum, proteins) stabilises them. Demulsification: breaking by heating, freezing, centrifuging, or adding electrolytes. Test: dye test or dilution test.

Questions & Answers (short, long, MCQ)

Q1. Define adsorption. Why does it occur?

Accumulation of a substance on the surface of another. Surface atoms/molecules have unbalanced residual forces, which attract and hold the adsorbate.

Q2. Why is adsorption always exothermic?

Free energy decreases (ΔG < 0) and randomness decreases (ΔS < 0) as the gas gets restricted on the surface. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, so ΔH must be negative.

Q3. Difference between adsorption and absorption?

Adsorption occurs at the surface only (concentration differs from bulk); absorption occurs uniformly through the whole bulk. Example: water on silica gel (adsorption) vs on anhydrous CaCl₂ (absorption).

Q4. Why is chemisorption irreversible while physisorption is reversible?

Chemisorption forms strong chemical bonds; physisorption involves weak van der Waals forces that can be undone by changing T or P.

Q5. Why does a finely divided substance act as a better adsorbent?

Finely divided or porous solids have a larger surface area per unit mass, giving more adsorption sites.

Q6. What is Freundlich equation? Write its log form.

x/m = kP^(1/n); log(x/m) = log k + (1/n) log P, a straight line with slope 1/n.

Q7. What are promoters and poisons? Give examples.

Promoters enhance catalyst activity (Mo for Fe in Haber). Poisons reduce activity (As for Pt in contact process; H₂S for Fe).

Q8. Why does a catalyst not change the equilibrium constant?

It speeds up forward and backward reactions equally and does not change ΔG, so only the time to reach equilibrium changes.

Q9. What is shape-selective catalysis?

Catalysis depending on pore structure of the catalyst and the size/shape of reactant and product molecules, e.g. zeolite ZSM-5 converting alcohols to gasoline.

Q10. State the characteristics of enzyme catalysis.

Highly efficient, specific (lock and key), optimum temperature and pH, activated by co-enzymes and inhibited by poisons.

Q11. Distinguish lyophilic and lyophobic sols.

Lyophilic: solvent-loving, reversible, stable, with strong interaction (gelatin). Lyophobic: solvent-hating, irreversible, need stabiliser, unstable (As₂S₃).

Q12. What is Tyndall effect? Conditions?

Scattering of light by colloidal particles making the path visible. Conditions: particle size not much smaller than wavelength of light and large refractive-index difference between phase and medium.

Q13. State Hardy–Schulze rule.

The greater the valency of the oppositely charged ion of the electrolyte, the greater its coagulating power. Example for As₂S₃ (negative): Al³⁺ > Ba²⁺ > Na⁺.

Q14. What is gold number?

Milligrams of protective colloid that just prevent coagulation of 10 mL of standard gold sol on adding 1 mL of 10% NaCl. Smaller number means better protection.

Q15. MCQ: Which is a positively charged sol? (a) As₂S₃ (b) Fe(OH)₃ (c) Gold (d) Clay

(b) Fe(OH)₃.

Numericals – step by step (Easy → Hard)

Easy: 3 g of gas is adsorbed on 1.5 g of charcoal. Find x/m.

Given: x = 3 g, m = 1.5 g. Formula: x/m = amount adsorbed per gram. Step 1: x/m = 3 ÷ 1.5. Answer: 2 g per g.

Intermediate: Freundlich constants k = 0.5, n = 2. Find x/m at P = 4 atm.

Formula: x/m = kP^(1/n). Step 1: 1/n = 0.5. Step 2: 4^0.5 = 2. Step 3: x/m = 0.5 × 2 = 1.

Hard: x/m = 2 at P = 1 atm and 4 at P = 4 atm. Find n and k.

Step 1: Write both: 2 = k(1)^(1/n) so k = 2. Step 2: 4 = 2 × 4^(1/n) → 4^(1/n) = 2. Step 3: (1/n) log 4 = log 2 → 1/n = 0.5, so n = 2. Answer: k = 2, n = 2.
