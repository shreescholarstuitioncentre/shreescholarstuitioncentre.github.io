10.1 Classification of Halogen Derivatives

Replacing H of a hydrocarbon by a halogen (F, Cl, Br, I) gives a halogen derivative.

(a) On the basis of number of halogen atoms

  • Mono (one X): CH₃Cl
  • Di (two X): CH₂Cl₂ — gem-dihalide (same C), vic-dihalide (adjacent C)
  • Tri / Poly: CHCl₃, CCl₄

(b) On the basis of the carbon bearing X

  • C(sp³)–X: Alkyl halides — primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°) by the number of carbons attached to the C–X carbon (1, 2, 3).
  • Allylic: X on sp³ C next to C=C (CH₂=CH–CH₂–X). Benzylic: X on sp³ C next to a benzene ring (C₆H₅CH₂X).
  • C(sp²)–X: Vinylic (CH₂=CH–X) and aryl halides (C₆H₅–X, halogen directly on ring).
Memory: Allylic/benzylic are very reactive (stable carbocation); vinylic/aryl are very unreactive to nucleophilic substitution (C–X has partial double-bond character due to resonance, and sp² carbon is more electronegative).

10.2 Haloalkanes (Alkyl Halides)

Nomenclature

Common: alkyl + halide (CH₃CH₂Br = ethyl bromide). IUPAC: halo-alkane; longest chain, lowest locant to the substituent (alphabetical order).

StructureIUPAC
CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl1-Chloropropane
(CH₃)₂CHBr2-Bromopropane
(CH₃)₃C–Br2-Bromo-2-methylpropane
CH₂=CH–CH₂Cl3-Chloroprop-1-ene

Nature of C–X bond

X is more electronegative than C, so C carries δ⁺ and X δ⁻. Bond length: C–F < C–Cl < C–Br < C–I. Bond enthalpy: C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I. So reactivity R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F.

Methods of Preparation

  1. From alcohols: R–OH + HX (ZnCl₂, Lucas) → R–X + H₂O; R–OH + SOCl₂ → R–Cl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑ (best method, gaseous by-products); R–OH + PCl₅/PCl₃/P+I₂ also work. Order: 3°>2°>1°; HI>HBr>HCl.
  2. From hydrocarbons: free-radical halogenation of alkanes (mixture, poor); electrophilic addition of HX to alkenes (Markovnikov; peroxide effect gives anti-Markovnikov only with HBr); X₂ to alkenes gives vic-dihalides.
  3. Halide exchange: Finkelstein: R–Cl/Br + NaI (dry acetone) → R–I + NaCl/NaBr↓. Swarts: R–Br + AgF/Hg₂F₂/CoF₂/SbF₃ → R–F.
  4. Allylic halogenation: alkene + NBS → allylic bromide.

Physical Properties

  • Boiling point rises with molecular mass (more surface area, stronger van der Waals); for isomers, branching lowers b.p.
  • b.p. order for same R: RI > RBr > RCl > RF; much higher than hydrocarbons of similar mass (dipole–dipole forces).
  • Insoluble in water (cannot break H-bonds of water) but soluble in organic solvents. Denser than water when containing Br/I or several Cl.

Ambident nucleophiles (CN⁻, NO₂⁻) attack through different atoms depending on the reagent.

SN2 Mechanism (animated)

  • Rate = k[R–X][Nu⁻] (second order). Favoured by 1° > 2° > 3° (steric hindrance), polar aprotic solvents.
  • Optically active halide → inversion of configuration (d → l).

SN1 Mechanism

Step 1 (slow, rate-determining): R–X → R⁺ + X⁻. Step 2 (fast): R⁺ + Nu⁻ → R–Nu.

  • Rate = k[R–X] (first order). Order 3° > 2° > 1° (carbocation stability), polar protic solvents.
  • Planar carbocation attacked from both sides → racemisation (partial inversion + retention).

Stereochemistry terms

Chiral carbon: attached to four different groups. Enantiomers: non-superimposable mirror images, rotate plane-polarised light equally and oppositely. Racemic mixture: 50:50 d/l, optically inactive (external compensation). Retention: bonds to chiral C not broken. Inversion: configuration flips. Racemisation: loss of optical activity.

2. Elimination (Dehydrohalogenation)

Alc. KOH, heat: β-hydrogen removed with X → alkene. Saytzeff rule: the more substituted (more stable) alkene is the major product. E.g. 2-bromobutane → but-2-ene (major) + but-1-ene (minor).

3. Reaction with metals

R–X + Mg (dry ether) → R–MgX (Grignard reagent); carbon is δ⁻, so it reacts with water to give alkane (R–MgX + H₂O → R–H + Mg(OH)X). Wurtz: 2R–X + 2Na (dry ether) → R–R + 2NaX.

10.3 Haloarenes

Preparation

  • Electrophilic substitution: benzene + X₂ / FeCl₃ (or Fe) → chlorobenzene. Iodination needs an oxidising agent (HNO₃) to remove HI.
  • Sandmeyer: ArN₂⁺Cl⁻ + CuCl/HCl → ArCl (CuBr/HBr → ArBr). Gattermann: Cu powder + HX instead. Iodide: ArN₂⁺ + KI → ArI. Fluoride: Balz–Schiemann (ArN₂⁺BF₄⁻ heat).

Properties

Para isomers have higher m.p. than ortho/meta due to better crystal packing (symmetry). Dipole moment of chlorobenzene is lower than CH₃Cl because of resonance and sp² carbon.

Reactions

  1. Nucleophilic substitution (hard): C–X has partial double-bond character; sp² C; ring electrons repel Nu⁻. With –NO₂ at o/p positions it becomes easy (stabilised carbanion, Meisenheimer complex): 2,4,6-trinitrochlorobenzene + H₂O → picric acid readily. Chlorobenzene + NaOH, 623 K, 300 atm → phenol (Dow process).
  2. Electrophilic substitution: halogen is deactivating (–I) but o/p-directing (+R). Nitration → o- and p-nitrochlorobenzene; also sulphonation, Friedel–Crafts.
  3. Fittig: 2ArX + 2Na → Ar–Ar. Wurtz–Fittig: ArX + RX + 2Na → Ar–R.

Solved Numericals (step by step)

Easy: Mass of ethyl bromide from 4.6 g ethanol (100% yield)

Given: C₂H₅OH = 46 g/mol; C₂H₅Br = 109 g/mol. To find: mass of product.
Step 1: moles = 4.6/46 = 0.1. Step 2: 1 mol alcohol gives 1 mol RBr, so 0.1 mol. Step 3: mass = 0.1 × 109 = 10.9 g.

Intermediate: Same reaction, 80% yield

Theoretical = 10.9 g, actual = 10.9 × 80/100 = 8.72 g.

Hard: Carius method — 0.2 g organic compound gives 0.376 g AgBr. Find % Br.

AgBr = 108 + 80 = 188 g/mol. Br in 188 g AgBr = 80 g.
Mass of Br = (80/188) × 0.376 = 0.16 g.
% Br = (0.16/0.2) × 100 = 80%.

Q & A and MCQs

Q1. Why is haloalkane insoluble in water?

Energy released by haloalkane–water attraction is less than energy needed to break water's H-bonds and haloalkane's own forces.

Q2. Why is SOCl₂ the preferred reagent for R–Cl from R–OH?

By-products SO₂ and HCl escape as gases, so the product is pure.

Q3. Why does AgCN give isonitrile while KCN gives nitrile?

KCN is ionic, so C (the better nucleophile) attacks, forming C–C bond. AgCN is covalent; the lone pair on N attacks, forming R–NC.

Q4. Why is vinyl chloride less reactive than ethyl chloride?

Resonance (CH₂=CH–Cl ↔ ⁻CH₂–CH=Cl⁺) gives C–Cl partial double-bond character, and the bond to sp² carbon is shorter and stronger.

Q5. Why is chlorobenzene o/p-directing yet deactivating?

–I effect withdraws electrons (deactivates ring overall); +R effect increases electron density at o/p positions (directs there).

Q6. Why is chloroform stored in dark bottles?

To prevent oxidation by air in light to toxic phosgene.

Q7. Why does SN1 give racemic mixture?

The carbocation is planar sp²; nucleophile attacks from either face with near equal probability, giving d and l forms.

Q8. Write Saytzeff product of 2-bromopentane + alc. KOH.

Pent-2-ene (major, more substituted), pent-1-ene (minor).

MCQ1. Best reagent to convert ethanol into chloroethane: (a) HCl (b) PCl₅ (c) SOCl₂ (d) Cl₂

Answer: (c), gaseous by-products.

MCQ2. Which reacts fastest by SN1? (a) CH₃Br (b) (CH₃)₂CHBr (c) (CH₃)₃CBr (d) CH₃CH₂Br

Answer: (c), forms stable 3° carbocation.

MCQ3. Finkelstein reaction uses: (a) NaI in acetone (b) AgF (c) Mg (d) Na in ether

Answer: (a).

MCQ4. Chlorobenzene + NaOH (623 K, 300 atm) gives: (a) benzene (b) phenol (c) benzyl alcohol (d) aniline

Answer: (b) phenol.
