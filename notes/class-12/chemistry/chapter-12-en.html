12.1 Aldehydes and Ketones

Nomenclature and Structure

Aldehyde R–CHO (suffix -al, C1 is always the CHO carbon); ketone R–CO–R′ (suffix -one, lowest locant to C=O). The carbonyl carbon is sp², trigonal planar (about 120°), with one σ and one π bond to O. Because O is more electronegative, C=O is polar: C(δ⁺), O(δ⁻).

Preparation

Common to both:

  1. Oxidation of alcohols: 1° → aldehyde (PCC, CrO₃), 2° → ketone (K₂Cr₂O₇).
  2. Dehydrogenation (Cu, 573 K): 1° → aldehyde, 2° → ketone.
  3. Hydration of alkynes (Hg²⁺, dil. H₂SO₄): ethyne → ethanal; all other alkynes → ketones (Markovnikov).
  4. Ozonolysis of alkenes (O₃, then Zn/H₂O).

Aldehydes only:

  1. Rosenmund: RCOCl + H₂ (Pd/BaSO₄, poisoned catalyst stops at aldehyde).
  2. Stephen: RCN + SnCl₂ + HCl → imine salt → H₃O⁺ → RCHO. (DIBAL-H also reduces nitriles and esters to aldehydes.)
  3. Etard: toluene + CrO₂Cl₂ (CS₂) → chromium complex → H₃O⁺ → benzaldehyde.
  4. Gattermann–Koch: benzene + CO + HCl (anhyd. AlCl₃/CuCl) → benzaldehyde.

Ketones only: (i) acid chloride + dialkylcadmium (R₂Cd); (ii) nitrile + Grignard reagent, then H₃O⁺; (iii) Friedel–Crafts acylation: benzene + RCOCl / anhyd. AlCl₃ → aryl ketone (acetophenone).

Physical Properties

  • Methanal is a gas, ethanal is volatile (b.p. 294 K), others are liquids/solids.
  • Boiling points are higher than hydrocarbons and ethers of similar mass (dipole–dipole attraction) but lower than alcohols (no intermolecular H-bonding). Order: alcohol > aldehyde/ketone > ether > alkane.
  • Lower members dissolve in water (H-bonding with water); solubility falls with chain length. Aromatic aldehydes have pleasant smells.

Chemical Reactions

A. Nucleophilic addition

Reactivity: HCHO > CH₃CHO > CH₃COCH₃ > PhCOCH₃. Reasons: (1) steric: ketones have two bulky groups; (2) electronic: two +I alkyl groups reduce δ⁺ on C of ketones. Aromatic aldehydes are less reactive than aliphatic since the ring donates by resonance.

B. Reduction

  • To alcohols: NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄, or H₂/Ni, Pd.
  • To alkane: Clemmensen (Zn–Hg, conc. HCl) in acid; Wolff–Kishner (NH₂NH₂, then KOH/ethylene glycol, heat) in base.

C. Oxidation

Aldehydes are easily oxidised to acids, ketones are not (strong oxidants cut C–C on either side of C=O, Popoff’s rule). Distinguishing tests for aldehydes:

  • Tollens’ test [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺: bright silver mirror. RCHO + 2[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + 3OH⁻ → RCOO⁻ + 2Ag↓ + 4NH₃ + 2H₂O.
  • Fehling’s test (alkaline Cu²⁺–tartrate): red-brown Cu₂O precipitate; given by aliphatic aldehydes, not aromatic aldehydes or ketones. Benedict’s solution works similarly.

D. Haloform reaction

Methyl ketones (CH₃CO–) and CH₃CH(OH)–R compounds react with NaOX (X₂ + NaOH) to give carboxylate + CHX₃. With iodine: yellow iodoform precipitate (CHI₃), the iodoform test. Ethanal and ethanol also respond.

E. Reactions due to α-hydrogen

The α-H is acidic because the enolate is stabilised by resonance.

  • Aldol condensation: aldehyde/ketone with α-H + dil. NaOH → β-hydroxy carbonyl (aldol); on heating loses water → α,β-unsaturated carbonyl. 2CH₃CHO → CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO (3-hydroxybutanal) → CH₃CH=CHCHO (but-2-enal). Acetone gives 4-hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one (diacetone alcohol), then mesityl oxide. A mixture of two different carbonyls gives a cross-aldol.
  • Cannizzaro reaction: aldehydes without α-H + conc. NaOH: disproportionation. 2HCHO → CH₃OH + HCOONa; 2C₆H₅CHO → C₆H₅CH₂OH + C₆H₅COONa.

F. Electrophilic substitution (aromatic)

–CHO deactivates the ring and is meta-directing: benzaldehyde + HNO₃/H₂SO₄ → 3-nitrobenzaldehyde.

Uses

Formalin (40% HCHO) preserves biological specimens; HCHO makes bakelite and urea-formaldehyde resins; acetone and ethanal are solvents and raw materials; benzaldehyde in perfumes and dyes.

12.2 Carboxylic Acids

Nomenclature and Structure

R–COOH, suffix -oic acid: HCOOH methanoic (formic), CH₃COOH ethanoic (acetic), C₆H₅COOH benzoic acid (benzenecarboxylic acid), HOOC–COOH ethanedioic (oxalic). The carboxyl carbon is sp², planar; –COOH is a hybrid of carbonyl and hydroxyl groups.

Preparation

  1. Oxidation of 1° alcohols or aldehydes (KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, CrO₃).
  2. Hydrolysis of nitriles, amides, esters, acid chlorides and anhydrides.
  3. Grignard reagent + dry ice (CO₂), then H₃O⁺ → acid with one more carbon.
  4. Vigorous oxidation of alkylbenzenes (KMnO₄/KOH, then H⁺): the entire side chain becomes –COOH whatever its length (provided it has a benzylic H).

Physical Properties

Formic to nonanoic are liquids with sharp smells; higher ones are waxy solids. Carboxylic acids have b.p. higher than alcohols of similar mass because they exist as H-bonded dimers. Soluble in water up to about four carbons.

Acidity

RCOOH ⇌ RCOO⁻ + H⁺. Carboxylate ion is stabilised by resonance (the charge is shared equally by two oxygens; both C–O bonds are identical), so acids are far stronger than alcohols and phenols. Order: carboxylic acid > carbonic acid > phenol > alcohol, so acids liberate CO₂ from NaHCO₃ (test), while phenol does not.

  • Electron-withdrawing groups (–F, –Cl, –NO₂) increase acidity: FCH₂COOH > ClCH₂COOH > BrCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH. More halogens and closer to –COOH raise the strength: Cl₃CCOOH > Cl₂CHCOOH > ClCH₂COOH.
  • Electron-releasing groups (–CH₃) decrease it: HCOOH > CH₃COOH > CH₃CH₂COOH.
  • Benzoic acid (pKa 4.19) is stronger than acetic acid (4.76) because the sp² carbon of the ring is electron-withdrawing.

Reactions

  1. Acid chloride: RCOOH + SOCl₂ (best, gaseous by-products), PCl₅ or PCl₃ → RCOCl.
  2. Anhydride: heat with P₂O₅ or dehydration of two molecules.
  3. Ester: RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ (conc. H₂SO₄, reversible).
  4. Amide: RCOOH + NH₃ → RCOONH₄ → heat → RCONH₂.
  5. Reduction: LiAlH₄ or B₂H₆ → 1° alcohol (NaBH₄ does not reduce acids).
  6. Decarboxylation: sodalime (NaOH + CaO), heat: RCOONa → R–H + Na₂CO₃. Kolbe electrolysis: 2RCOONa → R–R at the anode.
  7. Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ): RCH₂COOH + X₂ / red P, then H₂O → α-halo acid.
  8. Ring substitution: –COOH is deactivating and meta-directing (benzoic acid → 3-nitrobenzoic acid); it does not undergo Friedel–Crafts.

Uses

Methanoic acid: rubber, leather, textile dyeing. Ethanoic acid: vinegar (5–8%), solvent. Benzoic acid: preservative (sodium benzoate). Hexanedioic acid: nylon-6,6.

Solved Numericals (step by step)

Easy: What mass of NaOH neutralises 6 g of acetic acid?

CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O. Molar masses: CH₃COOH 60, NaOH 40.
Step 1: moles acid = 6/60 = 0.1. Step 2: 1:1 ratio so NaOH = 0.1 mol. Step 3: mass = 0.1 × 40 = 4 g.

Intermediate: Silver deposited when 4.4 g ethanal reacts with excess Tollens’ reagent

CH₃CHO (44 g/mol) gives 2 Ag per molecule. Moles = 4.4/44 = 0.1. Ag = 0.2 mol. Mass = 0.2 × 108 = 21.6 g.

Hard: Ka of acetic acid = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵. Find pH and % dissociation of 0.1 M solution.

CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺; Ka = x²/(0.1 − x) ≈ x²/0.1.
x² = 1.8 × 10⁻⁶; x = 1.34 × 10⁻³ M.
pH = −log(1.34 × 10⁻³) = 3 − 0.127 = 2.87.
% dissociation = (1.34 × 10⁻³/0.1) × 100 = 1.34%.

Q & A and MCQs

Q1. Why do aldehydes and ketones have lower boiling points than alcohols?

They cannot form H-bonds with each other (no O–H), only dipole–dipole attractions.

Q2. Why are aldehydes more reactive than ketones in nucleophilic addition?

Steric hindrance is less (one alkyl group, one H) and the carbon carries more positive charge, as only one +I alkyl group reduces it.

Q3. Why does benzaldehyde not give Fehling’s test?

Aromatic aldehydes are less easily oxidised; the carbonyl is conjugated with the ring, so Fehling’s mild oxidant does not oxidise it.

Q4. Why does HCHO give the Cannizzaro reaction?

It has no α-hydrogen, so with conc. NaOH it disproportionates into methanol and sodium formate.

Q5. Why is carboxylic acid more acidic than phenol?

Carboxylate has the charge delocalised over two equivalent electronegative O atoms; phenoxide spreads it onto less electronegative carbons.

Q6. Why is Cl₃CCOOH a stronger acid than CH₃COOH?

Three –I chlorine atoms pull electrons and stabilise the carboxylate ion.

Q7. How do you distinguish ethanal from propanone?

Tollens’ or Fehling’s test: only ethanal responds. (Both give the iodoform test.)

Q8. How do you distinguish benzoic acid from phenol?

Benzoic acid gives effervescence of CO₂ with NaHCO₃; phenol does not (phenol gives violet colour with neutral FeCl₃).

Q9. Why do carboxylic acids not give the usual carbonyl tests (e.g. with 2,4-DNP)?

The lone pair on –OH is delocalised into C=O, reducing the electrophilic character of the carbonyl carbon.

MCQ1. Rosenmund reduction converts: (a) RCN to RCHO (b) RCOCl to RCHO (c) RCOOH to RCH₂OH (d) RCHO to RCH₃

Answer: (b).

MCQ2. Which gives iodoform test? (a) CH₃CH₂CHO (b) C₆H₅CHO (c) CH₃COCH₃ (d) HCHO

Answer: (c), a methyl ketone.

MCQ3. Wolff–Kishner reduction uses: (a) Zn–Hg/HCl (b) NH₂NH₂/KOH (c) LiAlH₄ (d) H₂/Pd

Answer: (b).

MCQ4. Strongest acid: (a) CH₃COOH (b) FCH₂COOH (c) ClCH₂COOH (d) BrCH₂COOH

Answer: (b), fluorine has the strongest –I effect.

MCQ5. Toluene on vigorous oxidation with KMnO₄ gives: (a) benzaldehyde (b) benzyl alcohol (c) benzoic acid (d) phenol

Answer: (c).
