Chapter 23: Photoelectric Effect — Class 12 Physics (UP Board)

Animated figure: ultraviolet light (red dashes) falls on emitter C; photoelectrons (green) fly to collector A and the microammeter records the photocurrent.

1. Photoelectric Effect — Meaning

Emission of electrons from a metal surface when light of suitable frequency falls on it is the photoelectric effect. The emitted electrons are photoelectrons, and the current they form is the photocurrent. Alkali metals (Cs, K, Na) respond even to visible light; Zn, Cu need ultraviolet.

2. Hertz Experiment (1887)

While studying electromagnetic waves, Heinrich Hertz noticed that the spark across the gap of his receiver became stronger when ultraviolet light fell on the metal knobs. This was the first observation of the photoelectric effect. Hallwachs and Righi later showed that a negatively charged zinc plate loses charge under UV light, while a positively charged one does not, proving that negative charges are emitted. J. J. Thomson showed these were electrons.

3. Lenard Experiment (1902)

Set-up: an evacuated quartz tube holds emitter C and collector A, connected through a variable battery (with commutator) and a microammeter. Light falls on C through the quartz window.

Observations

  1. Photocurrent flows instantly (≈10⁻⁹ s) once light falls.
  2. Intensity: saturation current ∝ intensity (number of photoelectrons).
  3. Stopping potential V₀ does not depend on intensity.
  4. V₀ (hence Kmax) increases linearly with frequency.
  5. Below a threshold frequency ν₀ no emission occurs, however intense the light.

Wave theory fails here: it predicts K.E. should depend on intensity, no threshold frequency, and a time delay.

4. Key Terms

Work function φ₀: minimum energy to free an electron from the metal (in eV). Cs: 2.14 eV, Na: 2.75 eV, Zn: 4.3 eV.
Threshold frequency ν₀ = φ₀/h; threshold wavelength λ₀ = hc/φ₀.
Stopping potential V₀: reverse (retarding) potential at which photocurrent becomes zero; eV₀ = Kmax.
1 eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J; hc = 1240 eV·nm.

5. Einstein’s Photoelectric Equation (1905)

Light consists of packets (photons), each of energy E = hν. One photon gives all its energy to one electron.

hν = φ₀ + Kmax
hν = hν₀ + ½mv²max
eV₀ = hν − φ₀ ⇒ V₀ = (h/e)ν − φ₀/e

Explains: no emission for ν < ν₀ (photon energy < φ₀); Kmax independent of intensity; intensity = number of photons per second ⇒ photocurrent ∝ intensity; instantaneous emission (one-photon, one-electron collision). Photon momentum p = h/λ.

6. Planck’s Constant from Graph

The V₀–ν graph is a straight line cutting the frequency axis at ν₀ and with slope = h/e for every metal (parallel lines for different metals). Hence h = slope × e = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J·s (Millikan verified it). Intercept on V₀ axis = −φ₀/e.

Short / Long Questions with Answers

Q1. What is the photoelectric effect?

Emission of electrons from a metal surface when light of sufficient frequency falls on it.

Q2. Who first observed it?

Hertz, in 1887.

Q3. Define work function.

Minimum energy needed to take an electron out of the metal surface.

Q4. Define threshold frequency.

Minimum frequency of light below which no photoemission occurs.

Q5. Define stopping potential.

Minimum negative potential of the collector that stops even the fastest photoelectrons: eV₀ = Kmax.

Q6. Write Einstein’s equation.

hν = φ₀ + ½mv²max.

Q7. Does stopping potential depend on intensity?

No; it depends only on frequency and the metal.

Q8. What happens to saturation current if intensity doubles?

It doubles, since twice as many photons eject twice as many electrons.

Q9. Why did wave theory fail?

It could not explain threshold frequency, intensity-independent K.E., and instantaneous emission.

Q10. Why is the collector kept in vacuum?

So electrons do not collide with air molecules and the metal does not oxidise.

MCQs

1. Photoelectric effect supports: (a) wave theory (b) particle (photon) nature (c) interference (d) diffraction

Answer: (b)

2. Unit of Planck’s constant: (a) J/s (b) J·s (c) J·m (d) N·s

Answer: (b) J·s

3. Slope of V₀–ν graph is: (a) h (b) e (c) h/e (d) φ₀

Answer: (c) h/e

4. If intensity increases, Kmax: (a) increases (b) decreases (c) unchanged (d) zero

Answer: (c)

5. Threshold wavelength λ₀ = (a) φ₀/hc (b) hc/φ₀ (c) h/φ₀ (d) c/φ₀

Answer: (b)

Solved Numericals (Step by Step)

Ex.1 (Easy) Find the threshold wavelength for φ₀ = 2.0 eV.
λ₀ = hc/φ₀ = 1240 eV·nm / 2.0 eV = 620 nm.
Ex.2 (Medium) Light of λ = 300 nm falls on a metal with φ₀ = 2.5 eV. Find Kmax, V₀ and vmax.
Step 1: E = 1240/300 = 4.13 eV.
Step 2: Kmax = 4.13 − 2.5 = 1.63 eV; V₀ = 1.63 V.
Step 3: v = √(2K/m) = √(2×1.63×1.6×10⁻¹⁹/9.1×10⁻³¹) ≈ 7.6×10⁵ m/s.
Ex.3 (Medium) ν₀ = 5×10¹⁴ Hz, ν = 8×10¹⁴ Hz. Find V₀.
K = h(ν−ν₀) = 6.63×10⁻³⁴×3×10¹⁴ = 1.99×10⁻¹⁹ J. V₀ = K/e = 1.99×10⁻¹⁹/1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.24 V.
Ex.4 (Hard) A V₀–ν graph has slope 4.14×10⁻¹⁵ V·s. Find h.
h = slope × e = 4.14×10⁻¹⁵ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 6.62×10⁻³⁴ J·s.
Ex.5 (Hard) Prove that V₀ is linear in ν.
eV₀ = Kmax = hν − φ₀ ⇒ V₀ = (h/e)ν − φ₀/e, of the form y = mx + c with slope h/e. Hence proved.
