Chapter 26: Atom — Bohr’s Theory of Hydrogen Atom (Class 12 Physics, UP Board)

Animated figure: energy-level diagram of hydrogen. Dashed arrows show the downward jumps that make the five spectral series; the green dot is an electron jumping up (absorption) and falling back (emission).

1. Alpha Particle Scattering (Geiger–Marsden, 1909)

Set-up: α-particles from a radioactive source in a lead box pass through a narrow slit and fall on a very thin gold foil (~10⁻⁷ m). Scattered particles are counted on a rotatable ZnS screen with a microscope.

Observations

  1. Most α-particles pass undeviated.
  2. A small number are deflected through small angles.
  3. About 1 in 8000 is deflected by more than 90°; very rarely 180°.

Conclusions

Atom is mostly empty; whole positive charge and almost all mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus (~10⁻¹⁵ m, atom ~10⁻¹⁰ m). Large deflection occurs for small impact parameter b; b = (Ze²/4πε₀K) cot(θ/2). At head-on collision the α stops at the distance of closest approach r₀ = 2Ze²/(4πε₀K), which estimates nuclear size.

2. Rutherford Model (1911)

Electrons revolve around a central positively charged nucleus like planets around the Sun, held by Coulomb force. Drawbacks: (1) A revolving electron is accelerated, so it should radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus — atom would be unstable. (2) Radiation would be continuous, but atoms give discrete line spectra.

3. Bohr Model (1913) — Postulates

  1. Electrons revolve in certain stationary orbits without radiating energy.
  2. Allowed orbits satisfy mvr = nh/2π (n = 1, 2, 3…).
  3. An electron jumping from higher energy E2 to lower E1 emits a photon: hν = E2 − E1; absorption is the reverse.

Derivation

Coulomb force gives centripetal force: mv²/r = e²/(4πε₀r²). With mvr = nh/2π:

rn = n²a₀, a₀ = 0.529 Å (Bohr radius)
vn = 2.19×10⁶/n m/s
En = −13.6/n² eV

K.E. = +13.6/n², P.E. = −27.2/n², total = −13.6/n² (negative = bound). Orbit radius ∝ n², speed ∝ 1/n, energy ∝ 1/n²; for hydrogen-like ions, r ∝ n²/Z, E = −13.6Z²/n² eV.

4. Energy Levels

n = 1 is the ground state (−13.6 eV); n = 2, 3… are excited states (−3.4, −1.51, −0.85 eV). Ionisation energy = 13.6 eV; excitation energy of n = 2 = 10.2 eV. As n increases, levels get closer; at n = ∞, E = 0 (free electron). From level n the number of possible spectral lines = n(n−1)/2.

5. Hydrogen Spectrum and Rydberg Formula

1/λ = R (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), n₂ > n₁; R = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹ (Rydberg constant).

Derivation: hc/λ = En₂ − En₁ = 13.6(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) eV gives the formula with R = me⁴/(8ε₀²h³c).

6. Limitations of Bohr’s Theory

Works only for hydrogen-like (one-electron) atoms; cannot explain fine structure, relative intensities of lines, Zeeman effect or why only certain orbits are allowed (answered later by de Broglie: 2πr = nλ).

Short / Long Questions with Answers

Q1. What did the α-scattering experiment show?

Atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus; most of the atom is empty space.

Q2. Why was a thin gold foil used?

Gold is highly malleable, so a very thin foil causes single scattering.

Q3. Why do most α-particles pass undeviated?

They pass through the vast empty space far from the nucleus.

Q4. Define distance of closest approach.

The distance at which the α-particle’s kinetic energy is completely converted to potential energy: r₀ = 2Ze²/4πε₀K.

Q5. State Bohr’s quantum condition.

mvr = nh/2π.

Q6. Write the energy of the nth level of hydrogen.

En = −13.6/n² eV.

Q7. What does the negative sign of energy mean?

The electron is bound to the nucleus.

Q8. Which series lies in the visible region?

Balmer series.

Q9. What is the ionisation energy of hydrogen?

13.6 eV.

Q10. Why is Rutherford’s model unstable?

An accelerating electron should radiate and spiral into the nucleus.

Solved Numericals (Step by Step)

Ex.1 (Easy) Find the longest wavelength of the Balmer series.
n₁ = 2, n₂ = 3: 1/λ = 1.097×10⁷(1/4 − 1/9) = 1.097×10⁷×5/36 = 1.524×10⁶ m⁻¹ ⇒ λ = 656 nm.
Ex.2 (Easy) Find the shortest wavelength of the Lyman series.
n₂ = ∞: 1/λ = R ⇒ λ = 1/1.097×10⁷ = 91.2 nm.
Ex.3 (Medium) Energy of the photon emitted in n = 3 → 2.
ΔE = 13.6(1/4 − 1/9) = 13.6×5/36 = 1.89 eV.
Ex.4 (Medium) Radius and speed of the electron in n = 3.
r₃ = 9×0.529 = 4.76 Å; v₃ = 2.19×10⁶/3 = 7.3×10⁵ m/s.
Ex.5 (Hard) A 5.5 MeV α-particle approaches a gold nucleus (Z = 79). Find r₀.
r₀ = 2Ze²/(4πε₀K) = 9×10⁹×2×79×(1.6×10⁻¹⁹)²/(5.5×10⁶×1.6×10⁻¹⁹) = 9×10⁹×158×1.6×10⁻¹⁹/5.5×10⁶ ≈ 4.1×10⁻¹⁴ m.
Ex.6 (Hard) Prove En = −13.6/n² eV.
K.E. = ½mv² = e²/(8πε₀r); P.E. = −e²/(4πε₀r); E = −e²/(8πε₀r). With r = n²a₀: En = −e²/(8πε₀a₀n²) = −2.18×10⁻¹⁸/n² J = −13.6/n² eV. Hence proved.
