Class 12 Physics – D.C. Circuits and Measurements (Notes)

1. EMF, Internal Resistance and Terminal Voltage

EMF (E): the work done by the source per unit charge in taking it once round the complete circuit (the energy a cell converts from chemical to electrical per coulomb). Unit volt. It is not a force; it equals the terminal voltage when no current is drawn (open circuit).

Internal resistance (r): the resistance offered by the electrolyte and electrodes of the cell to the flow of current inside the cell.

For external resistance R: I = E / (R + r). Terminal voltage V = potential difference across the cell terminals while current flows:

V = E − I r  (discharging),   V = E + I r  (charging)

Since V = IR: E = V + Ir, so r = (E/V − 1) R = R(E − V)/V. r depends on electrode distance (less if close), plate area (less if large), and electrolyte concentration/temperature.

Maximum power: power to R is P = E²R/(R + r)². It is maximum when R = r, then P_max = E²/4r.

2. Combination of Cells

Series (n identical cells, each E, r)

E_eq = nE, r_eq = nr. I = nE / (R + nr). Best when R ≫ r (then I ≈ nE/R). Cells connected in opposition: E_eq = E₁ − E₂, r_eq = r₁ + r₂.

Parallel (n identical cells)

E_eq = E, r_eq = r/n. I = E / (R + r/n). Best when R ≪ r. Two unequal cells: E_eq = (E₁r₂ + E₂r₁)/(r₁ + r₂), r_eq = r₁r₂/(r₁ + r₂).

Mixed grouping (m rows, n cells in each row)

I = mnE / (mr + nR). Maximum I when mr = nR, i.e. internal resistance of the group equals external R (R = nr/m).

3. Kirchhoff's Laws

First law (junction rule): the algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero, ΣI = 0. Current in = current out. Reason: conservation of charge.

Second law (loop rule): the algebraic sum of potential changes around any closed loop is zero, ΣE + ΣIR = 0. Reason: conservation of energy.

Sign conventions

  • Moving through a resistor along the current: −IR (potential drops); against the current: +IR.
  • Moving through a cell from − to + terminal: +E; from + to −: −E.
  • Assume current directions; a negative answer means the actual direction is opposite.

Method: mark currents with junction law, write a loop equation for each independent loop, solve simultaneous equations.

4. Wheatstone Bridge

Four resistances P, Q, R, S form a quadrilateral; a battery connects A–C and a galvanometer connects B–D.

Balance condition (no current through G): V_B = V_D. Then the current through P and Q is the same (I₁) and through R and S the same (I₂). From potentials: I₁P = I₂R and I₁Q = I₂S. Dividing:

P / Q = R / S

Use: finding an unknown S = RQ/P. Bridge is most sensitive when all four resistances are of the same order; press the battery key first and then the galvanometer key (to avoid self-induction effects).

5. Meter Bridge (Slide Wire Bridge)

A practical Wheatstone bridge: a 1 m uniform wire (manganin/constantan) of resistance per cm constant, two gaps with R (resistance box) and X (unknown), a galvanometer and a jockey.

At balance the jockey is at l cm from the left end. The wire lengths are proportional to resistances: P/Q = l/(100 − l). So

X = R (100 − l) / l

Precautions: take R such that the balance point is near the middle (best accuracy, 40–60 cm); interchange R and X and take the mean; do not slide the jockey (tap it); use thick copper strips to reduce end errors; avoid heating the wire by pressing the key briefly. Resistivity ρ = X π r²/L.

6. Potentiometer

Principle

When a constant current flows through a uniform wire, the potential difference across any part is proportional to its length: V ∝ l, V = k l.

Potential gradient k = potential drop per unit length (V/m) = I ρ/A = (E/(R_total))·(R_wire/L), where L = wire length.

Comparison of EMFs of two cells

Balance lengths l₁ and l₂ for E₁ and E₂ (using the two-way key): E₁ = k l₁ and E₂ = k l₂.

E₁ / E₂ = l₁ / l₂

Internal resistance of a cell

Balance length l₁ with open circuit (E = k l₁). Then connect resistance R across the cell and find l₂ (V = k l₂). Since E/V = (R + r)/R = l₁/l₂:

r = R (l₁ − l₂) / l₂

Sensitivity

A potentiometer is more sensitive when the potential gradient is smaller, because a given change in E then shifts the null point by a greater length. Reduce k by (i) increasing wire length L, (ii) decreasing current with a rheostat. Accuracy also increases with the wire's length.

Why better than a voltmeter?

At the null point no current flows from the cell, so it measures the true EMF (no internal drop); a voltmeter draws current and reads V < E. Conditions: driving cell EMF must exceed the EMF measured; the wire must be uniform, long, with high resistivity and low temperature coefficient.

Questions & Answers (Theory and MCQs)

Q1. Define EMF and terminal voltage. Why is V < E? (3)

EMF is the work per unit charge done by the source round the circuit; V is the potential difference across the terminals during current flow. V = E − Ir, so V < E due to the drop across internal resistance.

Q2. Derive the expression for internal resistance of a cell. (3)

I = E/(R + r), V = IR. So E = V + Ir, I = V/R: r = (E − V)R/V.

Q3. State and explain Kirchhoff's laws. (3)

Junction law ΣI = 0 (charge conservation). Loop law ΣE = ΣIR round a loop (energy conservation).

Q4. Derive the balance condition of a Wheatstone bridge. (5)

At balance V_B = V_D so I₁P = I₂R, I₁Q = I₂S. Divide: P/Q = R/S.

Q5. Explain the meter bridge and derive X. (5)

P/Q = R/X = l/(100 − l), hence X = R(100 − l)/l. Wire resistance is proportional to its length.

Q6. State the principle of the potentiometer. (2)

For a uniform wire carrying constant current, potential difference across a length is proportional to that length.

Q7. How is E₁/E₂ determined by a potentiometer? (3)

Find null lengths l₁, l₂ for each cell with the same k: E₁/E₂ = l₁/l₂.

Q8. How is internal resistance found using a potentiometer? Derive. (5)

Null lengths l₁ (open) and l₂ (with R). E/V = l₁/l₂ = (R + r)/R so r = R(l₁ − l₂)/l₂.

Q9. How can the sensitivity of a potentiometer be increased? (2)

Lower the potential gradient: use a longer wire and a rheostat to reduce current.

Q10. Why is a potentiometer preferred over a voltmeter to measure EMF? (2)

At null it draws no current from the cell, so it reads the true EMF; a voltmeter draws current and reads terminal voltage.

Solved Numericals (Easy → Hard)

Easy 1. A cell E = 2 V, r = 0.5 Ω drives R = 4.5 Ω. Find I and V.
I = E/(R + r) = 2/5 = 0.4 A. V = IR = 0.4 × 4.5 = 1.8 V (check: E − Ir = 2 − 0.2 = 1.8 V).
Easy 2. Wheatstone bridge P = 10 Ω, Q = 20 Ω, R = 30 Ω is balanced. Find S.
P/Q = R/S → S = RQ/P = 30 × 20/10 = 60 Ω.
Medium 1. E = 1.5 V (open), V = 1.2 V with R = 4 Ω. Find r.
r = (E/V − 1)R = (1.5/1.2 − 1) × 4 = 0.25 × 4 = 1 Ω.
Medium 2. Meter bridge balances at 40 cm with R = 12 Ω. Find X.
X = R(100 − l)/l = 12 × 60/40 = 18 Ω.
Medium 3. Six cells, each 1.5 V and 0.5 Ω, connected to R = 3 Ω: (i) series (ii) parallel. Find I.
(i) E = 9 V, r = 3 Ω: I = 9/(3 + 3) = 1.5 A. (ii) E = 1.5 V, r = 0.5/6 = 0.083 Ω: I = 1.5/3.083 = 0.49 A. Series is better since R is not small compared to r.
Hard 1. Branch 1: 6 V with 2 Ω; branch 2: 3 V with 1 Ω; both feed a 3 Ω resistor (current I₁ + I₂ through it). Find I₁, I₂, I₃.
Loop 1: 6 − 2I₁ − 3(I₁ + I₂) = 0 → 5I₁ + 3I₂ = 6. Loop 2: 3 − I₂ − 3(I₁ + I₂) = 0 → 3I₁ + 4I₂ = 3. Eliminate I₂: 20I₁ + 12I₂ = 24 and 9I₁ + 12I₂ = 9; subtract: 11I₁ = 15, I₁ = 15/11 = 1.36 A. I₂ = (3 − 3I₁)/4 = −3/11 = −0.27 A (negative: the 3 V cell is being charged). I₃ = I₁ + I₂ = 12/11 = 1.09 A.
Hard 2. Potentiometer wire 10 m, resistance 10 Ω, driven by a 2 V cell with 10 Ω in series. A cell balances at 6 m. Find its EMF.
I = 2/(10 + 10) = 0.1 A. V across wire = 0.1 × 10 = 1 V. k = 1/10 = 0.1 V/m. E = k l = 0.1 × 6 = 0.6 V.
Hard 3. Null lengths 3.5 m (E₁) and 2.1 m (E₂). Find E₁/E₂.
E₁/E₂ = l₁/l₂ = 3.5/2.1 = 5/3 ≈ 1.67.
Hard 4. Null length 2.5 m open, 2.0 m with R = 10 Ω. Find r.
r = R(l₁ − l₂)/l₂ = 10 × 0.5/2.0 = 2.5 Ω.
Hard 5. E = 12 V, r = 3 Ω. For what R is the power maximum? Find it.
R = r = 3 Ω. I = 12/6 = 2 A. P = I²R = 4 × 3 = 12 W (= E²/4r = 144/12).
