Physics Class 12 – Optics – Chapter 15: Refraction of Light at Plane Interface

Key formulas: sin i / sin r = n₂/n₁  |  n = c/v  |  sin C = 1/n  |  apparent depth = real depth / n

1. Refraction of Light

When a ray passes obliquely from one transparent medium into another, it changes direction at the boundary. This is refraction, and it happens because light travels at different speeds in the two media. From rarer to denser the ray bends towards the normal; from denser to rarer it bends away from the normal. A ray along the normal (i = 0) goes straight through.

2. Refractive Index

Absolute refractive index n = speed of light in vacuum / speed in the medium = c/v. It has no unit and is always greater than 1. Frequency stays constant; speed and wavelength change (λ′ = λ/n). Relative refractive index ₁n₂ = n₂/n₁ = v₁/v₂. Examples: water 4/3, glass 3/2, diamond 2.42.

3. Laws of Refraction (Snell’s Law)

(1) The incident ray, refracted ray and normal at the point of incidence lie in one plane. (2) For two given media and a given colour, sin i / sin r = constant = n₂/n₁, i.e. n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r.

5. Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

For a ray going from a denser to a rarer medium, increasing the angle of incidence increases the angle of refraction. At one particular angle of incidence the refraction angle becomes 90°; this is the critical angle (C). Beyond it the ray is totally reflected back into the denser medium.

Conditions: (1) the ray travels from denser to rarer medium; (2) angle of incidence i > C.
Formula: n₁ sin C = n₂ sin 90° ⇒ sin C = 1/n (rarer medium = air). Higher n ⇒ smaller C. Water C ≈ 48.6°, glass ≈ 41.8°, diamond ≈ 24.4°.

7. Examples of TIR

Sparkle of diamond: small C gives many internal reflections. Mirage: air near a hot surface is rarer; rays from above undergo TIR and appear to come from water. Air bubble in water shines silvery. Totally reflecting prisms (turn light by 90° or 180°) and optical fibres.

8. Optical Fibre

A very thin strand of glass or plastic with a high-index core surrounded by a low-index cladding. A ray entering the core strikes the core–cladding boundary at i > C and undergoes repeated TIR until it leaves at the other end, with almost no loss. Uses: communication (internet, telephone), endoscopy, decorative lamps. Advantages: low loss, high speed, free from electromagnetic interference.

9. Real and Apparent Depth

Derivation: Object O lies at real depth OP = d. A ray leaving water bends away from the normal, so the observer sees the object raised at I. For small angles n = sin r / sin i ≈ tan r / tan i = (x/IP)/(x/OP) = OP/IP. So n = real depth / apparent depth, i.e. apparent depth = d/n. Normal shift = d(1 − 1/n).

10. Solved Numericals

Q1 (Easy): A ray in air strikes glass (n = 1.5) at 60°. Find the angle of refraction.
sin r = sin 60°/1.5 = 0.866/1.5 = 0.577 ⇒ r ≈ 35.3°.
Q2 (Easy): Find the critical angle for water (n = 4/3).
sin C = 1/n = 3/4 = 0.75 ⇒ C ≈ 48.6°.
Q3 (Medium): A pond is 2.4 m deep, n = 4/3. Find the apparent depth and the rise of the bottom.
Apparent depth = 2.4/(4/3) = 1.8 m. Rise = 2.4 − 1.8 = 0.6 m.
Q4 (Medium): Light travels at 2×10⁸ m/s in a medium. Find n and C.
n = c/v = 3×10⁸/2×10⁸ = 1.5. sin C = 1/1.5 = 0.667 ⇒ C ≈ 41.8°.
Q5 (Hard): A vessel holds liquid 20 cm deep. A coin at the bottom appears raised by 5 cm. Find n.
Apparent depth = 20 − 5 = 15 cm. n = 20/15 = 4/3 ≈ 1.33.

11. Important Questions & Answers

Why does a pencil partly dipped in water look bent?
Rays from the submerged part bend on leaving water, so that part appears raised.
On what does the critical angle depend?
On the refractive indices of both media and on the colour (wavelength) of light: larger C for red, smaller for violet.
MCQ: A medium of higher refractive index has a critical angle that is? (a) larger (b) smaller (c) same (d) zero
Answer: (b) smaller, since sin C = 1/n.
MCQ: An optical fibre works on? (a) refraction (b) diffraction (c) total internal reflection (d) interference
Answer: (c).
