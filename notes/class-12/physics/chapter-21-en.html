1. Diffraction — Meaning

Bending of light around the edges of an obstacle or slit, and its spreading into the geometrical shadow, is called diffraction. It is clearly seen when slit width a is comparable to wavelength λ. It proves the wave nature of light.

2. Fraunhofer Diffraction

Source and screen are at infinite distance (practically, convex lenses are used), so incident and diffracted wavefronts are plane. Condition: a²/(λD) ≪ 1.

In Fresnel diffraction the source/screen are at finite distance and wavefronts are spherical or cylindrical.

3. Single Slit Diffraction — Pattern

A parallel beam falls on a narrow slit of width a. On the screen we get a bright, wide central maximum with weaker, narrower secondary maxima on both sides, separated by dark minima.

Condition of minima

Divide the slit into two halves. For direction θ the path difference between corresponding points of the halves is (a/2)sinθ. If it equals λ/2, they cancel pairwise, so:

a sinθ = nλ (n = ±1, ±2, ±3…) → dark
a sinθ = (n+½)λ → secondary maxima (approx.)
Centre (θ = 0): all waves in phase → principal maximum.

Intensity

I = I₀ (sinβ/β)², β = (πa sinθ)/λ. Secondary maxima have about 4.5%, 1.6%, 0.8% of I₀ — intensity falls rapidly.

4. Central Maximum and Angular Width

Central maximum lies between the first minima on both sides (n = ±1). For small θ, sinθ ≈ θ = λ/a.

Angular width = 2θ = 2λ/a
Linear width β₀ = 2λD/a (D = slit–screen distance)

Results: narrower slit (a↓) → wider pattern; larger λ (red) → wider pattern; width of each secondary maximum = λD/a, i.e. half of the central one. Central maximum is brightest and twice as wide as the others.

5. Difference: Interference vs Diffraction

Interference: superposition of waves from two coherent sources; fringes of equal width and nearly equal intensity. Diffraction: superposition of secondary wavelets from one wavefront; unequal width and rapidly falling intensity.

Short / Long Questions with Answers

Q1. What is diffraction?

Bending of light around obstacles/slit edges is diffraction.

Q2. Define Fraunhofer diffraction.

Diffraction in which source and screen are effectively at infinity and wavefronts are plane.

Q3. Condition for diffraction to be noticeable?

Size of obstacle/slit must be comparable to the wavelength.

Q4. Why is diffraction of sound easier than of light?

Sound wavelength (~metres) is comparable to everyday obstacles; light's (~10⁻⁷ m) is not.

Q5. Write the condition of minima in a single slit.

a sinθ = nλ, n = 1, 2, 3…

Q6. Derive the angular width of the central maximum.

First minimum: a sinθ₁ = λ ⇒ θ₁ ≈ λ/a. Central maximum extends from −θ₁ to +θ₁, so angular width = 2λ/a.

Q7. What happens if the slit width is doubled?

Width becomes half; the pattern shrinks and becomes brighter.

Q8. What happens in white light?

Central maximum is white; sides are coloured, red being farthest (largest λ).

Q9. What happens if the whole apparatus is immersed in water?

λ becomes λ/μ, so the width decreases by the factor μ.

Q10. Why are secondary maxima weaker?

Fewer slit portions contribute in phase as n increases, so intensity falls.

MCQs

1. Central maximum angular width is: (a) λ/a (b) 2λ/a (c) λ/2a (d) 2a/λ

Answer: (b) 2λ/a

2. If slit width increases, central maximum: (a) widens (b) narrows (c) unchanged (d) vanishes

Answer: (b) narrows

3. Diffraction of light proves: (a) particle nature (b) wave nature (c) polarization (d) none

Answer: (b)

4. Fraunhofer diffraction uses: (a) spherical wavefronts (b) plane wavefronts (c) no lens (d) none

Answer: (b)

5. Minima occur when a sinθ = (a) nλ (b) (n+½)λ (c) λ/2 only (d) 0

Answer: (a) nλ

Solved Numericals (Step by Step)

Ex.1 (Easy) To find: linear width of central maximum. a = 0.2 mm, λ = 600 nm, D = 2 m.
Step 1: β₀ = 2λD/a.
Step 2: = 2×600×10⁻⁹×2 / (0.2×10⁻³).
Step 3: = 2.4×10⁻⁶/2×10⁻⁴ = 1.2×10⁻² m = 1.2 cm.
Ex.2 (Medium) First minimum is at 30° for λ = 500 nm. Find a.
a sinθ = λ ⇒ a = 500×10⁻⁹/sin30° = 500×10⁻⁹/0.5 = 1 μm.
Ex.3 (Hard) Prove that on doubling slit width, linear width of the central maximum becomes half.
β₀ = 2λD/a. For a′ = 2a: β₀′ = 2λD/2a = β₀/2. Hence proved; intensity increases as more light passes.
