Chapter 29: Nuclear Energy — Class 12 Physics (UP Board)

Animated figure: a slow neutron hits U-235, which splits into Ba-141 and Kr-92 releasing 3 neutrons and about 200 MeV; each neutron can split another nucleus — a chain reaction.

1. Nuclear Fission

Splitting of a heavy nucleus into two medium nuclei of comparable mass with release of energy. Discovered by Hahn and Strassmann (1938); explained by Meitner and Frisch.

n + 235U → 236U* → 141Ba + 92Kr + 3n + 200 MeV

Reason: B.E. per nucleon of U (≈7.6 MeV) is less than that of the fragments (≈8.5 MeV); the gain is ≈ 236×0.9 ≈ 200 MeV per fission. About 85% appears as kinetic energy of fragments (finally heat). Complete fission of 1 kg U-235 gives ≈ 8.2×10¹³ J, equal to about 2.7×10⁶ kg of coal.

2. Chain Reaction

Neutrons released in a fission cause more fissions in a self-sustaining series. Multiplication factor k = neutrons in a generation / neutrons in the previous generation.

  • k < 1 subcritical (dies out); k = 1 critical (steady, controlled – reactor); k > 1 supercritical (explosive, uncontrolled – atom bomb).
  • Critical mass: minimum mass for k ≥ 1; smaller pieces lose too many neutrons by leakage.

3. Nuclear Reactor

A device for a controlled chain reaction.

  • Fuel: enriched U-235 (~3%) or Pu-239.
  • Moderator: heavy water, graphite or water slows neutrons to thermal energy (~0.025 eV) so that U-235 captures them.
  • Control rods: cadmium or boron absorb neutrons; pushed in to reduce, pulled out to raise power.
  • Coolant: water, liquid sodium or CO₂ carries heat to a steam generator.
  • Shield: thick concrete walls stop radiation.

Breeder reactor converts non-fissile U-238 into fissile Pu-239. India’s first power station: Tarapur (1969).

4. Nuclear Fusion

Joining of two light nuclei into a heavier one with release of energy.

2H + 3H → 4He + n + 17.6 MeV
2H + 2H → 3He + n + 3.27 MeV
Proton–proton cycle in the Sun: 4 1H → 4He + 2e⁺ + 2ν + 26.7 MeV

Nuclei repel electrostatically, so very high temperature (10⁷–10⁸ K) is needed; hence thermonuclear reaction. Energy per nucleon: fusion ≈ 3.5 MeV (D–T), fission ≈ 0.9 MeV. The Sun and stars are powered by fusion (Hans Bethe).

5. Hydrogen Bomb and Thermonuclear Energy

A hydrogen bomb is an uncontrolled fusion device using deuterium/tritium (or lithium deuteride). A fission (atom) bomb acts as the trigger to give the enormous temperature needed. It is far more powerful than an atom bomb and its fuel is abundant. Thermonuclear energy is energy from fusion at high temperature; controlled fusion (tokamak, ITER) is being developed. Advantages: abundant fuel (heavy water in oceans), little radioactive waste.

6. Fission vs Fusion

Fission: heavy nucleus splits, started by a neutron at ordinary temperature, radioactive waste, controllable. Fusion: light nuclei join, needs ~10⁸ K, little waste, not yet controllable commercially, more energy per unit mass.

Short / Long Questions with Answers

Q1. What is nuclear fission?

Splitting of a heavy nucleus into two medium nuclei with release of energy.

Q2. How much energy is released per fission of U-235?

About 200 MeV.

Q3. What is a chain reaction?

Self-sustaining series of fissions caused by neutrons produced in earlier fissions.

Q4. Define critical mass.

Minimum mass of fissile material needed to sustain a chain reaction.

Q5. What is the function of a moderator?

It slows fast neutrons to thermal energies.

Q6. What is the role of control rods?

They absorb neutrons to control the rate of the chain reaction.

Q7. Why is high temperature needed for fusion?

To give nuclei enough kinetic energy to overcome Coulomb repulsion.

Q8. Name the source of the Sun’s energy.

Nuclear fusion (proton–proton cycle).

Q9. How does a hydrogen bomb work?

Uncontrolled fusion of hydrogen isotopes, triggered by a fission bomb.

Q10. Which is more energy per unit mass: fission or fusion?

Fusion.

Solved Numericals (Step by Step)

Ex.1 (Easy) Energy from complete fission of 1 g U-235 (200 MeV per fission).
N = 6.02×10²³/235 = 2.56×10²¹ nuclei.
E = 2.56×10²¹×200 MeV = 5.12×10²³ MeV = 5.12×10²³×1.6×10⁻¹³ J = 8.2×10¹⁰ J.
Ex.2 (Easy) Neutrons after 5 generations starting from 1000 with k = 1.1.
N = 1000×(1.1)⁵ = 1000×1.61 = 1610.
Ex.3 (Medium) Find Q for D + T → He + n (m: 2.014102, 3.016049, 4.002603, 1.008665 u).
Reactants = 5.030151 u; products = 5.011268 u; Δm = 0.018883 u; Q = 0.018883×931.5 = 17.6 MeV.
Ex.4 (Medium) Compare energy per nucleon: fission (200 MeV, A = 236) and D–T fusion (17.6 MeV, A = 5).
Fission: 200/236 = 0.85 MeV; fusion: 17.6/5 = 3.52 MeV. Ratio ≈ 4, so fusion gives about 4 times more energy per nucleon.
Ex.5 (Hard) A 100 MW reactor works on U-235 (200 MeV per fission). Find the mass fissioned per day.
Fissions/s = 10⁸/(200×1.6×10⁻¹³) = 3.125×10¹⁸. Per day = 3.125×10¹⁸×86400 = 2.7×10²³.
Moles = 2.7×10²³/6.02×10²³ = 0.449; mass = 0.449×235 = ≈ 105 g.
Ex.6 (Hard) Show from the B.E. curve that fission releases ≈ 200 MeV.
U-236: B.E./A ≈ 7.6 MeV; fragments (A ≈ 118): ≈ 8.5 MeV. Gain = 236×(8.5 − 7.6) = 236×0.9 ≈ 212 MeV ≈ 200 MeV. Hence shown.
