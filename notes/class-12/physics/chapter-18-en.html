1. Simple Microscope

A convex lens of small focal length used to see a tiny object. The object is placed within the focal length (u < f), giving an erect, magnified, virtual image. Also called a magnifying glass.
Magnifying power M = (angle subtended by image at eye) / (angle subtended by object at the near point D) = tanβ/tanα.
Image at near point D: M = 1 + D/f
Image at infinity (relaxed eye): M = D/f

D = 25 cm (least distance of distinct vision). A smaller f gives greater M. The eye is least strained for the image at infinity.

2. Compound Microscope

Construction: two convex lenses on a common axis. The objective (small f₀, small aperture) is near the object; the eyepiece (larger fe) is near the eye.
Working: 1) The object is placed just beyond f₀ of the objective. 2) The objective forms a real, inverted, magnified intermediate image. 3) This image lies within the focal length of the eyepiece, which acts as a simple microscope. 4) The final image is virtual, inverted and highly magnified.
M = m₀ × mₑ = (v₀/|u₀|)(1 + D/fₑ)   (final image at D)
M = (v₀/|u₀|)(D/fₑ) ≈ (L/f₀)(D/fₑ)   (final image at ∞, L = tube length)

For large M, f₀ and fₑ should both be small, and L large. The objective has a small aperture, and the eyepiece has a larger one.

3. Astronomical Telescope (Refracting)

It is used to view distant heavenly bodies. The objective has large f₀ and large aperture; the eyepiece has small fₑ. The objective forms a real, inverted image of the distant object at its focus, and the eyepiece magnifies it.
Normal adjustment (image at ∞): M = −f₀/fₑ, L = f₀ + fₑ
Image at D: M = −(f₀/fₑ)(1 + fₑ/D), L = f₀ + uₑ

The negative sign shows the final image is inverted. For a terrestrial telescope an erecting lens is added.

4. Reflecting Telescope

The objective is a large concave parabolic mirror. Newtonian: a small plane mirror at 45° sends the image to the side eyepiece. Cassegrain: a convex secondary mirror reflects light through a hole in the primary.
Advantages over refractors: 1) no chromatic aberration; 2) the parabolic mirror removes spherical aberration; 3) large mirrors are easier to build and support (supported at the back); 4) a brighter image and greater resolving power from the large aperture.

5. Resolving Power

Resolving power is the ability of an instrument to show two close objects as separate. The diffraction limit sets it.
Microscope: smallest separation d = 1.22λ/(2μ sinθ), and RP = 1/d = 2μ sinθ/(1.22λ). (μ sinθ = numerical aperture.) Remedy: use shorter λ, or immerse the objective in oil of higher μ.
Telescope: smallest angle dθ = 1.22λ/D, and RP = D/(1.22λ). A larger objective aperture D gives higher RP.

Solved Numericals (step by step)

Ex.1 A simple microscope has f = 5 cm. Find M at the near point and at infinity.
M(D) = 1 + D/f = 1 + 25/5 = 6. M(∞) = D/f = 5.
Ex.2 Compound microscope: f₀ = 2 cm, fₑ = 5 cm, object at 2.5 cm from the objective, final image at D. Find M.
Objective: u = −2.5, f = 2 ⇒ 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/2 − 1/2.5 = 0.1 ⇒ v₀ = 10 cm. m₀ = 10/2.5 = 4. mₑ = 1 + 25/5 = 6. M = 4 × 6 = 24.
Ex.3 Telescope: f₀ = 100 cm, fₑ = 5 cm. Find M and length in normal adjustment.
M = f₀/fₑ = 20; L = 100 + 5 = 105 cm.
Ex.4 Same telescope, final image at D = 25 cm.
M = (100/5)(1 + 5/25) = 20 × 1.2 = 24. For the eyepiece, 1/u = 1/v − 1/f = −1/25 − 1/5 = −6/25 ⇒ u = −4.17 cm. L = 100 + 4.17 = 104.17 cm.
Ex.5 Find the limit of resolution of a telescope of aperture 10 cm for λ = 5500 Å.
dθ = 1.22λ/D = 1.22 × 5.5×10⁻⁷ / 0.1 = 6.7×10⁻⁶ rad.
Ex.6 Microscope: λ = 5000 Å, μ sinθ = 1.0. Find d.
d = 1.22λ/(2μ sinθ) = 1.22 × 5×10⁻⁷ / 2 = 3.05×10⁻⁷ m.
Proof M = 1 + D/f. The lens forms an image at D: v = −D. With 1/v − 1/u = 1/f, we get 1/u = −1/D − 1/f. Then M = v/u = −D(−1/D − 1/f) = 1 + D/f. ∎

Q & A

Q1. What is the working principle of a compound microscope?
A real intermediate image from the objective is magnified further by the eyepiece, which acts as a simple microscope.
Q2. Why must the objective of a telescope have a large aperture?
It gathers more light for a brighter image, and RP = D/1.22λ increases.
Q3. Why is a reflecting telescope preferred?
It has no chromatic aberration, a parabolic mirror removes spherical aberration, and it is lighter and easier to support at large sizes.
Q4. How can the resolving power of a microscope be increased?
Use a smaller wavelength, or use an oil-immersion objective with higher μ.
Q5. Why is the final image in a telescope inverted?
The objective forms an inverted real image, and the eyepiece does not invert it again.
Q6. MCQ: M of a telescope in normal adjustment = (a) f₀fₑ (b) f₀/fₑ (c) fₑ/f₀ (d) f₀+fₑ → (b).
Q7. MCQ: Resolving power of a telescope ∝ (a) λ (b) 1/D (c) D (d) 1/μ → (c) D.
Q8. In which direction do you look through a microscope for a relaxed eye?
The final image is set at infinity, giving M = D/fₑ for the eyepiece.
Q9. Why is the Cassegrain design compact?
The secondary mirror folds the light path back through the primary.
Q10. Define numerical aperture.
N.A. = μ sinθ, where θ is half the angle of the cone of light from the object.
