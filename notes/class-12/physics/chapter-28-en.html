1. Radioactive Decay

Becquerel (1896) discovered that uranium salts emit penetrating rays spontaneously; Marie and Pierre Curie found polonium and radium. Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of α, β or γ radiation from unstable nuclei. It is a nuclear process, independent of temperature, pressure or chemical state, and random for each nucleus.

2. Radioactive Decay Law

The rate of decay is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present:

dN/dt = −λN ⇒ N = N₀e−λt
λ = decay constant (s⁻¹). Activity R = |dN/dt| = λN = R₀e−λt

Unit: becquerel (1 Bq = 1 decay/s); 1 curie = 3.7×10¹⁰ Bq; 1 rutherford = 10⁶ Bq.

3. Half Life

Time in which half of the nuclei decay. Put N = N₀/2: N₀/2 = N₀e−λT ⇒

T½ = ln2/λ = 0.693/λ
N = N₀(1/2)t/T
After n half-lives N = N₀/2ⁿ. Half-life is a constant for a nuclide (ranges from 10⁻⁷ s to 10¹⁰ years).

4. Mean Life

Average lifetime of the nuclei: τ = 1/λ = T½/0.693 = 1.44 T½. At t = τ, N = N₀/e ≈ 37% of N₀.

5. Alpha Decay

Emission of an α-particle (⁴₂He nucleus).

AZX → A−4Z−2Y + 42He
Example: 238U → 234Th + 4He
Properties: energy 4–9 MeV, discrete line spectrum; strongly ionising, least penetrating (stopped by paper); deflected by E and B fields. Occurs in heavy nuclei (Z > 82).

6. Beta Decay

β⁻: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄, so AZX → AZ+1Y + e⁻ + ν̄.
β⁺: p → n + e⁺ + ν, so AZX → AZ−1Y + e⁺ + ν.

The electron comes from the nucleus, not from orbits. The β-energy spectrum is continuous (0 to Emax), which led Pauli to propose the neutrino (Fermi: beta theory). Moderately ionising, penetrates a few mm of aluminium.

7. Gamma Decay

A nucleus left in an excited state after α or β decay drops to a lower level, emitting a γ-photon (energy keV–MeV). Z and A do not change. γ-rays are electromagnetic waves, uncharged, undeflected by fields, weakly ionising, highly penetrating (need thick lead).

8. Soddy–Fajans Displacement Laws

α-emission: Z decreases by 2, A by 4. β⁻-emission: Z increases by 1, A same. γ-emission: no change.

9. Nuclear Reactions

A process in which a nucleus is changed by bombardment: a + X → Y + b (Rutherford, 1919: ¹⁴N + ⁴He → ¹⁷O + ¹H; Chadwick, 1932: ⁹Be + ⁴He → ¹²C + n). Conserved: charge, nucleon number, energy and momentum.

Q-value = (mreactants − mproducts) × 931.5 MeV
Q > 0 exothermic; Q < 0 endothermic.

Short / Long Questions with Answers

Q1. Who discovered radioactivity?

Henri Becquerel, in 1896.

Q2. State the radioactive decay law.

Rate of decay is directly proportional to the number of nuclei present: dN/dt = −λN.

Q3. Define half-life.

Time in which half of the radioactive nuclei decay; T½ = 0.693/λ.

Q4. Define mean life.

Average life of a nucleus; τ = 1/λ = 1.44 T½.

Q5. Why is beta spectrum continuous?

Energy is shared between the electron and the antineutrino in varying ratios.

Q6. Which radiation is most penetrating?

Gamma rays.

Q7. What happens to Z and A in α-decay?

Z decreases by 2 and A by 4.

Q8. Define the unit curie.

1 curie = 3.7×10¹⁰ decays per second.

Q9. Does temperature affect radioactivity?

No, it is a nuclear process.

Q10. What is the Q-value of a nuclear reaction?

Energy released (or absorbed) in the reaction, equal to the mass defect times 931.5 MeV.

Solved Numericals (Step by Step)

Ex.1 (Easy) T½ = 20 min. What fraction remains after 1 hour?
n = 60/20 = 3; N/N₀ = (1/2)³ = 1/8.
Ex.2 (Easy) Mean life is 100 s. Find half-life.
T½ = 0.693τ = 0.693×100 = 69.3 s.
Ex.3 (Medium) 64 g of a sample has T½ = 3 days. How much is left after 15 days?
n = 15/3 = 5; m = 64/2⁵ = 64/32 = 2 g.
Ex.4 (Medium) Find the decay constant of radium (T½ = 1600 years).
λ = 0.693/1600 = 4.33×10⁻⁴ per year.
Ex.5 (Hard) Activity of 1 g of Ra-226 (T½ = 1600 y, Avogadro NA = 6.02×10²³).
N = 6.02×10²³/226 = 2.66×10²¹.
λ = 0.693/(1600×3.156×10⁷ s) = 1.37×10⁻¹¹ s⁻¹.
R = λN = 3.65×10¹⁰ Bq ≈ 1 Ci.
Ex.6 (Hard) Prove T½ = 0.693/λ and find Q for ²²⁶Ra → ²²²Rn + α (masses 226.0254, 222.0176, 4.0026 u).
Proof: N₀/2 = N₀e−λT ⇒ eλT = 2 ⇒ λT = ln2 = 0.693.
Q: Δm = 226.0254 − (222.0176 + 4.0026) = 0.0052 u ⇒ Q = 0.0052×931.5 = 4.84 MeV.
