1. Energy Bands in Solids

In an isolated atom electrons occupy discrete energy levels. In a crystal, the atoms are close, so interaction (Pauli exclusion principle) splits each level into many closely spaced levels forming energy bands.

  • Valence band (VB): band of the outermost bound electrons; at 0 K it is filled for semiconductors/insulators.
  • Conduction band (CB): higher band whose electrons are free to carry current.
  • Forbidden gap Eg: energy gap between top of VB and bottom of CB where no electron can exist.

2. Conductors

CB is partly filled or VB and CB overlap (Eg = 0), so plenty of free electrons exist even at low temperature (Cu, Al, Ag). Resistivity 10⁻² to 10⁻⁸ Ω·m. Resistance increases with temperature (more lattice collisions).

3. Insulators

VB is full, CB empty, and Eg is large (> 3 eV; diamond 5.4 eV). Thermal energy cannot lift electrons across the gap, so conduction is negligible. Resistivity 10¹¹–10¹⁹ Ω·m (glass, rubber, diamond).

4. Semiconductors

Eg is small (< 3 eV): Ge 0.72 eV, Si 1.1 eV. At 0 K they behave as insulators; at room temperature some electrons gain thermal energy and jump to CB, leaving vacancies called holes. Resistivity 10⁻⁵ to 10⁶ Ω·m. Resistance decreases with temperature (negative temperature coefficient) because more charge carriers are produced. Conductivity can be controlled by doping.

5. Intrinsic Semiconductor

A pure semiconductor (pure Si or Ge, each atom forming four covalent bonds). Thermal energy breaks bonds, creating electron–hole pairs, so ne = nh = ni. A hole is the vacant bond that behaves as a positive charge carrier of equal magnitude e. Total current I = Ie + Ih. Conductivity is small at room temperature.

6. Extrinsic Semiconductor (Doping)

Adding a tiny amount (1 in 10⁶–10⁸) of impurity atoms to a pure crystal increases conductivity greatly.

n-type

Doped with pentavalent atoms (P, As, Sb) called donors. Four valence electrons form bonds; the fifth becomes free. Donor level lies just below the CB (≈ 0.01 eV in Ge, 0.05 eV in Si). Majority carriers: electrons; minority: holes (ne ≫ nh).

p-type

Doped with trivalent atoms (B, Al, Ga, In) called acceptors. One bond remains incomplete, creating a hole. Acceptor level lies just above the VB. Majority carriers: holes; minority: electrons (nh ≫ ne).

Both types are electrically neutral. Mass action law: nenh = ni². Conductivity σ = e(neμe + nhμh).

7. Majority and Minority Carriers

In n-type, electrons are majority and holes are minority carriers; in p-type, holes are majority and electrons are minority carriers. Doping raises the majority count and, because nenh = ni² must hold, reduces the minority count. Minority carriers are mostly thermally generated, so they depend strongly on temperature.

Short / Long Questions with Answers

Q1. What is a forbidden energy gap?

The energy gap between the valence band and conduction band in which electrons cannot exist.

Q2. Compare Eg of conductor, semiconductor and insulator.

Conductor 0 (overlap); semiconductor < 3 eV; insulator > 3 eV.

Q3. What is a hole?

A vacancy of an electron in a covalent bond that behaves as a positive charge carrier.

Q4. How does resistance of a semiconductor change with temperature?

It decreases, since more electron–hole pairs form.

Q5. Define intrinsic semiconductor.

A pure semiconductor having ne = nh.

Q6. What is doping?

Deliberate addition of a small amount of impurity to a pure semiconductor to increase conductivity.

Q7. Name donor and acceptor impurities.

Donor: P, As, Sb (pentavalent); acceptor: B, Al, In (trivalent).

Q8. Is n-type semiconductor negatively charged?

No, it is electrically neutral; donor ions carry equal positive charge.

Q9. State the law of mass action for semiconductors.

nenh = ni².

Q10. Why are minority carriers important?

They contribute to reverse current in junction devices and depend on temperature.

Solved Numericals (Step by Step)

Ex.1 (Easy) Find the longest wavelength that can excite an electron in Si (Eg = 1.1 eV).
λ = hc/Eg = 1240 eV·nm/1.1 eV = 1127 nm ≈ 1.13 μm.
Ex.2 (Easy) Same for Ge (0.72 eV).
λ = 1240/0.72 = 1722 nm.
Ex.3 (Medium) Silicon (ni = 1.5×10¹⁶ m⁻³) is doped so that ne = 5×10²² m⁻³. Find nh.
nh = ni²/ne = (1.5×10¹⁶)²/5×10²² = 2.25×10³²/5×10²² = 4.5×10⁹ m⁻³.
Ex.4 (Medium) Conductivity of intrinsic Si: ne = nh = 1.5×10¹⁶ m⁻³, μe = 0.135, μh = 0.048 m²/V·s.
σ = e ni(μe + μh) = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹×1.5×10¹⁶×0.183 = 4.4×10⁻⁴ S/m; ρ = 1/σ ≈ 2280 Ω·m.
Ex.5 (Hard) Ge has 4.4×10²⁸ atoms/m³ and ni = 2.4×10¹⁹ m⁻³. It is doped with 1 donor per 10⁶ atoms. Find ne and nh.
ND = 4.4×10²⁸/10⁶ = 4.4×10²² m⁻³ ≈ ne.
nh = ni²/ne = 5.76×10³⁸/4.4×10²² = 1.3×10¹⁶ m⁻³.
Ex.6 (Hard) Prove that an n-type crystal is electrically neutral.
Positive charges: ionised donors ND+ plus holes nh. Negative charges: free electrons ne. Each donor atom is neutral before ionising; each free electron arises from one ionised donor or one broken bond (which also gives one hole), so ne = ND + nh. Hence total charge = e(ND + nh − ne) = 0.
