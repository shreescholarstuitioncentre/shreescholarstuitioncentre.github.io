Chapter 24: Matter Waves — Class 12 Physics (UP Board)

Animated figure: electrons accelerated by 54 V strike a nickel crystal; the detector, kept at φ = 50°, records a strong peak — the electron diffracts like a wave.

1. De Broglie Hypothesis (1924)

Light shows both wave and particle nature. Louis de Broglie argued that nature is symmetric, so moving material particles must also have a wave associated with them. These are called matter waves (de Broglie waves).

λ = h/p = h/mv
h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J·s, p = momentum.

Derivation idea: for a photon E = hν = hc/λ and E = pc, so p = h/λ. De Broglie applied the same relation to particles.

2. Matter Waves — Properties and Formulae

  • λ ∝ 1/p: heavier or faster particle → smaller wavelength.
  • Matter waves are not electromagnetic; they exist for uncharged particles too (neutrons) and are not emitted by charge acceleration.
  • With kinetic energy K: λ = h/√(2mK).
  • Electron accelerated through V volts: λ = h/√(2meV) = 12.27/√V Å.
  • Proton: λ = 0.286/√V Å; α-particle: λ = 0.101/√V Å.
  • Thermal neutron / gas atom: λ = h/√(3mkT).
  • Group velocity of the wave packet = particle velocity v; phase velocity = c²/v.

Why not seen for everyday objects: h is extremely small, so λ of a cricket ball is ~10⁻³⁴ m, far below any detectable size.

3. Davisson–Germer Experiment (1927)

Aim: to verify the wave nature of electrons.

Set-up: electrons from a heated filament are accelerated by potential V through an electron gun, form a narrow beam and strike a nickel crystal. A movable detector (Faraday cylinder + galvanometer) measures electrons scattered at angle φ from the incident beam.

Observation

At V = 54 V a sharp peak of scattered intensity occurred at φ = 50°; at other voltages the peak was weaker or absent.

Calculation

Bragg’s law: 2d sinθ = nλ, with θ = (180° − φ)/2 = 65° and d = 0.91 Å for Ni. So λ = 2×0.91×sin65° = 1.65 Å.
De Broglie: λ = 12.27/√54 = 1.67 Å. The values agree, confirming the wave nature of electrons.

G. P. Thomson independently got diffraction rings by passing electrons through thin metal foil.

4. Wave Nature of Matter — Evidence and Uses

Diffraction of electrons, neutrons, protons and even atoms/molecules (He, H₂) is observed. Uses: electron microscope (λ ~ 0.1 Å gives resolution far better than optical), neutron diffraction for crystal structure, and electron diffraction for surface studies.

Light: wave (interference, diffraction) and particle (photoelectric effect). Matter: particle (mass, momentum) and wave (diffraction). This is wave–particle duality.

Short / Long Questions with Answers

Q1. State the de Broglie hypothesis.

A moving particle has an associated wave of wavelength λ = h/p.

Q2. What are matter waves?

Waves associated with every moving material particle; not electromagnetic.

Q3. Write de Broglie wavelength for an electron accelerated through V.

λ = 12.27/√V Å.

Q4. Why are matter waves not observed for a cricket ball?

Its λ is ~10⁻³⁴ m, immeasurably small because h is tiny.

Q5. Which experiment verified matter waves?

Davisson–Germer experiment (1927).

Q6. What was the peak angle and voltage there?

φ = 50° at 54 V.

Q7. Why is a nickel crystal used?

Its atomic spacing (~0.91 Å) is comparable to the electron wavelength, so it acts as a diffraction grating.

Q8. If speed of a particle doubles, what happens to λ?

It becomes half.

Q9. Do matter waves require a medium?

No.

Q10. Why does an electron microscope give high resolution?

Electron λ is much smaller than that of visible light, and resolution improves with smaller λ.

Solved Numericals (Step by Step)

Ex.1 (Easy) Find λ of an electron accelerated through 100 V.
λ = 12.27/√100 = 12.27/10 = 1.227 Å.
Ex.2 (Easy) Find λ for a 0.1 kg ball moving at 10 m/s.
p = mv = 1 kg·m/s. λ = h/p = 6.63×10⁻³⁴/1 = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ m (undetectable).
Ex.3 (Medium) Find the voltage for λ = 1 Å of an electron.
λ = 12.27/√V ⇒ √V = 12.27 ⇒ V = 150.6 ≈ 151 V.
Ex.4 (Hard) A proton and an α-particle are accelerated through the same V. Find λp/λα.
λ = h/√(2mqV) ∝ 1/√(mq). mα = 4mp, qα = 2qp. Ratio = √(4×2) = 2√2 ≈ 2.83.
Ex.5 (Hard) Prove λ = h/√(2meV) for an electron.
eV = ½mv² = p²/2m ⇒ p = √(2meV). λ = h/p = h/√(2meV). Substituting values gives 12.27/√V Å. Hence proved.
