Animated figure: electrons boiled off the heated filament are accelerated by the high voltage and strike the tungsten target; X-rays (red) leave the slanted face.

1. X-Rays — Discovery and Nature

Discovered by W. C. Röntgen (1895). X-rays are electromagnetic waves of wavelength about 0.1 Å to 100 Å (frequency ~10¹⁶–10¹⁹ Hz), lying between ultraviolet and gamma rays. Properties: travel in straight lines at speed c; not deflected by electric or magnetic fields (uncharged); ionise gases; affect photographic plates; cause fluorescence; produce photoelectric effect; penetrate matter (less by dense materials such as lead and bone); show diffraction by crystals.

2. Coolidge Tube (1913)

A highly evacuated glass tube containing:

  • Cathode: tungsten filament heated by a low-voltage supply; it emits electrons by thermionic emission. A molybdenum focusing cup makes them a narrow beam.
  • Anode (target): tungsten (high melting point, high Z) embedded in a copper stem, slanted at 45° so X-rays leave sideways. Copper conducts heat to cooling fins (or oil/water/rotating anode).
  • High voltage (30–100 kV) between cathode and anode accelerates electrons.
Intensity of X-rays is controlled by filament current (number of electrons).
Penetrating power (quality) is controlled by tube voltage: high voltage → hard X-rays; low voltage → soft X-rays.

Only about 1% of the electron energy becomes X-rays; about 99% becomes heat.

3. Production of X-Rays

When fast electrons strike the target, they are suddenly stopped or deflected by the nuclei and by inner electrons of the atoms. This produces two kinds of X-rays: a continuous spectrum and a characteristic spectrum.

4. Continuous Spectrum

A decelerating charge radiates (bremsstrahlung, ‘braking radiation’). Different electrons lose different fractions of energy, so all wavelengths above a limit appear.

Maximum photon energy = eV, so
λmin = hc/eV = 12400/V Å (V in volts)
(Duane–Hunt law). λmin depends only on V, not on the target material.

Raising V shifts λmin to shorter wavelengths and raises intensity.

5. Characteristic X-Rays

If a fast electron knocks out an inner-shell (K) electron of a target atom, a vacancy forms. An electron from a higher shell drops in, emitting a photon of fixed energy equal to the shell energy difference.

  • Kα: L → K transition; Kβ: M → K; L-series: M, N → L.

These sharp lines appear on the continuous background only when V exceeds a critical value. They depend on the target element (its fingerprint), not on V.

6. Moseley’s Law (1913)

√ν = a(Z − b)
For Kα line: ν = (3Rc/4)(Z − 1)², b = 1 (screening by the remaining K electron).
A plot of √ν against Z is a straight line.

Proof (from Bohr): 1/λ = R(Z−1)²(1/1² − 1/2²) = (3R/4)(Z−1)². Importance: atomic number Z, not atomic mass, decides the properties of an element; Moseley fixed the position of elements in the periodic table and predicted missing elements (Tc, Pm, Hf, Re).

7. Uses and Hazards

Medical diagnosis (bone fractures), cancer radiotherapy, industrial flaw detection, airport security, crystal structure study (Bragg’s law 2d sinθ = nλ). Overexposure damages tissues, so lead shields are used.

Short / Long Questions with Answers

Q1. Who discovered X-rays?

W. C. Röntgen, in 1895.

Q2. What is the nature of X-rays?

Electromagnetic waves of wavelength about 0.1–100 Å.

Q3. Why are X-rays not deflected by fields?

They carry no charge.

Q4. Why is tungsten used as target?

High atomic number and very high melting point.

Q5. What controls the intensity of X-rays?

Filament current (number of electrons striking the target).

Q6. What controls the penetrating power?

Accelerating voltage across the tube.

Q7. Define cut-off wavelength.

Minimum wavelength λmin = hc/eV in the continuous spectrum.

Q8. Why does the continuous spectrum have a sharp cut-off?

A photon cannot have energy larger than the electron’s kinetic energy eV.

Q9. State Moseley’s law.

The square root of the frequency of a characteristic X-ray line is proportional to (Z − b).

Q10. What is the difference between hard and soft X-rays?

Hard X-rays have short λ and high penetration; soft X-rays have long λ and low penetration.

Answer: (c)

Answer: (b)

Solved Numericals (Step by Step)

Ex.1 (Easy) Find λmin for a 40 kV tube.
λmin = 12400/40000 = 0.31 Å.
Ex.2 (Easy) λmin = 0.62 Å. Find the tube voltage.
V = 12400/0.62 = 20000 V = 20 kV.
Ex.3 (Medium) Find maximum frequency of X-rays at 20 kV.
νmax = eV/h = (1.6×10⁻¹⁹×2×10⁴)/(6.63×10⁻³⁴) = 4.83×10¹⁸ Hz.
Ex.4 (Hard) Find Kα wavelength of copper (Z = 29), R = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹.
1/λ = (3R/4)(Z−1)² = 0.75×1.097×10⁷×28² = 0.75×1.097×10⁷×784 = 6.45×10⁹ m⁻¹ ⇒ λ = 1.55×10⁻¹⁰ m = 1.55 Å.
Ex.5 (Hard) Prove ν ∝ (Z−1)² for Kα and find Z of an element whose Kα frequency is 4 times that of Cu.
Proof: 1/λ = (3R/4)(Z−1)² ⇒ ν = c/λ ∝ (Z−1)².
Find Z: (Z−1)²/(29−1)² = 4 ⇒ Z−1 = 2×28 = 56 ⇒ Z = 57.
