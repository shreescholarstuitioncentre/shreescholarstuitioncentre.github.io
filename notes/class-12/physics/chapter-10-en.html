Ch 10: Magnetic Properties of Matter

1. Basic Terms

  • Magnetic intensity (H) = magnetising field applied from outside. For a solenoid, H = nI (unit A/m).
  • Magnetisation (I or M) = magnetic moment per unit volume, I = m/V (unit A/m). It tells how strongly the material gets magnetised.
  • Magnetic induction (B) = total field inside the material.
  • Permeability (μ) = B/H. For vacuum μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A.
  • Relative permeability (μr) = μ/μ₀. It has no unit.
  • Susceptibility (χ) = I/H. It has no unit and shows how easily a material is magnetised.
B = μ₀(H + I)  |  μr = 1 + χ  |  μ = μ₀(1 + χ)  |  B = μ₀μr H

Proof of μr = 1 + χ: B = μ₀(H+I). Divide by μ₀H: B/(μ₀H) = 1 + I/H. The left side is μr and I/H is χ, so μr = 1 + χ. ∎

3. Diamagnetic Materials

  • Atoms have no permanent magnetic moment because the moments of orbiting and spinning electrons cancel in pairs.
  • In an external field, by Lenz's law, an induced moment appears opposite to the field. So the material is weakly repelled and moves from the stronger to the weaker field region.
  • χ is small and negative (about −10⁻⁵) and μr is slightly less than 1.
  • χ is independent of temperature.
  • Examples: Bi, Cu, Ag, Au, Zn, Pb, water, NaCl, diamond, N₂, H₂.
  • A rod suspended freely sets itself perpendicular to the field.
  • Superconductors are perfect diamagnets (χ = −1, B = 0 inside). This is the Meissner effect.

4. Paramagnetic Materials

  • Atoms have permanent dipole moments but they point randomly, so the net moment is zero without a field.
  • In a field, the dipoles partly align along the field. Thermal motion opposes this alignment. The material is weakly attracted.
  • χ is small and positive (10⁻⁵ to 10⁻³) and μr is slightly greater than 1.
  • χ decreases as temperature rises.
  • Examples: Al, Na, Pt, Mn, Cr, CuCl₂, liquid and gaseous O₂.
  • A freely suspended rod sets itself parallel to the field.

5. Curie's Law

χ = C/T   (or I = C·B/T)
C = Curie constant (in kelvin), T = absolute temperature
  • It holds for paramagnets in a weak field and at high temperature.
  • Reason: a higher T gives stronger random thermal motion, so alignment is lower and χ is smaller. Doubling T halves χ.
  • For ferromagnets above the Curie temperature, Curie–Weiss law: χ = C/(T − Tc).
  • At very low T with a strong field, magnetisation saturates and Curie's law fails.

6. Ferromagnetic Materials

  • Examples: Fe, Co, Ni, Gd, Alnico, and Fe₃O₄ (ferrite, nearly).
  • χ is very large and positive (10²–10⁵). They are strongly attracted by magnets.
  • Domain theory: a material is divided into small regions called domains (about 10⁻⁶ m size), each fully magnetised. Without a field, domains point in random directions, so the net moment is zero. In a field, domains aligned with it grow (wall motion) and others rotate toward it. At saturation all are aligned.
  • Curie temperature (Tc): above Tc the domains break up and the material becomes paramagnetic. Fe: 1043 K, Co: 1388 K, Ni: 627 K.

7. Hysteresis Curve (B–H loop)

Hysteresis means B lags behind H. The point travels along the path: O → S (saturation) → Br (retentivity) → Hc (coercivity) → S′ → −Br → +Hc → S.

  • Retentivity: B left in the material when H = 0.
  • Coercivity: reverse H needed to bring B to zero.
  • Loop area = energy lost as heat per unit volume per cycle (J/m³).
  • Soft iron: narrow loop, low retentivity, low coercivity, small loss. Used in electromagnets, transformer cores, motors.
  • Steel: wide loop, high retentivity, high coercivity. Used for permanent magnets.

8. Electromagnets and Permanent Magnets

Electromagnet: a soft-iron core inside a solenoid. Strength ∝ nI, and the core multiplies the field by μr. It can be switched on/off and its strength varied. Uses: cranes, electric bells, relays, MRI.
Permanent magnet: made from material with high retentivity and high coercivity, such as steel, alnico, cobalt steel, ticonal, nipermag. It keeps its magnetism for a long time. Uses: compass, loudspeaker, galvanometer, dynamo.

9. Solved Numericals (step by step)

Easy 1. A solenoid has 1000 turns/m and carries 2 A. Find H.

Given: n = 1000 m⁻¹, I = 2 A. Formula: H = nI. Solution: H = 1000 × 2 = 2000 A/m.

Easy 2. A material has μ = 4π×10⁻⁴ T·m/A. Find μr and χ.

μr = μ/μ₀ = (4π×10⁻⁴)/(4π×10⁻⁷) = 1000. χ = μr − 1 = 999.

Intermediate 3. Al has χ = 2.3×10⁻⁵ at 300 K. Find χ at 200 K.

Curie's law: χ ∝ 1/T. χ₂ = χ₁ × T₁/T₂ = 2.3×10⁻⁵ × 300/200 = 3.45×10⁻⁵.

Intermediate 4. Iron core (μr = 1000) in a solenoid with n = 1000 m⁻¹, I = 2 A. Find B.

H = nI = 2000 A/m. B = μ₀μr H = 4π×10⁻⁷ × 1000 × 2000 = 2.51 T.

Hard 5. Al has χ = 2.3×10⁻⁵ at 300 K. At what temperature is χ = 6.9×10⁻⁵?

χ₁T₁ = χ₂T₂. T₂ = (2.3×10⁻⁵ × 300)/(6.9×10⁻⁵) = 100 K.

Hard 6. B–H loop area = 250 J/m³ per cycle. Core volume = 0.02 m³, 50 Hz. Find hysteresis loss per hour.

Energy per cycle = 250 × 0.02 = 5 J. Per second: 5 × 50 = 250 W. Per hour: 250 × 3600 = 9×10⁵ J.

10. Q & A

Q1 (Short). Why are diamagnetic substances repelled by a magnet?

An induced magnetic moment opposite to the applied field appears (Lenz's law), so the material is pushed toward the weaker field.

Q2 (Short). State Curie's law.

Susceptibility of a paramagnet is inversely proportional to absolute temperature: χ = C/T.

Q3 (Short). What is Curie temperature?

The temperature above which a ferromagnet becomes paramagnetic because its domains are destroyed.

Q4 (Short). Why is soft iron used for transformer cores?

It has low retentivity and coercivity, so a small hysteresis loop and small energy loss.

Q5 (Long). Explain domain theory of ferromagnetism.

Ferromagnets contain domains, each fully magnetised. With no field their moments cancel. A field makes favourably aligned domains grow and others rotate. At saturation all point along the field. Heating above Tc breaks domains, giving paramagnetism.

Q6 (Long). Draw and explain the hysteresis loop; define retentivity and coercivity.

See section 7. Retentivity is B at H = 0. Coercivity is reverse H needed to make B zero. Loop area is energy loss per cycle per unit volume.

Q7 (Long). Distinguish dia, para and ferromagnetic substances.

Refer to the table in section 6.

Q8 (MCQ). Which has the largest χ? (a) Cu (b) Al (c) Fe (d) Bi

(c) Fe. Iron is ferromagnetic with a very large χ.

Q9 (MCQ). The unit of magnetisation is: (a) T (b) A/m (c) Wb (d) A·m²

(b) A/m. It is magnetic moment per unit volume.

Q10 (MCQ). Which material's χ is independent of temperature? (a) para (b) dia (c) ferro (d) all

(b) diamagnetic.
