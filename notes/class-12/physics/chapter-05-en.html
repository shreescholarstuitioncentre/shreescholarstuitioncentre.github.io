Class 12 Physics – Electric Current and Ohm's Law (Notes)

1. Electric Current

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a cross-section of a conductor.

I = q / t,   I = dq/dt

SI unit ampere (A) = coulomb/second. Dimension [A]. Current is a scalar though it has a direction (it adds algebraically). Conventional current flows from +ve to −ve terminal (direction of motion of positive charge); in metals electrons actually move opposite. 1 A means 1 C charge crosses per second, i.e. about 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons. Steady current is DC; one that reverses periodically is AC.

2. Drift Velocity

Free electrons in a metal move randomly at thermal speed (~10⁵–10⁶ m/s) and collide with ions, so their average velocity is zero and no current flows. On applying an electric field E, each electron gets acceleration a = eE/m opposite to E. It gains speed until the next collision and then loses it. The average velocity gained is the drift velocity.

Let τ = relaxation time (average time between collisions). Then

v_d = a τ = e E τ / m

Current in terms of v_d: in time Δt electrons within a length v_dΔt cross area A. Number = n A v_d Δt (n = free electrons per unit volume). Charge = n e A v_d Δt, so

I = n e A v_d

Typical v_d ≈ 10⁻⁴ m/s (very small). The bulb glows at once because the electric field travels through the wire at nearly the speed of light and sets all electrons drifting together.

3. Mobility

Drift velocity per unit electric field.

μ = v_d / E = e τ / m

Unit m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹. Larger mobility means better conduction; mobility is higher in good conductors with larger τ.

4. Current Density

Current per unit area normal to the flow. J = I/A = n e v_d. Vector (direction of E for positive charge), unit A/m². Vector relation: J = σ E (σ = conductivity). This is the microscopic form of Ohm's law.

5. Ohm's Law

V ∝ I  ⟹  V = I R

At constant temperature (and other physical conditions), current through a conductor is proportional to the potential difference across its ends. R is the resistance.

Derivation of R from drift (microscopic)

V = E l, so E = V/l. I = neAv_d = neA(eEτ/m) = (ne²τA/m)(V/l). So V/I = R:

R = (m / ne²τ)(l / A) = ρ l / A,   ρ = m / (n e² τ)

ρ depends only on the material (n, τ), not on l or A.

6. V–I Characteristics

  • Ohmic conductors (metals at constant temperature): straight line through the origin, slope = 1/R constant.
  • Non-ohmic devices (diode, thermistor, vacuum tube, electrolyte): curved or unsymmetrical V–I graph, R changes with V.

7. Resistance, Resistivity, Conductance, Conductivity

Resistance R = V/I, unit ohm (Ω) = V/A, dimensions [M L² T⁻³ A⁻²]. R = ρ l / A. R depends on length, area, material and temperature.

Resistivity ρ = RA/l; unit Ω m. Property of the material; copper 1.7×10⁻⁸ Ω m, nichrome ≈ 10⁻⁶ Ω m. Conductance G = 1/R, unit siemens (S) = Ω⁻¹. Conductivity σ = 1/ρ, unit S/m.

Stretching a wire (volume constant): A l = const. If l becomes n times, A becomes 1/n, so R′ = n² R. R ∝ l² ∝ 1/A² ∝ 1/r⁴.

8. Colour Code of Resistors

First two bands: significant digits; third: multiplier (power of 10); fourth: tolerance (gold ±5%, silver ±10%, none ±20%).

Black 0, Brown 1, Red 2, Orange 3, Yellow 4, Green 5, Blue 6, Violet 7, Grey 8, White 9. Memory aid: B B ROY of Great Britain had a Very Good Wife.

9. Temperature Dependence

R_t = R₀[1 + α(t − t₀)],   α = (R_t − R₀)/R₀(t − t₀)

α = temperature coefficient of resistance, unit °C⁻¹ (K⁻¹).

  • Metals: α positive. Higher T gives more ion vibration so τ falls, R rises.
  • Semiconductors/insulators: α negative. Higher T frees many more charge carriers (n rises greatly), so R falls.
  • Alloys (manganin, constantan): ρ high, α very small, hence used for standard resistors and resistance boxes.
  • Superconductors: R = 0 below a critical temperature.

10. Electrical Energy and Power

Work done by the source in time t: W = V I t = I²R t = V²t/R (joule). This appears as heat in a resistor (Joule heating). Power P = W/t: P = VI = I²R = V²/R, unit watt (W) = J/s.

Commercial unit: kilowatt-hour (kWh) = "1 unit". 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J. Bill = units consumed × rate.

11. Combination of Resistors

Series

Same current; voltages add. R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃. V divides in proportion to R. R is larger than the largest.

Parallel

Same voltage; currents add. 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃. Two resistors: R₁R₂/(R₁+R₂). n equal: R/n. R is smaller than the smallest. Current divides inversely as R.

Mixed

Reduce step by step: combine parallel groups, then series.

Questions & Answers (Theory and MCQs)

Q1. Define current and its SI unit. Is it a vector? (2)

I = dq/dt; unit ampere. It has direction but follows scalar addition, so it is a scalar.

Q2. Define drift velocity and relaxation time. (2)

Drift velocity: average velocity of electrons under a field, v_d = eEτ/m. Relaxation time: average time between two successive collisions.

Q3. Derive I = neAv_d. (3)

Electrons in length v_dΔt cross A in Δt: charge = n·A·v_dΔt·e. I = charge/Δt = neAv_d.

Q4. Why does a bulb glow instantly if v_d is so small? (2)

The field spreads at nearly c, so electrons all along the wire start drifting at once.

Q5. Define mobility. Give its relation and unit. (2)

μ = v_d/E = eτ/m; unit m²V⁻¹s⁻¹.

Q6. Derive Ohm's law and R = ρl/A from drift velocity. (5)

I = neAv_d = (ne²τA/m)(V/l), so V/I = (m/ne²τ)(l/A) = R = ρl/A, ρ = m/ne²τ.

Q7. Why does resistance of a metal increase and of a semiconductor decrease with temperature? (3)

Metal: τ decreases (more collisions), n nearly constant, so R rises. Semiconductor: n increases much faster than τ falls, so R falls.

Q8. Why are standard resistances made of manganin or constantan? (2)

High resistivity and very low α, so R stays nearly the same as temperature changes.

Q9. Derive: P = VI = I²R = V²/R. (3)

W = qV = VIt, so P = VI. With V = IR: P = I²R = V²/R.

Q10. Why do we connect household appliances in parallel? (2)

Each gets full mains voltage, can be switched independently, and one failing does not stop the others.

Solved Numericals (Easy → Hard)

Easy 1. 120 C of charge passes in 1 minute. Find I.
I = q/t = 120/60 = 2 A.
Easy 2. V = 12 V across R = 4 Ω. Find I.
I = V/R = 12/4 = 3 A.
Medium 1. Copper wire I = 3 A, A = 2×10⁻⁶ m², n = 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³. Find v_d.
v_d = I/(neA) = 3/(8.5×10²⁸ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 2×10⁻⁶). Denominator = 1.36×10¹⁰ × 2×10⁻⁶ = 2.72×10⁴. v_d = 1.1×10⁻⁴ m/s.
Medium 2. R of copper wire l = 2 m, d = 0.5 mm, ρ = 1.7×10⁻⁸ Ω m.
A = πr² = 3.14×(0.25×10⁻³)² = 1.96×10⁻⁷ m². R = ρl/A = 1.7×10⁻⁸ × 2/1.96×10⁻⁷ = 0.17 Ω.
Medium 3. 2 kW heater runs 5 h at ₹7 per unit. Find the cost.
Energy = 2 kW × 5 h = 10 kWh = 10 units. Cost = 10 × 7 = ₹70.
Hard 1. A wire of resistance 5 Ω is stretched to twice its length. New R?
Volume constant, A halves. R′ = ρ(2l)/(A/2) = 4ρl/A = 4R = 20 Ω.
Hard 2. R₁ = 4 Ω in series with 6 Ω ∥ 3 Ω, across 12 V. Find currents.
6 ∥ 3 = 6×3/9 = 2 Ω. R_total = 4 + 2 = 6 Ω. I = 12/6 = 2 A. V across parallel = 2×2 = 4 V. I(6 Ω) = 4/6 = 0.67 A; I(3 Ω) = 4/3 = 1.33 A (sum 2 A).
Hard 3. R = 10 Ω at 20 °C, α = 0.004 °C⁻¹. Find R at 120 °C.
R = R₀[1 + αΔT] = 10[1 + 0.004×100] = 10 × 1.4 = 14 Ω.
Hard 4. A bulb is marked 100 W, 220 V. Find R, I, and power at 110 V.
R = V²/P = 48400/100 = 484 Ω. I = P/V = 100/220 = 0.45 A. At 110 V (R constant): P′ = V²/R = 12100/484 = 25 W.
Hard 5. Read the colour code Yellow, Violet, Orange, Gold.
Yellow = 4, Violet = 7, Orange = ×10³, Gold = ±5%. R = 47 × 10³ = 47 kΩ ± 5%.
