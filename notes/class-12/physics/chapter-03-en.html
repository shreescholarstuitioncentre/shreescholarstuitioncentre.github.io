Class 12 Physics – Electric Potential and Potential Energy (Notes)

1. Electric Potential and Potential Difference

Potential difference between two points is the work done per unit positive test charge in moving it slowly (without acceleration) from one point to the other against the electric field.

V_B − V_A = W_AB / q₀

Electric potential at a point is the work done per unit positive charge in bringing it from infinity to that point. Unit: volt (V) = J/C. Dimensions [M L² T⁻³ A⁻¹]. It is a scalar. 1 volt: 1 joule of work to bring 1 coulomb.

Relation with field: E = − dV/dr. The field points in the direction in which potential falls fastest. Unit of E can be V/m. For uniform field, E = ΔV/d.

Potential due to a point charge

Bring a unit +ve test charge from infinity to distance r. Force at distance x is kq/x² (outward for +q), work done against it: V = −∫(∞→r) E·dx = −∫ kq/x² dx = kq/r.

V = kq / r = q / (4πε₀ r)

+ve for +q, −ve for −q. V = 0 at infinity (reference). Note V ∝ 1/r but E ∝ 1/r².

Potential due to a system of charges

Potentials are scalars, so just add them algebraically with sign: V = kq₁/r₁ + kq₂/r₂ + kq₃/r₃ + … For a continuous distribution V = ∫ k dq / r.

Potential due to an electric dipole

Dipole ±q, separation 2a, P at distance r from the centre, the line OP making angle θ with p.

The distances: r₁ ≈ r − a cosθ from +q, r₂ ≈ r + a cosθ from −q. V = kq(1/r₁ − 1/r₂) = kq·2a cosθ/(r² − a²cos²θ). For r ≫ a:

V = k p cosθ / r²

  • Axial (θ = 0° or 180°): V = ± kp/r².
  • Equatorial (θ = 90°): V = 0, so moving a charge along the equatorial line needs no work.

Dipole potential falls as 1/r² (faster than point charge).

2. Equipotential Surfaces

A surface on which every point has the same potential.

Properties

  • No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface (W = qΔV = 0).
  • The electric field is always perpendicular to the surface, because if E had a component along the surface, work would be done.
  • Two equipotential surfaces never intersect (the point would have two potentials).
  • E points from higher to lower potential; spacing small means E is strong, spacing wide means E is weak.
  • Shapes: point charge gives concentric spheres; uniform field gives parallel planes ⟂ E; line charge gives coaxial cylinders.
  • The surface of a conductor in electrostatics is equipotential.

3. Electrostatic Potential Energy

Of two point charges

Work done to bring q₂ from infinity to distance r from q₁ stored as energy: U = k q₁q₂ / r. Like charges: U positive (repulsive, work needed). Unlike: U negative (bound).

Of a system of charges

Add the energy for every pair once: U = k[q₁q₂/r₁₂ + q₂q₃/r₂₃ + q₃q₁/r₃₁]. For n charges there are n(n−1)/2 pairs. Signs of charges must be included.

Of a dipole in a uniform field

Torque on dipole at angle θ is pE sinθ. Work to rotate from θ₁ to θ₂: W = ∫pE sinθ dθ = pE(cosθ₁ − cosθ₂).

U = − p E cosθ = − p · E

(taking U = 0 at θ = 90°). Minimum U = −pE at θ = 0° (stable). Maximum U = +pE at θ = 180° (unstable). Work to turn from 0° to 180° is 2pE.

4. Conductors and Insulators

Conductors have free charges (electrons in metals) that move in response to a field. Insulators have no free charges; all electrons are bound to their atoms.

Electrostatics of conductors

  • Inside a conductor E = 0 (free charges rearrange until the internal field cancels).
  • Excess charge resides only on the outer surface.
  • E just outside the surface is perpendicular to it with magnitude σ/ε₀.
  • The potential is constant throughout (inside and on the surface).
  • Electrostatic shielding: a cavity inside a conductor is free from outside fields, so instruments are put inside metal boxes; a car or a building with metallic frame is safe in lightning.

5. Free Charges and Bound Charges

Free charges are mobile charges (electrons in metals, ions in electrolytes) which can move through the material. Bound charges are the charges tied to atoms/molecules of a dielectric; they can only be displaced slightly. Bound charges appear on the faces of a dielectric when it is polarised.

6. Dielectrics and Polarisation

Dielectrics are insulating substances that get polarised in an external electric field.

  • Non-polar molecules (N₂, O₂, H₂, CO₂): centres of +ve and −ve charge coincide. The field separates them, producing an induced dipole moment.
  • Polar molecules (H₂O, HCl, NH₃): permanent dipole moment, randomly oriented. The field aligns them partly (thermal motion opposes).

Polarisation P = dipole moment per unit volume. P = χₑ ε₀ E (χₑ = electric susceptibility). Unit C/m².

Induced bound charges on the faces create a field E_p opposite to E₀: E = E₀ − E_p. So the net field inside is reduced:

K = E₀ / E = ε / ε₀ = 1 + χₑ

K is the dielectric constant (relative permittivity). K = 1 for vacuum, ≈ 80 for water, ∞ for a metal conductor. This is why capacitance rises to C = K C₀ when the dielectric fills the gap.

Questions & Answers (Theory and MCQs)

Q1. Define electric potential and potential difference. Give units. (2)

Potential: work done per unit +ve charge in bringing it from infinity to the point. P.D.: work per unit charge between two points. Unit volt = joule/coulomb.

Q2. Derive the potential due to a point charge. (3)

V = −∫(∞ to r)(kq/x²)dx = kq[1/x] from ∞ to r = kq/r. (Work done against the field per unit charge.)

Q3. Derive V due to a dipole at any point. (5)

r₁ ≈ r − a cosθ, r₂ ≈ r + a cosθ. V = kq(1/r₁ − 1/r₂) = kq(2a cosθ)/(r² − a²cos²θ). For r ≫ a, V = kp cosθ/r².

Q4. Why is the potential zero on the equatorial line of a dipole? (2)

θ = 90°, cosθ = 0, so V = 0. Equivalently the point is equidistant from +q and −q, so the potentials cancel.

Q5. What are equipotential surfaces? State their properties. (3)

Surfaces with equal potential. Work along them is zero; E ⟂ surface; they never intersect; they are closer where E is stronger.

Q6. Prove E is perpendicular to the equipotential surface. (2)

If E had a component along the surface, a charge moved along it would have work done, so potentials would differ, contradicting equal potential. So E ⟂ surface.

Q7. Derive the P.E. of a dipole in a uniform field. (5)

W = ∫pE sinθ dθ from 90° to θ = −pE cosθ stored as U = −pE cosθ. Min at 0°, max at 180°.

Q8. Why is E = 0 inside a conductor? (2)

Free charges move until the field they create cancels the applied field. Any non-zero E would keep driving charges.

Q9. What is polarisation? How does a dielectric reduce the field? (3)

Polarisation is dipole moment per unit volume. Bound charges on the faces produce E_p opposite to E₀, so E = E₀ − E_p < E₀.

Q10. Difference between polar and non-polar dielectrics. (2)

Polar: permanent dipoles (H₂O); field only aligns them. Non-polar: no permanent dipole (O₂); the field induces one.

Solved Numericals (Easy → Hard)

Easy 1. Potential at 30 cm from a 2 µC charge.
V = kq/r = 9×10⁹ × 2×10⁻⁶ / 0.3 = 1.8×10⁴/0.3 = 6×10⁴ V.
Easy 2. Work to move 5 µC through 100 V.
W = qΔV = 5×10⁻⁶ × 100 = 5×10⁻⁴ J.
Medium 1. +2 µC and −2 µC are 20 cm apart. Find V and E at the midpoint.
V = kq/0.1 + k(−q)/0.1 = 0 (scalar sum). E: both fields point towards −q, so E = 2kq/r² = 2×9×10⁹×2×10⁻⁶/(0.1)² = 3.6×10⁶ N/C. Note V = 0 but E ≠ 0.
Medium 2. P.E. of 2 µC and 3 µC at 10 cm.
U = kq₁q₂/r = 9×10⁹ × 6×10⁻¹² / 0.1 = 5.4×10⁻²/0.1 = 0.54 J.
Medium 3. Dipole p = 4×10⁻⁹ C m. Find V at 20 cm on its axis and on its equator.
Axis: V = kp/r² = 9×10⁹ × 4×10⁻⁹/0.04 = 36/0.04 = 900 V. Equator: 0.
Hard 1. P.E. of three 1 µC charges at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side 10 cm.
3 pairs, each kq²/r = 9×10⁹×10⁻¹²/0.1 = 0.09 J. U = 3 × 0.09 = 0.27 J.
Hard 2. Work to rotate a dipole p = 3×10⁻⁸ C m from 0° to 180° in E = 2×10⁴ N/C.
W = pE(cos0° − cos180°) = pE × 2 = 2 × 3×10⁻⁸ × 2×10⁴ = 1.2×10⁻³ J.
Hard 3. An electron is accelerated through 100 V from rest. Find KE and speed.
KE = eV = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 100 = 1.6×10⁻¹⁷ J. v = √(2KE/m) = √(3.2×10⁻¹⁷/9.1×10⁻³¹) = √(3.5×10¹³) ≈ 5.9×10⁶ m/s.
Hard 4. K = 5 and E₀ = 10⁴ N/C. Find E inside and E_p.
E = E₀/K = 10⁴/5 = 2×10³ N/C. E_p = E₀ − E = 8×10³ N/C.
