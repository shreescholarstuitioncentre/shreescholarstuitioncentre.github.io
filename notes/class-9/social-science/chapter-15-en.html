Working of Institutions

Chapter at a glance: Democracy is not only about elections. Governments take decisions every day, and those decisions are taken through institutions – rules, offices and procedures. This chapter shows how the Legislature (Parliament), the Executive (Government) and the Judiciary (Courts) work together, using the famous Mandal Commission decision as a case study.
  • Who takes a major decision? ➜ Political executive (PM + Cabinet).
  • Who makes laws & controls the government? ➜ Parliament.
  • Who checks if laws and actions are legal? ➜ Judiciary.

1. How is a Major Policy Decision Taken?

On 13 August 1990 the Government of India issued an Office Memorandum (Government Order). It announced that 27% of vacancies in civil posts and services of the Central Government would be reserved for the Socially and Educationally Backward Classes (SEBC). This is one of the most important decisions in India’s recent history and helps us understand how institutions work.

Background

  • In 1979 the Janata Party government appointed the Second Backward Classes Commission under B.P. Mandal – hence the Mandal Commission.
  • Its job: to find out the social and educational backwardness of various castes and suggest measures for their upliftment.
  • The Commission gave its report in 1980 and recommended 27% reservation of jobs for backward classes (SEBC / OBC). SC & ST already had separate reservation.
  • Many governments kept the report aside. In August 1990 the National Front government led by Prime Minister V.P. Singh decided to implement it.

Who were the decision makers?

Institution / personRole in the decision
President of IndiaNominal head; all government orders are issued in his name. Appoints PM, ministers and judges.
Prime Minister & CabinetTook the real decision on 6 Aug 1990 – the political executive.
Civil servantsPrepared the order, issued it and implemented it – the permanent executive.
ParliamentDebated the issue; Opposition and members expressed views; controls the government.
Supreme CourtHeard the petitions and gave the final verdict in 1992 – judiciary.
Remember: The Supreme Court did not reject the decision – it approved reservation with a limit: total reservation should not cross 50%, and the creamy layer is excluded. In 1991 the next government had also added a further 10% for economically poorer sections, which the Court struck down in 1992.
Key idea: A decision is not final just because the government has taken it. It must be accepted by Parliament, implemented by the bureaucracy and may be tested in courts. Each institution has a defined role.

2. Need for Political Institutions

The Mandal story shows that many persons and offices are involved in a decision. A government has to do many things: collect taxes, spend money, maintain law and order, run schools and hospitals, defend the country. Every task needs a procedure and a responsible office.

Institution: a set of rules, offices and procedures through which decisions are taken and implemented – e.g. Parliament, the Cabinet, the Supreme Court.
  • All activities of government need institutions so that work is not left to the whims of one person.
  • Institutions have rules and procedures, so there is discussion, meetings, files and records. This can be slow and complicated – but it prevents hasty and wrong decisions.
  • Institutions also protect from one-man rule: when the person changes, the institution continues.
  • Sometimes the procedures lead to delay or conflict, but this is the price of democratic decision-making where everyone’s view can be heard.

3. Parliament

Why do we need a Parliament?

In a democracy the people are supreme. Their elected representatives sit in a national assembly called the Parliament at the national level (and Legislative Assembly at the state level). Parliament is the final authority for making laws. It is the symbol of the people’s will.

Functions of Parliament

  1. Law-making: makes new laws, changes or repeals old ones.
  2. Control over the Government: the government can take decisions only so long as it enjoys the support of the majority in the Lok Sabha. Parliament asks questions, debates, passes no-confidence motion.
  3. Financial control: the government cannot raise or spend money without Parliament’s approval – annual Budget is passed by it.
  4. Representation & debate: it is the highest forum of discussion on national issues, policies and laws.
  5. Electoral & judicial functions: elects the President and Vice-President (with state MLAs for the President), can impeach the President and judges.

Two Houses of Parliament

Large countries like India divide the role of Parliament into two houses (bicameral legislature) so that different regions and groups get representation and decisions are carefully reviewed.

Why is Lok Sabha more powerful?

  1. Ordinary law: both Houses have equal powers, but in case of disagreement the joint sitting is called, presided by the Speaker – the Lok Sabha (with more members) usually wins.
  2. Money bills: Lok Sabha alone decides; Rajya Sabha can only delay up to 14 days or suggest changes, which Lok Sabha can reject.
  3. Control over the Executive: only Lok Sabha can pass a no-confidence motion; the Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha. If the Lok Sabha passes it, the PM and all ministers must resign.

4. The Political Executive

Executive: a group of persons who have the authority to implement laws and run the government. Because they are in charge of day-to-day decisions, they are called the executive. When we say “the government” we usually mean the executive.

Political and Permanent Executive

Elected by the peopleAppointed through competitive exam (UPSC) – not elected
Temporary – for 5 years or till they have majorityPermanent – till retirement
Takes major decisionsImplements decisions, gives expert advice
PM, ministersSecretaries, IAS, IPS officers
Why is the political executive more powerful?
In a democracy the will of the people is supreme. The political executive is elected by and answerable to the people. Ministers are not expected to be experts, so they take the advice of civil servants – but the final decision is theirs.

Prime Minister and Council of Ministers

  • The Prime Minister is the most important political institution in the country. He is not directly elected by the people; the President appoints the leader of the majority party (or coalition) in the Lok Sabha.
  • There is no fixed term – he continues as long as he enjoys the majority support in the Lok Sabha.
  • The PM chooses the other ministers; the President appoints them on PM’s advice. They are usually MPs; a non-MP can become minister but must be elected within 6 months.
  • Council of Ministers = all ministers (60–80 in number).
Collective responsibility: All ministers are collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. They sink or swim together. If one minister loses the support of the House, the whole council must resign.

Powers of the Prime Minister

  • Chairs Cabinet meetings; coordinates the work of different departments.
  • His decisions are final when departments disagree.
  • Supervises all departments; distributes work to ministers; can dismiss ministers.
  • When the PM resigns, the whole Council of Ministers resigns.
Coalition governments: in recent decades, when no single party gets majority, the PM has to consult and accommodate coalition partners – so the PM’s power seems less. Also, the 91st Constitutional Amendment (2003) says the size of the Council of Ministers cannot exceed 15% of the Lok Sabha strength. The anti-defection law disqualifies an MP who leaves the party.

The President

  • Head of the State – highest formal authority; but the powers are nominal – exercised on the advice of the Council of Ministers.
  • Election: not directly by the people. By an Electoral College – elected MPs and elected MLAs (including Delhi & Puducherry) – by proportional representation, single transferable vote. Term: 5 years; can be re-elected. Minimum age: 35.

Powers of the President

TypePowers

Nominal vs Real executive: The President is the nominal executive; the PM and Council of Ministers are the real executive. The President can do almost nothing on his own – every major decision is taken by the PM and Cabinet in his name.

5. The Judiciary

All courts at the national, state and district levels together are called the judiciary. India has an integrated judicial system – the Supreme Court controls all the courts under it, and its decisions are binding on all.

Independence of the Judiciary

  • Judges are appointed by the President on the advice of the PM in consultation with the Chief Justice of India (in practice, the senior judges of the collegium have the major say).
  • Once appointed, it is very difficult to remove them – only by impeachment by Parliament with a special majority (2/3 of members present and voting, and majority of total strength).
  • No judge can be removed for giving judgments against the government.
  • Judges’ salaries and service conditions cannot be reduced to their disadvantage.

Powers of the Judiciary

  1. Dispute settlement between citizens, citizens and government, between states, between Union & states.
  2. Guardian of Fundamental Rights: any citizen can approach courts if rights are violated (writs).
  3. Judicial review: courts can examine the laws and actions of the executive; if unconstitutional (ultra vires) they can declare them invalid.
  4. Interpreting the Constitution: final interpreter of the Constitution.
  5. Public Interest Litigation (PIL): the courts allow any public-spirited person to file a case for the public good.
Supreme Court of India: highest court; its decisions are binding; retirement age of judges is 65 (High Court – 62). Supreme Court heard the Mandal case and many others related to the rights of citizens.

Q&A Section C – Short Answer (2–3 marks)

1. Why do we need political institutions?
  • Governments have to do many tasks – make laws, collect taxes, spend money, maintain order – and each needs a procedure and a responsible office.
  • Institutions have rules, so decisions are not left to one person’s wish.
  • They allow discussion, avoid hasty decisions and give continuity even when persons change.
  • Although procedures can cause delay, they give everyone a chance to be heard – this is the essence of democracy.
2. What was the Mandal Commission? What did it recommend?

It was the Second Backward Classes Commission set up in 1979 under B.P. Mandal. It studied the social and educational backwardness of castes and in 1980 recommended 27% reservation in central government jobs for the socially and educationally backward classes, in addition to the existing reservation for SC and ST.

3. Explain the role of the Supreme Court in the Mandal case.

Many people challenged the government order in the courts. The Supreme Court (Indra Sawhney case, 16 Nov 1992) upheld the 27% reservation but excluded the creamy layer and said total reservation should not exceed 50%. This shows the court’s power to examine government decisions.

4. Why is Lok Sabha considered more powerful than the Rajya Sabha?
  • Money bills are introduced and finally decided by the Lok Sabha; Rajya Sabha can only delay by 14 days.
  • The Council of Ministers is responsible only to the Lok Sabha.
  • In joint sittings the Lok Sabha’s larger numbers prevail.

6. Distinguish between political executive and permanent executive.
  • Political executive – elected by people, temporary, takes major decisions; e.g. PM, ministers.
  • Permanent executive – appointed through exam, stay till retirement, implement decisions and advise ministers; e.g. IAS officers.
7. Why is the political executive more powerful than the permanent executive?

In a democracy people are supreme. The political executive is elected by the people and answerable to Parliament. Ministers set policy, while the civil servants are experts who advise and implement. Experts should be ‘on tap, not on top’.

8. What is the difference between nominal and real executive?

The nominal executive (President) has formal powers; decisions are taken in his name. The real executive (PM and Council of Ministers) actually takes decisions and runs the administration.

9. Describe the composition of the Council of Ministers.

Cabinet Ministers (senior, about 25, decide policy), Ministers of State with Independent Charge (smaller ministries) and Ministers of State (assist cabinet ministers). Together they form the Council of Ministers headed by the PM; size ≤ 15% of the Lok Sabha.

10. What is collective responsibility?

The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. If the House passes a no-confidence motion against one minister or the government, all ministers must resign together.

11. How is the President elected?

By an electoral college of the elected MPs and the elected MLAs (including Delhi and Puducherry), through proportional representation with single transferable vote and secret ballot. Term 5 years.

12. What are the discretionary powers of the President?
  • He can return a bill once for reconsideration (not a money bill).
  • When no party or alliance gets a clear majority, he uses his discretion to appoint the PM.
13. How is the independence of the judiciary ensured?
  • Judges are appointed by the President after consultation with the CJI.
  • Removal only by impeachment with special majority in Parliament.
  • Judges’ salaries and conditions cannot be changed to their disadvantage.
  • Courts are not under the control of the executive or legislature.
14. What is Public Interest Litigation? Why is it important?

PIL allows any public-spirited person to approach the court for the public good, even by a letter. It helps the poor and the ignorant who cannot go to court, and makes the government accountable.

15. State any three functions of Parliament.
  1. Making laws
  2. Controlling the government and passing the budget
  3. Debating national issues; electing President; impeachment
16. What is judicial review?

Courts can examine any law or action of the executive. If it violates the Constitution the Supreme Court can declare it invalid. It protects fundamental rights and maintains the supremacy of the Constitution.

Q&A Section D – Long Answer (5 marks)

1. Describe how the Mandal Commission decision was taken and what it teaches about institutions.
  1. Background: Mandal Commission (1979) → report (1980): 27% reservation for backward classes.
  2. Cabinet decision – 6 August 1990 under PM V.P. Singh.
  3. Government Order – 13 August 1990 signed by a senior civil servant; implemented by the bureaucracy.
  4. Reactions: Debate in Parliament, protests, newspapers, and petitions in courts.
  5. Supreme Court verdict – 16 Nov 1992: 27% upheld; creamy layer excluded; 50% limit.

Lesson: Major decisions involve many institutions – the political executive takes the decision, civil servants implement, Parliament debates, and the judiciary examines. No one institution has unlimited power.

2. Explain the structure, powers and functions of the Parliament.

Structure: President + Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha (see Fig. 3).

Powers & functions:

  1. Law-making – final authority for making laws.
  2. Control over executive – questions, debates, no-confidence motion.
  3. Financial control – approval of Budget and taxation.
  4. Debates on national issues and representation of the people.
  5. Electoral and judicial – election of President / Vice-President, impeachment.

Why two houses? to represent regions, to have a second look at laws and to prevent hasty decisions. Lok Sabha is more powerful.

3. Explain in detail the position and powers of the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers.
  • Appointment: The President appoints the leader of the majority party/coalition in Lok Sabha as PM; other ministers are appointed on PM’s advice.
  • Categories: Cabinet ministers, Ministers of State (Independent Charge), Ministers of State. Size ≤ 15% of Lok Sabha.
  • Powers of PM: Chairs Cabinet; coordinates departments; final say in disputes; distributes work; supervises; can dismiss ministers. If PM resigns, the whole council resigns.
  • Collective responsibility to Lok Sabha.
  • Coalition politics has reduced PM’s absolute power and required consultation with allies.
4. Describe the position and powers of the President of India. Is he a real or nominal head?

The President is the head of the State. He is elected by an electoral college of elected MPs and MLAs for 5 years. His powers: executive (appoints PM, judges, governors etc.), legislative (assent to Bills; summons Parliament; ordinances), military (Supreme Commander), emergency & discretionary powers (return bills; choose PM when no majority).

But in practice he is only a nominal head; he acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers. The real power lies with the PM and Cabinet.

5. What is the Judiciary? Explain its independence and powers.

Judiciary – Supreme Court, High Courts, District & lower courts (integrated system).

Independence: Appointment by President with CJI’s consultation; removal only by impeachment; fixed service conditions; no control by executive.

Powers: dispute settlement; guardian of fundamental rights; judicial review; interpretation of Constitution; PIL – public justice. This makes the judiciary the guardian of the Constitution.

6. Explain with the help of a flow chart how a bill becomes a law.
  1. A bill is drafted and introduced in either House (Money Bill only in Lok Sabha).
  2. Debated in committees and in House; passed by majority.
  3. Sent to the other House, which also passes it (Rajya Sabha can only delay a money bill for 14 days).
  4. If disagreement – joint sitting presided by Speaker.
  5. The President gives assent – the Bill becomes an Act.

Flow: Bill → Lok Sabha → Rajya Sabha → President → Law (Fig. 4).

7. Write a note on political and permanent executive.

The political executive – elected leaders (PM, ministers) – takes major decisions, answerable to people. The permanent executive – civil servants appointed by UPSC – stays till retirement, implements decisions, gives advice. Ministers decide on policy, officers provide expertise. Even though the civil servants are experts, in a democracy the elected persons have the final say because they are accountable to the people.

8. ‘Parliament, Executive and Judiciary need each other.’ Explain.

Parliament makes laws and controls the government; the Executive implements; the Judiciary interprets and checks them. The Executive is answerable to Parliament; judges are appointed by the Executive; courts can strike down laws made by Parliament (judicial review). This system of checks and balances keeps any organ from becoming too powerful.

Higher Order / Case-based questions

1. A Minister’s order is opposed by his secretary on legal grounds. Who should take the final decision and why?
The Minister takes the final decision, since he is elected and answerable to Parliament; the secretary’s duty is to advise honestly. If the decision is illegal, the courts can still strike it down – showing checks and balances.
2. Suppose the Rajya Sabha rejects a Money Bill. What will happen?
Nothing much: the Lok Sabha can accept or reject the Rajya Sabha’s suggestions. If the Rajya Sabha does not return the bill within 14 days it is deemed passed. Hence the Rajya Sabha cannot stop a Money Bill.
3. A newspaper reports that a government school has no toilet for girls. A reader writes a letter to the High Court. Which feature of the judiciary helps here?
Public Interest Litigation (PIL). The court can treat the letter as a petition and direct the government to provide facilities.
4. Why can’t the President act on his own?
The Constitution requires him to act on the advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the PM, who is responsible to the Lok Sabha. The President is a symbol of unity; real power is with the elected representatives.
