Pastoralists in the Modern World

Class 9 | Social Science (History) | UP Board

1. Introduction

Pastoralists are people who rear animals (sheep, goats, cattle, camels) and move from place to place with their herds in search of pasture and water. Nomads are people who do not live in one place but move from one area to another to earn a living. In India and in Africa, millions of pastoral nomads still depend on herds. This chapter tells how colonial rule and modern changes affected them.

2. Pastoral Nomads and their Movements

(a) In the Mountains

  • Gujjar Bakarwals of Jammu and Kashmir: they spend winter in the dry scrub forests of the Siwalik range. By the end of April they begin the march north to summer pastures. They cross the Pir Panjal passes into the Kashmir valley, where the high meadows (bugyals) are green. By end of September they start the downward journey.
  • Gaddi shepherds of Himachal Pradesh: winter in the low hills of Siwalik; in April they move to Lahul and Spiti for summer; in September they return, stopping at villages to graze on harvested fields.
  • Bhotiyas, Sherpas and Kinnauris of Garhwal and Kumaun likewise move seasonally between low and high pastures (bugyals).

(b) On Plateaus, Plains and Deserts

  • Dhangars of Maharashtra: shepherds, blanket weavers and buffalo herders. During the monsoon they stay in the semi-arid central plateau, where their sheep graze. In October after the bajra harvest they move west to the Konkan; Konkani peasants welcomed their flocks because the sheep manured the fields. With the onset of monsoon they return to the plateau.
  • Gollas, Kurumas and Kurubas of Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh: reared sheep and goats and sold woollen blankets; move between the dry central plateau and the coast.
  • Banjaras of UP, Punjab, Rajasthan, MP and Maharashtra: travelling traders of grain and cattle; they moved long distances.
  • Raikas of Rajasthan: because rainfall is scarce, they combine cultivation with herding of camels, sheep and goats. In monsoon, they stay in their villages of Barmer, Jaisalmer, Jodhpur and Bikaner; after harvest they leave with herds and return with the next monsoon.

3. Colonial Rule and Pastoral Life

  • Waste Land Rules: from the mid-19th century, the colonial state took uncultivated land (which was grazing land) and gave it to select people as farms, cutting pastureland.
  • Forest Acts: reserved forests (deodar, sal) were closed to pastoralists. In protected forests they got permits, with limits on days and routes. Forest Department decided when they could enter and leave.
  • Criminal Tribes Act, 1871: many nomadic communities, craftsmen and traders were notified as "criminal by nature"; they were forced to live in notified village settlements and could not move without a permit; police supervised them.
  • Grazing Tax: introduced from mid-19th century; tax per head of cattle was collected; the rate rose steadily. Pastoralists carried passes and paid at entry points.

How Did These Changes Affect Pastoralists?

Grazing lands shrank, remaining pastures were over-grazed, animal quality fell, and many herds died of starvation, especially in droughts. Pastoralists tried to cope: they reduced the number of cattle, found new pastures, some like the Raikas shifted their routes (Rajasthan to Haryana), some rich pastoralists bought land and settled, while poor ones borrowed money and became labourers. After 1947 the India-Pakistan border blocked the Raikas' movement into Sindh, where they had grazed earlier.

4. Pastoral Life in Africa: The Maasai

Over half of the world's pastoralists live in Africa (Bedouins, Berbers, Maasai, Somali, Borana, Turkana). The Maasai of East Africa (about 300,000 in southern Kenya and 150,000 in northern Tanzania) herd cattle, sheep and goats and live on milk and meat.

Loss of Maasai Grazing Lands

  • Before colonialism, Maasai land extended from northern Kenya to the steppes of northern Tanzania.
  • In 1885, after the Scramble for Africa, Maasailand was cut in two by an international boundary between British Kenya and German Tanganyika.
  • The best grazing lands were taken for white settlement and, later, for game reserves (Maasai Mara in Kenya, Serengeti in Tanzania), where Maasai were not allowed to enter or graze. Maasai were pushed into semi-arid areas; they lost about 60% of their land.
  • The colonial state forced them to remain in reserves and restricted movement; they needed permits; they were taxed.

Droughts and Social Change

  • In a severe drought of 1933 and 1934 more than half the Maasai cattle in Kenya died.
  • Maasai society had two groups: elders (who ruled) and warriors (who protected the tribe). The British appointed chiefs who gathered wealth and bought animals. Poor Maasai had to work in towns as charcoal burners, labourers and in odd jobs. A new division between rich and poor developed (also between elders and warriors, and between men and women).

5. Common Themes

In both India and Africa colonial governments restricted movement, reduced pastures and taxed animals. Yet pastoralists adapted: they changed routes and herd size, took up other work or fought for rights. Pastoralism remains a sustainable way of using dry lands and deserves recognition.

Short Q&A (3 marks)

Q1. Why do pastoralists move seasonally?
Grass on one pasture finishes after some weeks; therefore they move with the season, using high meadows in summer and lowlands in winter so that land can recover and herds always have fodder.
Q2. What was the Criminal Tribes Act?
An 1871 law notifying many nomadic communities as "criminal by birth"; they were forced to live in notified settlements, needed passes to leave and were watched by police.
Q3. How did Forest Acts hurt pastoralists?
Reserved forests were closed, protected forests needed permits, the days and routes were limited and they were fined for exceeding them.
Q4. What was the grazing tax?
A tax on each head of cattle taken to grazing lands; pastoralists carried passes and paid at entry points; rates kept rising.
Q5. How did the Maasai lose land?
In 1885 Maasailand was divided by British Kenya and German Tanganyika; the best land went to white settlers and game reserves, pushing Maasai to dry areas.

Long Q&A (5 marks)

Q1. Describe the movement of pastoral groups in the mountains of India.
Gujjar Bakarwals spend winter in the Siwalik scrub forests and in April-May move through Pir Panjal passes to Kashmir's meadows; they return by September. Gaddis winter in Siwalik and summer in Lahul and Spiti. Bhotiyas and Kinnauris of Garhwal and Kumaun follow similar patterns. This keeps pastures from being overgrazed.
Q2. How did colonial rule affect pastoralists in India?
Waste Land Rules turned pastures into farms; Forest Acts closed forests and regulated entry; the Criminal Tribes Act restricted nomads; the grazing tax took money. Pastures shrank, over-grazing followed and animals starved. Pastoralists reduced herds, changed routes, took loans or became labourers; after 1947 borders blocked old routes.
Q3. Explain how colonial rule affected Maasai pastoralists.
The 1885 division of Maasailand, land lost to settlers and game reserves (Maasai Mara, Serengeti), movement restrictions and taxes reduced grazing. The 1933-34 drought killed half of the cattle. British-appointed chiefs became rich while poor Maasai became labourers, creating new inequality.
Q4. How did pastoralists in Rajasthan and Maharashtra cope with changes?
Raikas combined farming with herding and moved camel and sheep herds, shifting to new pastures in Haryana; Dhangars moved between plateau and Konkan with seasons and exchanged manure for grain; some bought land, some turned to trade or labour.
