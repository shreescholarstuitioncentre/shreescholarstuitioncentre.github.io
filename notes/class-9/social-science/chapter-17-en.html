The Story of Village Palampur
Class 9 Social Science – Economics (Chapter 1)

1. Introduction

Palampur is a fictional village in western Uttar Pradesh, well connected: roads link it to the village Raiganj and the small town Shahpur, with bullock carts, tongas, buggies, motorised trucks and jeeps on them. It has 450 families of several castes, two primary schools, one high school, a primary health centre and a private dispensary. Most houses have electricity; tubewells irrigate the fields. The chapter uses it to explain production (making goods and services) and factors of production.

Caste structure: 80 upper-caste families own most of the land with large brick-and-cement houses. Dalits are about one-third of the population; they live in one corner in small mud-and-straw houses, and most have no electricity.

2. Diagram – Four Requirements of Production

Land (natural resources) · Labour (physical effort) · Physical capital (fixed: tools, machines, buildings; working: raw material + money in hand) · Human capital – knowledge & enterprise to combine the first three and produce output.

3. Farming in Palampur

Main activity: farming – 75% of people depend on it (farmers and farm labourers). Only 25% do non-farm work.

Land is fixed: India's cultivated area has hardly increased since 1960; Palampur's is about 226 hectares and no new land can be added. So output must rise from the same land by two methods:

  • Multiple cropping: growing more than one crop on the same land in a year. In Palampur: kharif – jowar and bajra (animal fodder); then potato; rabi – wheat; part of the land has sugarcane (harvested once a year). Possible due to irrigation.
  • Modern farming methods: the Green Revolution (late 1960s) brought HYV (High Yielding Variety) seeds, chemical fertilisers, pesticides, tubewells and farm machines. Wheat yield rose from 1300 kg/ha (traditional seeds) to 3200 kg/ha (HYV), so farmers produced a surplus and sold it at Raiganj.

Drawbacks of modern farming: chemical fertilisers reduce soil fertility; over-use of tubewells lowers the groundwater level. Land must be protected for future generations (sustainability). Earlier, Persian wheels irrigated small areas; the state government built the first tubewells; electric tubewells followed.

4. Who Shares the Land?

Land is unequally distributed: 150 families (mostly Dalits) have no land and work as labourers; 240 families cultivate small plots under 2 hectares – not enough to support a family, so they also work on others' farms; 60 medium and large farmers cultivate more than 2 hectares, with some owning 10 ha or more, and they sell surplus.

5. Labour and Capital

Labour: small farmers and landless labourers provide it. Farm labourers like Dala and Ramkali are paid in cash or kind; they face competition for work, so wages are lower than the minimum wage (Rs 115/day in the textbook). The government must ensure minimum wages.

Capital: Medium/large farmers use their own savings from surplus. Small farmers must borrow from large farmers or moneylenders at high interest. Example: Savita borrowed Rs 3000 from Tejpal Singh at 24% interest for four months and had to work on his farm as a labourer at low wages – a debt trap.

6. Non-Farm Activities

  • Dairy: villagers feed buffaloes on jowar and bajra; milk is sold in Raiganj and Shahpur.
  • Small-scale manufacturing: Mishrilal uses an electric sugarcane crusher to make gur and sells it to traders; needs little capital and few workers.
  • Shopkeeping: about 25 small shops sell rice, wheat, sugar, tea, oil, biscuits, soap, toothpaste, copybooks etc.
  • Transport: rickshaws, tongas, bullock carts, jeeps, trucks; the number of people in transport has grown with better roads.
  • Services: Kareem runs a computer-class centre for students.

9. Short Questions

Q. What is production?Creation of goods and services using land, labour, capital and knowledge.
Q. Why can't more land be added in Palampur?Land area is fixed (226 ha) and all of it is already cultivated, so output must grow by multiple cropping and modern methods.
Q. Distinguish fixed and working capital.Fixed capital (tools, machines, buildings) lasts many years; working capital (raw material, money) is used up in each production cycle.
Q. Why do small farmers borrow?Their land gives little surplus and no savings, so they borrow for seeds, fertiliser and water at high interest.
Q. Name non-farm activities of Palampur.Dairy, gur making, shopkeeping, transport and computer classes.

10. Long Questions

Q. Explain the Green Revolution and its effects on Palampur.Late 1960s: HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation raised wheat output from 1300 to 3200 kg/ha, giving farmers surplus income and enabling multiple cropping. But fertilisers harmed soil and tubewells lowered groundwater, so environmental care is needed.
Q. How are land and capital distributed unequally in Palampur?150 families are landless, 240 hold under 2 ha and only 60 hold larger plots. Big farmers use their savings; small farmers borrow at high interest, lose income and sometimes work for the lender – showing inequality in village life.
