Class 9 Social Science – Electoral Politics

Democratic Politics–I (Civics) · UP Board · Notes with diagrams, Q&A and MCQs

1. Why Do We Need Elections?

In a large country all people cannot rule directly. So they choose representatives through elections. Representatives make laws, form the government and take decisions. Elections allow people to choose who will rule, which party’s policies to follow, and what laws to make. Without elections, a democracy cannot work.

Remember: Election = a mechanism by which people choose their representatives at regular intervals and change them if they wish.

2. What Makes an Election Democratic?

  • Everyone should be able to choose: one person, one vote, one value (universal adult franchise).
  • There must be something to choose from: parties and candidates free to contest and offer real alternatives.
  • Elections must be held regularly, after every few years.
  • The candidate preferred by the people should actually get elected.
  • Elections should be free and fair, so people can choose as they really wish.

Example: in the 1987 Haryana Assembly election, the Lok Dal promised to waive the loans of small farmers. It won and kept the promise, showing how elections make rulers answerable.

3. Is Competition Good?

  • Demerits: factionalism, disunity, mud-slinging, unfair methods, and the pressure to win may push good long-term policies aside.
  • Merits: it works like a reward and punishment system. Rulers know that if they do not work they will lose the next election; parties raise public issues; it gives regular feedback.

4. Our System of Elections

Fig 1: Steps of an election (animated)
  • Regular elections: Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabha elections every five years – a general election. If a seat falls vacant, a by-election is held.
  • Constituencies: the country is divided into areas; each elects one representative. Lok Sabha has 543 constituencies; each state is divided into assembly constituencies. Villages and towns have wards for local bodies.
Fig 2: Reserved and general seats in the Lok Sabha

Reserved constituencies

The makers of the Constitution wanted weaker sections to get a real chance. 84 seats in the Lok Sabha are reserved for Scheduled Castes and 47 for Scheduled Tribes; only they can contest there, though all voters vote. In local bodies, seats are reserved for women and for OBCs.

Voters’ list (electoral roll)

Every citizen aged 18 and above can vote. The list of eligible voters is prepared and updated by the government. Voters carry an Election Photo Identity Card (EPIC); names should be registered before the election.

Nomination of candidates

Anyone who can vote can contest (minimum age 25 for Lok Sabha and Assembly). Most contest with a party ticket. A candidate files a nomination with a security deposit and a legal declaration giving criminal cases pending, assets and liabilities, and educational qualifications, so voters can judge.

Election campaign

Parties and candidates convince voters through rallies, posters, door-to-door visits and the media, mainly in the two weeks before polling. The Model Code of Conduct says parties cannot: bribe or threaten voters; appeal in the name of caste or religion; use government vehicles, buildings or officials; announce new schemes after elections are declared.

Polling and counting

Voters go to a polling booth, get an ink mark on the finger and press a button on the Electronic Voting Machine (EVM) next to their candidate’s name and symbol. Agents of candidates watch. On the fixed counting day votes are counted; the candidate with the highest votes in a constituency wins; the party with a majority forms the government.

5. Election Commission

An independent body that conducts elections, from preparing the voters’ list to declaring results. The Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) is appointed by the President but, once appointed, cannot be removed easily by the government. The EC can order a re-poll, postpone elections, and tell the government to transfer officials in case of unfair practices. Courts also stay independent.

6. Popular Participation

Voter turnout is the percentage of eligible voters who actually vote. In India the poor, illiterate and underprivileged vote in the same or higher numbers than the rich and educated. People feel their vote matters; participation in campaigns and in election work has increased.

7. Challenges to Free and Fair Elections

  • Candidates and parties with a lot of money have an advantage.
  • Candidates with criminal connections may use muscle power.
  • Tickets often go to family members of leaders; ordinary persons find it hard.
  • Voters often have little choice between major parties; smaller parties and independents are disadvantaged.

Despite these, parties accept results and ruling parties often lose, showing that elections are largely free and fair; the EC, courts, media and citizens must keep vigilant.

Q1. What is an election?

A mechanism by which people choose their representatives at regular intervals.

Q2. What is a by-election?

An election held to fill a seat that falls vacant between general elections.

Q3. How many constituencies does the Lok Sabha have?

543.

Q4. What is a reserved constituency?

A constituency where only candidates of SC or ST can contest.

Q5. What is the minimum voting age?

18 years.

Q6. What is EVM?

Electronic Voting Machine used to cast and record votes.

Q7. What is the Model Code of Conduct?

Rules that parties and candidates must follow during elections.

Q8. Who appoints the Chief Election Commissioner?

The President of India.

9. Long Q&A

L1. Why are elections necessary in a democracy?

In big countries people cannot rule directly, so elections let them choose representatives who make laws and run the government. Elections hold rulers accountable and allow peaceful change.

L2. What are the minimum conditions of a democratic election?

Everyone has one vote of equal value; parties and candidates are free to contest and offer choice; elections are held regularly; people’s choice wins; and the election is free and fair.

L3. Describe the process of elections in India.

Constituencies are fixed; the voters’ list is updated; candidates file nominations with declarations; campaigns follow the Model Code; voters vote at polling booths using EVMs; votes are counted and the winner declared; the government is formed.

L4. Write a note on the Election Commission.

It is an independent body that supervises everything from the voters’ list to results, can order a re-poll, and can punish violations of the Model Code. The CEC cannot be easily removed, which keeps it independent.

L5. Explain the challenges to free and fair elections.

Money power, criminals, dynasty politics and limited choice for voters are the main problems. Still, public participation, an active media and a strong Election Commission help keep elections meaningful.
