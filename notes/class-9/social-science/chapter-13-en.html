Class 9 Social Science – Constitutional Design

Democratic Politics–I (Civics) · UP Board · Notes with diagrams, Q&A and MCQs

1. Why Do We Need a Constitution?

A constitution is a set of basic rules and principles by which a country is governed. It creates trust and coordination among people, says how rulers are chosen, what powers they have, what rights citizens enjoy, and what goals society should achieve. It limits the power of rulers so they cannot misuse it.

Remember: Constitutional design – how people of a country decide the rules by which they will live together. Two cases: South Africa and India.

2. Democratic Constitution in South Africa

  • Apartheid: a system of racial discrimination unique to South Africa, enforced by the white minority government from 1948. People were divided by skin colour (white, black, coloured, Indians); blacks could not vote, live or work where whites did, and were barred from many places; the majority black population suffered most.
  • The African National Congress (ANC) led the struggle with protests and strikes. Many leaders were jailed; Nelson Mandela spent 28 years in prison, mostly on Robben Island.
  • By the early 1990s the government ended apartheid and the ban on ANC. On 26 April 1994 the new flag of the Republic was raised and a multi-racial government came to power; Mandela became the first President.
  • The two sides negotiated. Instead of revenge, they chose reconciliation: “Never again”. A new constitution (adopted in 1996) gave equal rights to all races, and equality of men and women. South Africa is called the Rainbow Nation.
Lesson: Even bitter enemies can agree on a democratic constitution through trust, compromise and respect for rights.

3. The Making of the Indian Constitution

It was a difficult time: India was just independent, partitioned, with princely states to be integrated, and had deep diversity of language, religion and caste.

Fig 1: Timeline of the Constitution (animated)
  • Constituent Assembly: a body of people’s representatives that writes a constitution. Elected indirectly (by provincial assemblies) in July 1946; first met on 9 December 1946.
  • It had 389 members, reduced to 299 after Partition (a separate Assembly for Pakistan). Dr. Rajendra Prasad was its President; Dr. B. R. Ambedkar chaired the Drafting Committee.
  • It worked for 2 years, 11 months and 18 days, with about 114 days spent on debate; every word is recorded as the Constituent Assembly Debates.
  • Adopted on 26 November 1949; came into force on 26 January 1950, celebrated as Republic Day.

Why do we accept the Constitution?

  • It does not reflect the views of its members alone; it is a consensus reached through debate.
  • The Assembly represented the people of its time (all regions, languages, castes, religions) even though adult franchise did not exist.
  • It worked in an open, systematic and consensual manner, with debates and written records.

4. Guiding Philosophy

The ideas came from the freedom struggle: the 1928 draft by Motilal Nehru and others, the Karachi resolution (1931), and leaders like Gandhi, Nehru, Ambedkar, Patel, Maulana Azad. Nehru’s Objectives Resolution (22 Jan 1947) laid down aims; his “Tryst with Destiny” speech came at midnight on 14–15 August 1947. These ideas live in the Preamble.

5. The Preamble

Fig 2: Values and aims of the Preamble (animated)
  • We, the people of India: the Constitution is made by and for the people; power rests with them.
  • Sovereign: the people have supreme right to make decisions on internal and external matters; no outside power can dictate.
  • Socialist: wealth is socially generated and should be shared equally; the state reduces inequality. (Added in 1976.)
  • Secular: all citizens are free to follow any religion; the state has no official religion and treats all alike. (Added in 1976.)
  • Democratic: people enjoy equal political rights, elect rulers and hold them accountable; the government is bound by rules.
  • Republic: the head of state is an elected person, not a hereditary position.
  • Justice – social, economic and political; Liberty – of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; Equality – of status and opportunity; Fraternity – assuring dignity of the individual and unity and integrity of the nation.

6. Institutional Design

A constitution is not only values; it also sets up institutions (the legislature, executive, judiciary, Election Commission) and rules for them. It is a framework to give effect to the Preamble; it can be amended through a due process, with changes suited to time.

8. Long Q&A

L1. Describe how South Africa made a democratic constitution.

After decades of apartheid and struggle led by the ANC and Mandela, the white government agreed to end discrimination. In 1994 the first multi-racial election was held. Former opponents chose reconciliation instead of revenge, negotiated, and drew up a constitution giving equal rights to all races and genders. It shows trust and compromise can build democracy.

L2. Why was the Indian Constituent Assembly formed and how did it work?

Free India needed a basic law. Its members, chosen in 1946, met first on 9 December 1946. They debated openly, formed committees, recorded every word and decided by consensus, finishing in about 3 years.

L3. Why do we accept the Indian Constitution even today?

It is not just the views of its makers but a consensus; the Assembly represented the people and different groups; and it worked openly and systematically. Its values still guide us.

L4. Explain the keywords of the Preamble.

Sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic and republic describe what India is; justice, liberty, equality and fraternity are what it aims at. Each term is explained above.

L5. What is the significance of the Preamble?

It states the philosophy, values and goals of the Constitution, guides the interpretation of other provisions and is regarded as the soul of the Constitution.
