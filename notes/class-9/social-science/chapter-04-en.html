Forest Society and Colonialism

Class 9 | Social Science (History) | UP Board

1. Why Deforestation?

Deforestation is the disappearance of forests. It began centuries ago, but under colonial rule it became far more systematic and extensive.

(a) Land to be Improved

  • In 1600 about one-sixth of India's land was cultivated; today it is nearly half.
  • Population growth raised food demand, so farms expanded into forests.
  • The British saw forests as unproductive wasteland. Cultivation raised revenue, so colonial rulers encouraged commercial crops (jute, sugar, wheat, cotton) for Britain's industries. Between 1880 and 1920, cultivated area rose by 6.7 million hectares.

(b) Sleepers on the Tracks

From the 1850s railways were needed for trade and moving troops. Wooden sleepers were used to hold the rails; each mile of track needed 1,760-2,000 sleepers. By 1890 about 25,500 km of track was laid and by 1946 over 765,000 km. In the Madras Presidency alone about 35,000 trees were felled each year. Trees of the forests near the lines were cut and contractors got rich.

(c) Shipbuilding and Plantations

  • England's own oak forests were depleted, so the Royal Navy needed timber (teak, sal) from India for its ships.
  • Plantations of tea, coffee and rubber were set up on huge cleared forest lands.

2. The Rise of Commercial Forestry

The British needed timber for ships and railways but feared forests would vanish. In 1864 they appointed Dietrich Brandis, a German expert, as the first Inspector General of Forests and set up the Indian Forest Service. He introduced a system for scientific forestry, which meant: clearing mixed natural forests, planting trees of one kind (teak, sal) in straight rows, and fixing rules for who could use the forest. The Imperial Forest Research Institute was established at Dehradun in 1906.

Forest Acts

  • Indian Forest Act, 1865: gave the government power to declare any land a forest and make rules; amended in 1878 and 1927.
  • The 1878 Act divided forests into three categories: Reserved forests (best timber; villagers could take nothing), Protected forests (limited rights) and Village forests (for community needs).

3. How Were the Lives of People Affected?

Villagers used forests for fuel, fodder, fruits, roots, tubers, leaves for plates, herbs, bamboo, honey and grazing. The new laws banned these, so people had to steal wood and risk punishment, harassment by forest guards and bribes.

  • Shifting cultivation (swidden): parts of forest were cut and burnt, seeds sown after the monsoon, harvested by October-November, then left fallow for 12-18 years. Called jhum (north-east), podu (Andhra), bewar, penda etc. The British banned it as it was hard to tax and made trees unsuitable for timber, and displaced many communities.
  • Hunting: locals were prohibited but the British hunted for sport; bounties were paid to kill tigers, wolves, leopards. Tigers were hunted by maharajas and officers; the tiger population fell sharply.
  • Nomadic communities (Korava, Karacha, Yerukula) were declared criminal tribes and forced to work in factories, mines and plantations.
  • Forest villages were created: people got cheap labour jobs.
  • Trade in forest products was taken over by British trading firms.

4. Rebellion in the Forest: The Bastar Revolt (1910)

Bastar lies in southern Chhattisgarh and is home to Maria and Muria Gonds, Dhurwas, Bhatras and Halbas. Each village gave a small contribution to the earth, so people believed the land belonged to the earth and they were guardians.

  • In 1905 the colonial government proposed to reserve two-thirds of the forest, ban shifting cultivation, hunting and collection of produce. Villages were displaced, forced to work free on roads and forest.
  • Droughts and famines (1899-1900, 1907-08) made it worse. The Dhurwas of the Kanger forest were the first to protest.
  • The people of Bastar met, circulated mango boughs, lumps of earth, chillies and arrows as messages, and in 1910 rose in rebellion under Gunda Dhur. They looted bazaars, burnt houses of officials and schools, grain redistributed.
  • The British sent troops; adivasi leaders were flogged. It took 3 months to suppress the revolt, but reservation was suspended and the area to be reserved was halved.

5. Forest Transformations in Java

Java is a rice-producing island with teak forests. The Dutch colonisers controlled the forests as they did in India. The Kalangs, skilled forest cutters, were forced to work under the Dutch. In 1882 the Dutch allowed forest villagers free land if they worked on cutting trees and transporting timber (blandongdiensten). In 1890, Surontiko Samin of Randublatung village argued that the forest was not state property; the Samin movement refused to pay taxes and work. Later, in World War II Japanese occupation, Dutch burnt sawmills (scorched-earth) and the Japanese cut forests for war; after independence the forest department took over the Dutch control.

6. New Developments in Forestry

Since the 1980s, many governments recognise that scientific forestry ignored local needs. Now Joint Forest Management and participatory forestry involve local communities; sacred groves (e.g. in Mizoram and Rajasthan) are being respected. Local knowledge is valuable for biodiversity.

Dehradun (set up in 1906).

Skilled forest cutters of Java.

Short Q&A (3 marks)

Q1. Why did railways cause deforestation?
Railways needed wooden sleepers and fuel; each mile of track needed over 1,700 sleepers, so large numbers of trees were cut; in Madras alone about 35,000 trees were cut a year.
Q2. What is scientific forestry?
A system in which natural forests were cleared and trees of a single kind planted in straight rows, managed by experts for timber production.
Q3. Name three categories of forests under the 1878 Act.
Reserved forests (no use by villagers), Protected forests (limited rights) and Village forests (community use).
Q4. Why did the British ban shifting cultivation?
They felt it destroyed trees, made forests unsuitable for railway timber, risked fire, and made tax collection difficult.
Q5. What was the Samin movement?
A protest by Surontiko Samin and followers in Java (1890) claiming that the state did not own the forest; they refused taxes and labour.

Long Q&A (5 marks)

Q1. Explain the reasons for deforestation in colonial India.
The British expanded cultivation for commercial crops, clearing forests; between 1880 and 1920 cultivated land rose by 6.7 million hectares. Railways needed sleepers, shipbuilding needed teak and sal, and tea, coffee and rubber plantations occupied forest land. Commercial forestry replaced mixed forests with single-species plantations.
Q2. How did forest laws affect the lives of villagers?
Reserved forests barred villagers from collecting fuel, fodder and fruits. Shifting cultivation was banned, hunting prohibited and villagers were displaced. They were forced into cheap labour in forest villages and risked fines from forest guards. Nomadic groups were called criminal tribes, and trade passed to British firms.
Q3. Describe the Bastar revolt of 1910.
After the 1905 plan to reserve two-thirds of Bastar's forests, people lost rights and were forced to work free. Droughts worsened the burden. In 1910 people circulated mango boughs, earth, chillies and arrows, looted bazaars, and attacked officials under Gunda Dhur. The British crushed the revolt but halved the area to be reserved.
Q4. Describe the forest changes in Java under the Dutch.
The Dutch controlled teak forests and made the Kalangs work for them. Blandongdiensten gave villagers land in return for work. Samin's protest in 1890 questioned state ownership. In World War II the Dutch burned sawmills and the Japanese cut forests for war; later forest departments took over.
