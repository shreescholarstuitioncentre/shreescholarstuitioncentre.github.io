Democratic Rights
Class 9 Social Science – Civics (Chapter 6)

1. Life Without Rights – Three Cases

  • Guantanamo Bay (USA): After 9/11, the US army kept about 600 people from 30+ countries in a prison on Cuban land. They had no trial, no court access, no family contact. Amnesty International reported torture and the UN called it illegal.
  • Saudi Arabia: Hereditary king; no free elections. Women have limited rights; Muslims only are citizens; no freedom of religion for others; freedom of speech/press is restricted.
  • Kosovo (Yugoslavia): Ethnic Albanians were a majority, but the Serb government persecuted them. An Albanian family's life was destroyed by Serb soldiers – shows majority/minority rights must be protected.

2. Rights in a Democracy

Right = a claim of a person over other people, society and government. Conditions: it must be recognised by society, sanctioned by law, and must not harm others.

Why needed? (i) They protect individuals from the majority and the government. (ii) They ensure free and fair elections. (iii) They guard minorities. (iv) Rights are enforceable by courts – that's what separates them from mere wishes. Rights are given in the Constitution as Fundamental Rights (Part III).

4. Six Fundamental Rights in Detail

(1) Right to Equality (Art 14–18): Equality before law; no discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth; equal access to shops, wells, roads, hotels; equal opportunity in public jobs (reservation for SC/ST/OBC is allowed); untouchability abolished (Art 17); titles like Sir/Rai Bahadur abolished (Art 18).

(2) Right to Freedom (Art 19–22): Speech & expression, peaceful assembly, forming associations, moving freely, residing anywhere in India, any profession/trade. Also Right to Life & Personal Liberty (Art 21). Protection against arbitrary arrest (Art 22): must be told the reason, produced before a magistrate within 24 hours, can consult a lawyer. Freedom has reasonable restrictions (e.g., no speech inciting violence).

(3) Right against Exploitation (Art 23–24): Ban on trafficking, forced labour (begar), bonded labour, and employing children below 14 in factories/mines/hazardous work.

(4) Right to Freedom of Religion (Art 25–28): Every person may profess, practise and propagate any religion. India is secular: no state religion; no religious instruction in govt schools; no one forced to pay taxes for a religion. Government may regulate for public order, health, morality.

(5) Cultural & Educational Rights (Art 29–30): Minorities may conserve their language and culture and set up educational institutions of their choice; the state can't discriminate in aid.

(6) Right to Constitutional Remedies (Art 32): Move the Supreme Court/High Court if a right is violated. Dr B.R. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. Courts issue writs: Habeas Corpus (produce the detained person), Mandamus (do your duty), Prohibition (stop a lower court), Certiorari (quash a lower court order), Quo Warranto (by what authority?). Also PIL – Public Interest Litigation by any citizen for public cause.

5. Expanding Scope of Rights

Rights grow through court judgements and laws: Right to Education (Art 21A, 86th Amendment 2002) for ages 6–14; Right to Information (RTI 2005); Right to Food; Right to Privacy. Rights also come from international treaties (Universal Declaration of Human Rights) and constitutions of other nations (South Africa).

National Human Rights Commission (NHRC, 1993): independent body, appointed by the President; can investigate violations, recommend action and compensation, visit prisons – but cannot punish; it can't take up cases itself in courts. Amnesty International is a global NGO for human rights.

8. Short Questions

Q. What is a Fundamental Right?Rights in Part III of the Constitution that are guaranteed to all citizens, enforceable by courts, and cannot be taken away by the government.
Q. What does the Right against Exploitation prohibit?Trafficking, begar/forced labour, bonded labour, and child labour below 14 in hazardous jobs.
Q. What is Habeas Corpus?A writ ordering authorities to produce a detained person before the court so it can check whether the detention is legal.
Q. Why is Right to Constitutional Remedies important?Without it other rights would be only on paper; it lets citizens go to court to enforce them.
Q. What is PIL?Public Interest Litigation – anyone can approach a court for the benefit of the public or of those who cannot do so themselves.

9. Long Questions

Q. Explain the Right to Freedom with its restrictions.Art 19 gives six freedoms: speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession. Art 21 protects life and liberty; Art 22 protects against arbitrary arrest (reason, lawyer, magistrate in 24 hours). However freedoms are not absolute: reasonable restrictions apply for public order, security of the state, decency and to protect others' rights. Example: free speech doesn't allow inciting violence.
Q. How do courts and the NHRC protect rights?Courts (Art 32 & 226) issue writs, can strike down laws violating rights, and entertain PILs. NHRC investigates complaints, inspects jails, and recommends compensation/prosecution, but cannot punish. Together with RTI and media they make rights real.
