Chapter 3: Nazism and the Rise of Hitler

Class 9 | Social Science (History) | UP Board

1. Birth of the Weimar Republic

Germany fought the First World War (1914-18) with Austria-Hungary against the Allies (England, France, Russia and later the USA). After early gains it was defeated in November 1918. Kaiser Wilhelm II fled. A National Assembly met at Weimar and framed a democratic, federal constitution. This was the Weimar Republic. Many Germans blamed this new republic for the defeat and the humiliating peace.

The Treaty of Versailles (1919)

  • Germany lost all overseas colonies, about 10% of population, 13% of land, 75% of iron and 26% of coal to France, Poland, Denmark and Lithuania.
  • The Allies demilitarised Germany and occupied the Rhineland.
  • War Guilt Clause: Germany accepted blame; had to pay 6 billion pounds as reparations.
  • Germans saw it as a national humiliation and called the Weimar leaders the "November Criminals".

Political Radicalisation and Economic Crises

  • The war changed Europe: from creditor to debtor, Germany had huge debts. Fear of communist revolution (Spartacists, 1919) frightened the middle class, who turned to the right.
  • Hyperinflation 1923: Germany could not pay reparations; France occupied the Ruhr (iron and coal region). Germany printed paper money; the mark lost value. In April 1923 one US dollar = 24,000 marks; by December 1923 about 98,860,000 marks. People carried money in cartloads; savings were wiped out, hitting the middle class. The Dawes Plan (1924) later reduced the burden.
  • Great Depression (1929): after the Wall Street crash in the USA, American loans stopped. By 1932 industrial production was 40% of 1929; six million were unemployed. Youth turned to crime; the middle class feared proletarianisation; women worried about families.

2. Hitler's Rise to Power

  • Adolf Hitler was born in Austria in 1889, lived in poverty, served in WWI and developed hatred towards Jews and communists. He joined the German Workers' Party (1919), which became the National Socialist German Workers' Party (Nazi Party). It used the swastika and held rallies with the SA (Storm Troopers).
  • 1923: attempt to seize power in Munich (Beer Hall Putsch) failed; Hitler was tried for treason, then released. He wrote Mein Kampf (his ideology).
  • The Nazi party gained little till the Depression; then Hitler promised work, national pride and revenge for Versailles, and brilliant propaganda (rallies, posters, speeches, slogans) won votes: 2.6% (1928) → 18% (1930) → 37% (1932), becoming the largest party.
  • President Hindenburg appointed Hitler Chancellor on 30 January 1933.

3. Establishment of the Nazi Dictatorship

  • Fire Decree (28 Feb 1933): after the Reichstag fire, civil rights like free speech, press and assembly were suspended.
  • Enabling Act (3 March 1933): gave Hitler power to rule by decree and sideline parliament. Communists were jailed; other parties and trade unions banned.
  • Economy was handed to Hjalmar Schacht, who aimed at full employment through public works (e.g. Autobahn roads, Volkswagen).
  • Special forces: Gestapo (secret state police), SS (protection squads), Criminal Police and SD (security service) with extra-constitutional powers; people were jailed without trial in concentration camps.
  • Foreign policy: left the League of Nations (1933), reoccupied Rhineland (1936), merged Austria (1938), invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, beginning the Second World War.

4. The Nazi Worldview

Nazi ideology rested on racial hierarchy. Aryans (blond, blue-eyed Nordic Germans) were claimed the "master race"; Jews were placed at the bottom. Hitler applied Darwin's idea of "survival of the fittest" to races. He also promoted Lebensraum (living space): Germany must expand east to gain land and resources.

5. Establishment of the Racial State and Genocide

  • Undesirables (Jews, Gypsies, disabled people, Blacks, Russians, Poles, homosexuals) were persecuted; the physically and mentally disabled were killed in the euthanasia programme.
  • Nuremberg Laws (1935): took citizenship from Jews, banned marriage with Germans. Kristallnacht (9-10 Nov 1938): Jewish shops, homes and synagogues were smashed.
  • From 1939 Jews were herded into ghettos and later transported to gas chambers at camps like Auschwitz. Around 6 million Jews were murdered: the Holocaust ("Final Solution").
  • Nuremberg Trials (1945-46) tried Nazi leaders for crimes against humanity.

6. Youth and Women in Nazi Germany

  • Children were taught Nazi ideas in schools: Jews were removed from teaching; textbooks rewritten; boys trained for war, girls to be mothers. Hitler Youth (boys 14-18) pledged loyalty.
  • Women were told to be mothers of pure Aryan children, given the Honour Cross for many children; "undesirable" births were forbidden.

7. The Art of Propaganda

Under Joseph Goebbels, Nazis used a special language ("final solution", "special treatment"), radio, posters, films, mass rallies and newspapers to spread racial hatred and portray Hitler as a saviour.

8. Ordinary People and Crimes

Many Germans were convinced, while some protested (e.g. Pastor Niemoeller); others stayed silent. After the war, Germany learned from this and the Holocaust is remembered as a warning.

Very Short Q&A (1 mark)

Q1. Where was the Weimar Republic constitution drafted?
At Weimar.
Q2. What was Mein Kampf?
Hitler's book explaining his ideas.
Q3. What was the Gestapo?
The secret state police of Nazi Germany.
Q4. What is Lebensraum?
'Living space': territory Hitler wanted for Germany.
Q5. When did WWII start?
1 September 1939, with Germany's invasion of Poland.
Q6. What was Kristallnacht?
The night of 9-10 Nov 1938 when Jewish property was attacked.
Q7. Who was Goebbels?
Nazi propaganda minister.

Short Q&A (3 marks)

Q1. Why did Germans dislike the Treaty of Versailles?
It forced Germany to accept the war guilt, pay 6 billion pounds, lose colonies and land, and reduce its army. They felt humiliated.
Q2. What was hyperinflation?
In 1923 Germany printed excess currency to pay debts; prices soared and the mark became worthless, wiping out middle-class savings.
Q3. What was the Enabling Act?
A law of 3 March 1933 giving Hitler power to rule by decree, sidelining the Reichstag and establishing dictatorship.
Q4. How did the Nazis treat women?
Women were treated as mothers of the race, expected to stay at home, and rewarded with honour crosses for bearing 'pure' Aryan children; "undesirable" women were punished.
Q5. Why did Nazis become popular after 1929?
The Depression caused unemployment and fear; Hitler promised jobs, national greatness and revenge, using powerful propaganda.

Long Q&A (5 marks)

Q1. Explain the effects of the First World War on Germany and the birth of the Weimar Republic.
Defeated in 1918, Germany saw the Kaiser abdicate and a democratic republic established at Weimar. The Treaty of Versailles imposed war guilt, reparations, loss of territory and resources. The republic was linked with humiliation. Hyperinflation of 1923 ruined savings and political radicalisation grew, making democracy unpopular.
Q2. How did Hitler rise to power?
After a failed putsch, Hitler built the Nazi party. The Great Depression brought unemployment and despair; his speeches, rallies and propaganda promised revival. The party's vote rose from 2.6% to 37% by 1932, and Hindenburg appointed him Chancellor on 30 Jan 1933.
Q3. Describe how Hitler established a dictatorship.
The Fire Decree suspended civil rights; the Enabling Act allowed rule by decree. Parties and unions were banned. The Gestapo, SS and SD terrorised opponents with camps and no trial. Economy was revived under Schacht, schools and media were controlled, building loyalty and fear.
Q4. Describe the Nazi persecution of Jews.
Jews were removed from jobs, lost citizenship by the Nuremberg Laws (1935), faced Kristallnacht (1938), confined in ghettos and finally sent to gas chambers at camps like Auschwitz. About six million were killed in the Holocaust, along with Gypsies, disabled people and others.
