Class 9 Social Science – Population

Contemporary India–I (Geography) · UP Board · Notes with diagrams, Q&A and MCQs

1. Introduction

People are a nation’s most important resource. The study of population (size, distribution, growth, composition) tells us how many people live in a country and what they can contribute. Data comes from the Census, a count of people held every 10 years; the first census in India was in 1872 and the first complete one in 1881. The notes use the 2011 census figures given in the textbook.

Remember: Five themes – size and distribution, population growth and change, age composition, sex ratio, literacy; then occupational structure, health, adolescents and national population policy.

2. Size and Distribution

  • India’s population in 2011 was about 121 crore (1.21 billion), 17.5% of the world, while it has only 2.4% of the world’s area.
  • Uttar Pradesh is the most populous state (about 16.5% of India’s people). Rajasthan has the largest area (10.4%) but only 5.5% of the population. Sikkim and the small UTs have the least people.

Density of population

Density = total population ÷ area (people per sq km). India’s density in 2011 was 382 per sq km. Bihar (about 1,100) and West Bengal are very dense; Arunachal Pradesh (17) is the least. Delhi is the densest UT.

Factors: fertile plains, good climate and rainfall, industries and jobs raise density; mountains, deserts, thick forests and harsh climate lower it.

3. Population Growth and Processes of Change

Fig 1: Population growth of India (animated)

Growth rate is the rate of increase of population. Three processes decide it: birth rate (births per 1,000), death rate (deaths per 1,000) and migration. Natural increase = birth rate − death rate.

  • 1901–1921 (stagnant): high birth and death rates due to famines, epidemics. 1921 is the Great Divide, after which growth began.
  • 1921–1951 (steady): health and sanitation improved, death rate fell.
  • 1951–1981 (rapid): sharp fall in death rate, high birth rate – population explosion. Growth rate peaked at 2.2%.
  • 1981 to now: growth rate is slowly falling, yet the total still increases by over 1.5 crore every year.

Migration

Movement of people from one place to another: internal (within country, mostly rural to urban) and international. Push factors (poverty, unemployment, disasters) and pull factors (jobs, facilities) cause it. Migration changes population distribution and also causes urban crowding.

4. Age Composition

Three groups: children (0–14 years) – dependent, need food and schools; working age (15–59) – earn and support others; aged (60+) – dependent. A large working-age group is a strength. It is shown by an age-sex pyramid.

5. Sex Ratio

Number of females per 1,000 males. India’s 2011 sex ratio is 943. Kerala has the highest (1,084); Haryana is among the lowest. Reasons for a low ratio: preference for sons, female foeticide, neglect of girls, early marriage and women’s health.

6. Literacy Rate

Literacy is the ability to read and write with understanding, counted for those aged 7+. India’s 2011 rate: 74% (males 82%, females 65%). Kerala is the highest (about 94%), Bihar the lowest. Literacy is lower in rural areas and among women.

7. Occupational Structure

Distribution of workers by occupation: primary (agriculture, mining, fishing), secondary (manufacturing, construction), tertiary (transport, trade, banking, services). Agriculture still employs about half of Indian workers; the services share is rising.

8. Health and Adolescents

Better health is shown by low death and infant mortality rates (IMR – infant deaths per 1,000 live births), more life expectancy, medical facilities and clean water. Adolescents (10–19 years) are about one-fifth of the population and need nutrition, education and guidance.

9. National Population Policy 2000

  • Free and compulsory education up to age 14; reduce IMR to below 30 per 1,000 live births.
  • Universal immunisation of children against diseases; promote delayed marriage for girls (not before 18, preferably after 20).
  • Make family welfare a people-centred programme.

11. Long Q&A

L1. Describe the factors affecting the distribution of population.

Population is dense in fertile plains with good climate, water, transport and industries such as the Ganga plain and coasts, and thin in mountains, deserts and dense forests such as Arunachal and Rajasthan. Relief, climate, soil, jobs and urbanisation decide where people settle.

L2. Explain the stages of population growth in India.

1901–21 was stagnant, 1921–51 steady, 1951–81 rapid as the death rate fell, and since 1981 the growth rate is slowly decreasing though the total keeps rising.

L3. What are the causes and effects of migration?

Push factors like poverty and disasters and pull factors like jobs push people to move, mainly from villages to cities. It balances labour but causes overcrowding and pressure on housing and services.

L4. What are the main objectives of the National Population Policy 2000?

Free and compulsory education up to 14, IMR below 30, universal immunisation, delayed marriage of girls and a people-centred family welfare programme.

L5. Why is a low sex ratio a concern?

It shows discrimination against girls, causes social imbalance and needs awareness and strict laws.
