✎ My Childhood ✎

Class 9 • English (Beehive) • by A.P.J. Abdul Kalam • UP Board / NCERT • Detailed Notes + Q&A

About the author: Dr A.P.J. Abdul Kalam (born 1931, Rameswaram) – scientist, “Missile Man of India” and the 11th President of India. This chapter is an autobiographical excerpt from his book Wings of Fire.
Theme: Values learnt in childhood shape a person; secularism, friendship, equality, kindness and the courage to dream.
Main persons: Kalam; his father Jainulabdeen; mother Ashiamma; friends Ramanadha Sastry, Aravindan, Sivaprakasan; Lakshmana Sastry (temple priest); teacher Sivasubramania Iyer; cousin Samsuddin.

★ The Story in Steps (Animated)

1. Home & parents. Kalam was born in a middle-class Tamil family in the island town of Rameswaram (then Madras State). He lived in the ancestral house on Mosque Street. His father had little formal education and wealth but had great innate wisdom and generosity. From him Kalam learnt honesty and self-discipline; from his mother Ashiamma, goodness and deep kindness. Home life was secure and loving.
2. First wages. During World War II the train stopped halting at Rameswaram station, so newspaper bundles were thrown from the moving train on the road. Kalam’s cousin Samsuddin distributed newspapers; Kalam helped him catch the bundles and thus earned his first wages. He felt proud of earning.
3. Three friends. He had three close friends: Ramanadha Sastry (later head priest of the Rameswaram temple), Aravindan (later arranged transport for pilgrims) and Sivaprakasan (later a catering contractor for Southern Railways). Religion never divided them.
4. The new teacher. In class V at Rameswaram Elementary School a new teacher saw Kalam, wearing a Muslim cap, sitting beside Ramanadha Sastry, who wore the sacred thread of a Brahmin. The teacher disliked this and sent Kalam to the last row. Both boys were very sad; Ramanadha Sastry wept.
5. Lakshmana Sastry’s courage. Ramanadha’s father, the high priest Lakshmana Sastry, called the teacher and told him not to spread “the poison of social inequality and communal intolerance” in innocent minds. He asked him to apologise or leave. The teacher regretted and was reformed.
6. Sivasubramania Iyer. The science teacher, an orthodox Brahmin with a very conservative wife, invited Kalam to his house for a meal. His wife refused at first but he himself served Kalam. Next week she took Kalam into her kitchen and served him. Iyer said that to remove social barriers one must do it oneself.
7. Leaving Rameswaram. After the war India was moving towards freedom (Gandhi’s message). Kalam asked his father’s permission to go to Ramanathapuram for higher studies. His father, quoting Khalil Gibran, said his children are not his; they belong to the future, so Kalam should go and grow.

Character Sketch of Kalam (as a boy)

Short, curious, sensitive, hard-working and secular-minded. He earned early, valued friendship, felt hurt by discrimination but learnt from good elders, and dreamt of higher education.

Key Words

  • Innate wisdom – natural wisdom (not from books).
  • Secure – safe and well looked after.
  • Communal intolerance – hatred between religious communities.
  • Reformed – changed for the better.
  • Orthodox – strictly traditional.
  • Moral: Equality, tolerance and courage can heal social divisions; good elders and early values make a great person.

✎ Long Answer Questions

Q1. Describe Kalam’s family and home life.

Kalam was born into a middle-class Tamil family in Rameswaram and lived in the ancestral house on Mosque Street. His father Jainulabdeen, though not wealthy or formally educated, was wise and generous. His mother Ashiamma was kind and a great helpmate. Their home gave him a secure childhood, materially and emotionally, and he learnt honesty and self-discipline from his father and kindness from his mother.

Q2. Narrate the incident of the new teacher and Lakshmana Sastry.

In class V a new teacher saw Kalam, in his Muslim cap, sitting beside Ramanadha Sastry, the priest’s son, and sent Kalam to the last row. Both boys were deeply hurt. When Lakshmana Sastry heard, he summoned the teacher and told him in front of the boys not to spread the poison of social inequality and communal intolerance. He asked him to apologise or leave. The teacher regretted and was reformed.

Q3. How did Sivasubramania Iyer help remove social barriers?

An orthodox Brahmin with a conservative wife, Iyer was still a rebel. He invited Kalam to dinner at his home. His wife was horrified and refused to serve a Muslim boy, so Iyer served him himself and sat beside him. The next week he invited Kalam again; this time his wife took Kalam into the kitchen and served him. Iyer showed that change begins with one’s own actions.

Q4. How did World War II affect Kalam’s life?

The war stopped the train’s halt at Rameswaram, so newspaper bundles had to be thrown from the train. Kalam helped his cousin Samsuddin catch them and earned his first wages. After the war, India moved towards freedom, which inspired Kalam to leave Rameswaram for higher studies.

Q5. What values does the chapter teach?

Honesty, kindness, self-discipline, secularism, friendship across religions, courage to oppose injustice and the importance of education. Elders like Lakshmana Sastry and Iyer show how to fight prejudice.
