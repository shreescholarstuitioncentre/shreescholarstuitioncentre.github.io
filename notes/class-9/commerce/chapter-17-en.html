✎ Classification of Money: Metallic & Paper Money

Class 9 · Commerce · Chapter 18 · UP Board

1. Classification (Diagram)

2. Metallic Money

Money made of metals such as gold, silver, copper, nickel, aluminium.

(a) Standard coins (full-bodied): face value = metal value; unlimited legal tender; freely minted (old gold/silver coins).
(b) Token coins: face value is more than the metal value; limited legal tender; used for small payments (today’s ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10 coins).
Seigniorage = face value − cost of making the coin (profit of the government).
MeritsDemerits
Durable, uniform, divisibleHeavy, risky to carry large sums
Generally accepted, stable valueCostly to mint and replace worn coins
Hard to forgeSupply depends on metal mines, so not elastic

3. Paper Money

Notes and cheques issued on the authority of the government or central bank. In India the RBI issues notes (₹1 note and coins by the Government).

Convertible: can be exchanged for gold/silver on demand.
Inconvertible (fiat): cannot be exchanged for metal; value rests on the law and public trust (all modern notes).
Representative paper money: fully backed by metal reserves.

Note-issue systems

SystemMeaning
Fixed fiduciaryA fixed amount of notes without backing; all issue beyond it needs 100% metal reserve.
Proportional reserveReserve is a fixed % of the notes issued.
Minimum reserveA fixed minimum reserve of gold and foreign securities; notes can be issued freely above it. India follows this (since 1957): ₹200 crore reserve, of which ₹115 crore gold and ₹85 crore foreign securities.
Maximum limitThe government fixes an upper limit on total notes.
Merits of paper moneyDemerits
Light, easy to carry and countEasily damaged, burnt or forged
Cheap to produceOver-issue causes inflation
Elastic supply as per needValue depends on trust in the government

Gresham’s Law: “Bad money drives out good money” – when two kinds of money of the same face value circulate, people hoard the good one.

4. Solved Examples (Step by Step)

Easy: A ₹10 token coin contains metal worth ₹2 and costs ₹1 more to mint. Find the seigniorage.

To find: government’s profit.
Step 1: Cost = 2 + 1 = ₹3.
Step 2: Seigniorage = face value − cost = 10 − 3.
Step 3: = ₹7.

Intermediate: Under a 40% proportional reserve system, notes of ₹1,000 crore are issued. Find the reserve.

Step 1: Reserve = 40% of 1,000.
Step 2: = (40 / 100) × 1,000.
Step 3: = ₹400 crore.

Hard: Under the fixed fiduciary system the limit is ₹500 crore. If the reserve held is ₹300 crore, prove that the maximum notes issuable are ₹800 crore.

To prove: max notes = fiduciary limit + reserve.
Step 1: Notes up to ₹500 crore need no reserve.
Step 2: Every note above it needs 100% reserve, so ₹300 crore reserve supports ₹300 crore notes.
Step 3: Total = 500 + 300 = ₹800 crore. Hence proved.

1. Coins whose face value is more than metal value are—

Ans: (b) Token coins.

2. Inconvertible paper money is called—

B. Short Answers

7. What is metallic money?

Money made of metal, such as gold, silver, copper or nickel coins.

8. What are standard coins?

Coins whose face value equals their metal value and which are unlimited legal tender.

9. What is fiat money?

Money declared legal tender by the government, not convertible into metal.

10. Name two systems of note issue.

Fixed fiduciary and minimum reserve system.

11. Give two merits of paper money.

It is light and easy to carry, and its supply can be adjusted to need.

12. Give two demerits of metallic money.

It is heavy to carry and costly to mint.
