1. Introduction: Design and Decoration

Alekhan (Design) means arranging lines, shapes and colours according to a plan so that a beautiful, well-organised pattern is created. An artist first makes the design on paper and then applies it to an object.

Alankaran (Decoration) means making an object, building, cloth, pot or surface beautiful by adding ornament to it. The word alankar means ornament, so alankaran means “adorning” a thing.

RememberDesign (alekhan) = planning and making a beautiful composition; Decoration (alankaran) = using that composition to adorn an object. The two complete each other.

Difference between design and decoration

BasisDesign (Alekhan)Decoration (Alankaran)
MeaningMaking a pattern with line, shape, colourAdorning an object or place
NaturePlanning of the compositionApplication of the composition
OrderComes firstComes after design
AimTo create a beautiful, regular formTo increase beauty and value of the object
ExampleDrawing a border or flower design on paperUsing that design on a saree, plate, wall or floor

Qualities of a good design

  • Simplicity – easy to draw.
  • Clarity – lines are clean and shapes recognisable.
  • Order – all parts arranged by rule.
  • Repeatability – the unit can be repeated again and again.
  • Usefulness – can be used on cloth, pots, buildings, etc.

A short history

Humans have decorated pots, walls and clothes since very early times. Pottery of the Indus Valley Civilisation carries geometric lines and animal forms. The ceilings of the Ajanta-Ellora caves have rich ornament. Medieval buildings show stone lattice work and inlay, while textiles are known for bandhani, printing and embroidery designs.

2. Types of Design

On the basis of form, design is mainly of four types:

TypeFeatureExample
1. Geometric designMade from circles, squares, triangles, rectangles, lines, dots; based on measurement with compass and scaleSix-petal rosette, stars, square grids, mandala
2. Natural designTaken from nature – flowers, leaves, creepers, animals, birds, fish, peacockLotus, mango leaf, floral vines
3. Stylised / decorative designSimplified, symmetrical decorative form of a natural objectStylised peacock, simple lotus
4. Abstract designDoes not look like a real object; only a pleasing arrangement of line, shape and colourWavy line patterns, colour blocks
NoteSome textbooks classify designs as “geometric, natural, decorative and human figures”. In the exam, write the classification given in your own textbook; the main idea is the same.

What is stylisation?

Instead of copying a natural object exactly, we keep its main structure, remove unnecessary details, simplify and balance the lines and turn it into a decorative form. This is called stylisation.

Method of stylisation

  1. Observe the object carefully (shape, proportion, main parts).
  2. Find its simple basic shape (oval, triangle, circle).
  3. Remove uneven edges and small unnecessary lines.
  4. Make the form symmetrical.
  5. Turn veins/petals into straight lines or equal curves.
  6. Fill flat colours and darken the outline.

3. Design by Position / Arrangement

According to where the design is placed on the paper or object, the main forms are:

(a) Border design

A design made by repeating one unit at equal gaps in a long narrow strip. It is used in saree borders, bedsheets, the rim of a plate, page edges of a book and wall bands. Its main quality is repetition.

  • Draw two parallel lines to fix the strip width.
  • Divide the length into equal parts equal to the unit size.
  • Draw one unit, then repeat it by measurement or tracing.
  • Using alternate colours in alternate units looks even better.

(b) Corner design

A design made at the corners of a square or rectangular object (handkerchief, tablecloth, invitation card, frame). One corner is completed and its symmetrical copies are drawn at the other corners.

(c) Centre design

A design made in the middle of a surface in the form of a circle, square, hexagon or octagon. Layers (rings) are built from the centre outwards. A plate centre, rangoli and mandala are examples.

(d) All-over design

The unit is repeated across the whole surface in both horizontal and vertical directions. Printed cloth, wallpaper, floor tiles and carpets use this.

FormWhereKey point
BorderStrip / edgeRepetition in one direction
CornerFour cornersSymmetry about the diagonal
CentreMiddleEqual parts around the centre
All-overWhole surfaceRepetition in both directions

5. Making Geometric Designs

Example 1: Six-petal rosette (with a compass)

  1. Take a centre O and draw a circle of radius r (say 4 cm) with the compass.
  2. Keeping the same compass opening, place the point on a mark (1) on the circle and cut the circle to get mark (2). Continue to mark 3, 4, 5, 6. The circle is divided into six equal parts.
  3. With the compass point on each mark, draw an arc of radius r inside the circle.
  4. The arcs meet and form six petals.
  5. Colour the petals and darken the outline.
Why does this work?The triangle formed by O and two neighbouring marks has all three sides equal to r (an equilateral triangle), so each central angle is 60°. 360° ÷ 60° = 6 parts. That is why the side of a regular hexagon equals its radius.

Example 2: Geometric design in a square

  1. Draw a square of side 7 cm.
  2. Draw both diagonals; their meeting point O is the centre.
  3. Mark the mid-points of the sides and draw the mid-lines.
  4. Join the four mid-points to make the inner square (diamond).
  5. Draw the inscribed circle with radius 3.5 cm from O.
  6. Fill alternate small regions with two colours.
TipFor a neat look, use only two or three colours in a geometric design and fill them alternately.

6. Natural (Floral) Designs

In natural design, flowers, leaves, creepers, lotus, mango (ambi), peacock, parrot, fish, swan etc. are drawn in a stylised form.

Steps to draw an eight-petal stylised flower

  1. Draw a small circle at the centre O.
  2. Draw two perpendicular diameters (4 parts), then bisect the angles or use a protractor to draw lines at 45° (8 directions).
  3. On each line draw an oval petal outside the central circle. Keep all petals the same size.
  4. Add small dots or circles inside the centre.
  5. Draw a straight stem downwards with two identical leaves on both sides.
  6. Colour: petals red/pink, centre yellow, leaves green.

Points to remember

  • The object should remain recognisable, but without too many fine details.
  • Follow symmetry.
  • Spaces between shapes should be balanced.
  • Lines should flow rhythmically.

7. Symmetry and Repetition

Symmetry means both sides of a figure are equal in size, shape and distance. If a figure is folded along a line and the two halves coincide exactly, it has line symmetry and the line is called the axis of symmetry.

Kinds of symmetry

KindMeaningExample
Line (mirror) symmetryEqual parts on both sides of an axisButterfly, leaf, kalash
Rotational symmetryLooks the same when turned around the centreSix-petal rosette, star, wheel
Asymmetric (balanced)Different shapes on two sides but equal visual weightSome modern/abstract designs

Kinds of repetition

KindExplanation
Simple repetitionThe same unit repeated at equal distances
Alternate repetitionTwo different units (or two colours) one after the other
Mirror repetitionOne unit upright, the next its mirror image
Gradual repetitionUnit becomes gradually smaller or larger
Radial repetitionRepeated at equal angles around a centre

9. Colour and Colour Schemes

Groups of colours

  • Primary colours: red, yellow, blue – they cannot be made by mixing other colours.
  • Secondary colours: green (yellow+blue), orange (red+yellow), violet (red+blue).
  • Tertiary colours: a primary plus the neighbouring secondary, e.g. yellow-green, blue-green, red-orange.
  • Warm colours: red, orange, yellow – feeling of heat and energy.
  • Cool colours: blue, green, violet – feeling of calm and coolness.
  • Complementary colours: opposite each other on the wheel – red-green, blue-orange, yellow-violet. Together they give strong contrast.
  • Analogous colours: neighbours on the wheel, e.g. yellow, yellow-green, green.

Colour schemes

SchemeMeaningEffect
MonochromeLight and dark shades of one colourCalm, uniform
AnalogousNeighbouring coloursSoft, pleasant
ComplementaryOpposite coloursStrong, attractive
Warm / cool schemeOnly warm or only cool coloursMood-based
Tint and shadeAdding white to a colour gives a tint (lighter); adding black gives a shade (darker). Keep water low in poster colour so the colour stays flat and bright.

Precautions while colouring

  • Fill light colours first, then dark.
  • Do not take too much water on the brush; stay inside the lines.
  • Let one colour dry before filling the next to it.
  • Use few colours (3–4).
  • At the end, define shapes with a black outline.

Method of dot-based design

  1. Make a square grid of equally spaced dots (e.g. 5×5).
  2. Mark the axis of symmetry with a light line.
  3. Draw curved lines around or between the dots.
  4. Keep the same design on both sides.
  5. Colour the lines or highlight with flour/colour.

11. Complete Method of Making a Design

  1. Planning: decide the object, size and theme (floral/geometric).
  2. Construction lines: draw light boundary, mid-lines, diagonals and circles.
  3. Main form: draw the principal shape.
  4. Details and repetition: add small parts; repeat the unit.
  5. Outline: darken the correct lines and erase construction lines.
  6. Colouring: as per the colour scheme.
  7. Final check: neatness, symmetry, colour harmony.
Common mistakesHeavy lines, not measuring, unequal gaps, too many colours, colour going outside the lines, leaving construction lines.
