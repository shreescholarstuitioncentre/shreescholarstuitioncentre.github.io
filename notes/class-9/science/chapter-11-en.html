Class 9 Science – Chapter 11: Sound (UP Board)

1. Production of Sound

Sound is produced by vibrating objects. Vibration is a rapid to-and-fro motion about a mean position. Examples: a plucked guitar string, a struck tuning fork, a beaten drum, vocal cords in the throat, a flapping bee wing. Sound is a form of energy and is produced when energy is given to an object to make it vibrate.

2. Propagation of Sound

Sound needs a material medium (solid, liquid or gas) to travel, because it moves by vibrating the particles of the medium. It cannot travel in vacuum. Proof: in the electric bell in a bell jar experiment, the ring is heard clearly at first but fades as air is pumped out, and is not heard in vacuum. This is why astronauts talk by radio on the Moon.

The particles do not travel with the wave; they only vibrate about their positions and pass the disturbance on to neighbours.

3. Sound Waves

Sound waves are longitudinal waves: particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction of wave travel.
Fig 1: Sound as a longitudinal wave (compressions and rarefactions)

Compression (C): region where particles are crowded, high pressure and density. Rarefaction (R): region where particles are spread out, low pressure and density. Sound travels as alternate compressions and rarefactions.

Transverse waves (light, ripples on water, wave in a rope) have particle vibration perpendicular to wave direction, forming crests and troughs. A longitudinal wave is shown as a graph of density/pressure against distance.

4. Characteristics of a Sound Wave

Fig 2: Wave terms – crest, trough, amplitude, wavelength

Wavelength (λ): distance between two consecutive compressions (or crests); unit m.
Frequency (f): number of vibrations per second; unit hertz (Hz).
Time period (T): time for one complete vibration; T = 1/f; unit s.
Amplitude (A): maximum displacement of particles from the mean position.
Speed (v): distance travelled by the wave per unit time.

v = f × λ   (wave equation)

Pitch depends on frequency: high frequency gives shrill sound (female voice, whistle), low frequency gives grave sound (male voice, lion's roar). Loudness depends on amplitude (and is measured in decibel, dB): larger amplitude, louder sound. Quality or timbre lets us tell apart two sounds of the same pitch and loudness (e.g. sitar and flute). Intensity is the sound energy passing per second through unit area.

Fig 3: Vibrating tuning fork producing sound

5. Speed of Sound

Speed of sound is greatest in solids, less in liquids and least in gases: iron ≈ 5950 m/s, water ≈ 1500 m/s, air ≈ 344 m/s (at 22 °C). It increases with temperature and does not depend on loudness or pitch. Light travels much faster than sound, so lightning is seen before thunder is heard.

6. Reflection of Sound and Echo

Sound reflects from hard surfaces following the same laws as light: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, and the incident sound, reflected sound and normal lie in the same plane.

Fig 4: Reflection of sound – echo
Echo: reflected sound heard distinctly after the original sound. Persistence of hearing is about 0.1 s, so the minimum distance to the reflector = ½ × 344 × 0.1 ≈ 17.2 m. Distance of reflector: d = v × t / 2.

Reverberation: repeated reflections that make a sound persist (in big halls); reduced using curtains, carpets and sound-absorbing panels. Uses of reflection: megaphone, horn, stethoscope, curved sound boards in concert halls.

7. Range of Hearing, Ultrasound and SONAR

Audible range of humans: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.
Infrasound: below 20 Hz (earthquakes, whales, elephants).
Ultrasound: above 20 kHz (bats, dolphins, dog whistle).

Uses of ultrasound: cleaning parts, detecting cracks in metals, ultrasonography and echocardiography in medicine, breaking kidney stones.

SONAR (Sound Navigation And Ranging) sends ultrasonic waves and measures the time of the echo to find depth d = v × t / 2, locate submarines, shipwrecks and fish. Bats use echolocation to find prey.

Short Answer Questions

Q. What is a sound wave? Which type is it?

A disturbance that travels through a medium by vibrating its particles. It is a longitudinal wave.

Q. Define frequency, time period and give their relation.

Frequency is vibrations per second (Hz); time period is time for one vibration; T = 1/f.

Q. Why can't sound travel in vacuum?

There are no particles in vacuum to vibrate and carry the disturbance.

Q. Differentiate between compression and rarefaction.

Compression: particles crowded, high pressure. Rarefaction: particles spread, low pressure.

Q. What is an echo? Give two uses of reflection of sound.

Reflected sound heard separately after the original. Uses: megaphone, stethoscope, sound boards.

Q. What is infrasound and ultrasound?

Infrasound has frequency below 20 Hz; ultrasound above 20 kHz.

Q. Why do we see lightning before hearing thunder?

Light travels at 3×10⁸ m/s, far faster than sound at about 340 m/s.

Q. Name the characteristics of sound.

Pitch (frequency), loudness (amplitude) and quality or timbre.

Long Answer and Numerical Questions

Q. A sound has frequency 500 Hz and speed 340 m/s. Find its wavelength.

v = fλ, so λ = v/f = 340/500 = 0.68 m

Q. A person hears an echo 3 s after a shout. If speed of sound is 342 m/s, find the distance of the reflecting wall.

2d = v × t, d = 342×3/2 = 513 m

Q. Find the time period of a 50 Hz wave.

T = 1/f = 1/50 = 0.02 s

Q. A SONAR pulse returns after 4 s. Speed of sound in water is 1500 m/s. Find the depth of the sea.

d = v×t/2 = 1500×4/2 = 3000 m

Q. Thunder is heard 5 s after seeing lightning (v = 340 m/s). How far is the lightning?

d = v × t = 340 × 5 = 1700 m (light time is negligible)

Q. Explain with a diagram how a tuning fork produces sound waves.

When struck, the prongs vibrate. Moving outward, a prong pushes the air and creates a compression; moving inward, it leaves a low-pressure rarefaction. These alternate and travel outward as a longitudinal sound wave, as shown in Fig 3, until they reach the ear.
