Class 9 Science – Chapter 6: Tissues

UP Board / NCERT • Notes with Q&A

1. What are Tissues?

A tissue is a group of cells similar in structure and origin that work together to perform a specific function. Eg: blood, muscle, xylem, phloem.

In unicellular organisms (Amoeba) one cell does every job; in multicellular organisms there is division of labour – different groups of cells specialise in different tasks, making the body more efficient. Cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism.

Plants are stationary; need support, so mostly dead supportive tissueMove around; need more energy, mostly living tissue

2. Plant Tissues – Meristematic Tissue

Plant tissues are of two main types: meristematic (dividing) and permanent (non-dividing).

Meristematic tissue = actively dividing cells at growth regions. Cells are small, thin-walled, closely packed (no intercellular spaces), with dense cytoplasm, prominent nucleus and no vacuoles.
TypePositionFunction
ApicalTips of roots and shootsIncrease in length
Lateral (cambium)Sides of stem/root (in a ring)Increase in girth (thickness)
IntercalaryBase of leaves or internodesGrowth in length of such parts (grasses, mint)

Cells formed by meristem take permanent shape, size and function; this is differentiation, giving permanent tissues.

3. Permanent Tissue – Simple and Protective

A. Simple permanent tissues (one type of cell)

(i) Parenchyma: living, thin cellulose walls, loosely arranged with intercellular spaces. Stores food; chlorenchyma (with chloroplasts) does photosynthesis; aerenchyma (large air cavities) in aquatic plants gives buoyancy.
(ii) Collenchyma: living, elongated cells with thickened corners (pectin and cellulose). Gives flexibility and mechanical support; found in leaf stalks, below epidermis of stems.
(iii) Sclerenchyma: dead cells with thick walls of lignin, no intercellular spaces, narrow lumen. Makes plant hard and stiff: husk of coconut, stem, veins of leaves, seed coat, fibres.

B. Protective tissues

Epidermis: single outer layer, no intercellular spaces, flat cells. Covered by waxy cuticle (waterproof, in aerial parts). Roots have root hairs that absorb water. Contains stomata (Fig 3): pores enclosed by two bean-shaped guard cells, for gaseous exchange and transpiration; guard cells open/close the pore by changes in water pressure.
Cork (bark): in older stems/roots, cambium forms several layers of dead cells with suberin in walls – impermeable to gases and water, protective.

4. Complex Permanent Tissue – Xylem and Phloem

Made of more than one type of cell working together as a unit; called vascular or conducting tissue forming the vascular bundles.

Xylem – conducts water and minerals upward from roots to leaves. Components: tracheids and vessels (dead, tubular, thick walls), xylem parenchyma (living, stores food, lateral conduction), xylem fibres (support). Except parenchyma, all dead.
Phloem – conducts food (sucrose) from leaves to other parts, up and down (translocation). Components: sieve tubes (with perforated sieve plates), companion cells (control sieve tubes), phloem parenchyma, phloem fibres (dead). Except fibres, all living.

5. Animal Tissues – Epithelial Tissue

Four main types: epithelial, connective, muscular, nervous. Epithelial tissue forms a protective covering or lining of body and organs. Cells are tightly packed with little intercellular material and rest on a basement membrane; it acts as a barrier and controls exchange of materials (skin, mouth, blood vessel lining, alveoli, kidney tubules).

6. Connective Tissue

Cells are loosely spaced, embedded in an intercellular matrix (jelly, fluid, dense or rigid). It connects, supports and binds other tissues.

7. Muscular Tissue

Made of long cells called muscle fibres, containing contractile proteins; contraction and relaxation cause movement. Three types (Fig 7):

Striated (skeletal, voluntary): cylindrical, unbranched, multinucleate, light and dark stripes; limbs, move at will.
Smooth (unstriated, involuntary): long, spindle-shaped, single nucleus; no stripes; in alimentary canal, blood vessels, iris, ureter, bronchi; not under will.
Cardiac (involuntary): cylindrical, branched, single nucleus, rhythmic contraction throughout life; only in the heart; connected by intercalated discs.

Fig 7: Muscular tissues (animated)

8. Nervous Tissue

Made of neurons (nerve cells), which receive and transmit impulses rapidly, allowing quick response to stimuli. A neuron has a cell body (cyton) with a nucleus and cytoplasm, short branched dendrites that receive impulse, and a long axon that carries it away (up to a metre long). Many nerve fibres bound together form a nerve. Brain, spinal cord and nerves are made of nervous tissue.

Path of impulse: dendrite → cell body → axon → nerve ending (synapse) → next neuron or muscle/gland.

Short Answer Questions

Q1. What is a tissue?
✍ A group of similar cells with a common origin that perform a specific function.
Q2. What is meristematic tissue?
✍ Tissue of actively dividing cells found at growth regions.
Q3. Name the three types of meristem.
✍ Apical, lateral and intercalary.
Q4. Which tissue increases the girth of a stem?
✍ Lateral meristem (cambium).
Q5. What is differentiation?
✍ Taking up of a permanent shape, size and function by cells formed by meristem.
Q6. Write one function of parenchyma.
✍ Stores food; chlorenchyma does photosynthesis.
Q7. Why are collenchyma cells flexible?
✍ Their walls are thickened only at the corners with pectin.
Q8. What makes sclerenchyma hard?
✍ Lignin deposited in thick walls.
Q9. Which cells form stomata and what do they do?
✍ Guard cells; control gaseous exchange and transpiration.
Q10. What is the function of the cuticle?
✍ Waterproof coating that reduces water loss.
Q11. Name the two types of complex tissue.
✍ Xylem and phloem.
Q12. Which components of xylem are living?
✍ Xylem parenchyma only.
Q13. What do companion cells do?
✍ Help sieve tubes in transporting food.
Q14. What is cork? Why is it protective?
✍ Dead cells with suberin; impermeable to water and gas.
Q15. Name the four types of animal tissue.
✍ Epithelial, connective, muscular, nervous.
Q16. Why do epithelial cells have little intercellular space?
✍ So they form a continuous barrier.
Q17. Where is ciliated epithelium found?
✍ Respiratory tract – cilia move mucus.
Q18. What is the difference between ligament and tendon?
✍ Ligament joins bone to bone; tendon joins muscle to bone.
Q19. Why is blood considered a connective tissue?
✍ It has cells in a fluid matrix (plasma) and connects different body parts by transport.
Q20. What is adipose tissue?
✍ Connective tissue storing fat; insulator.
Q21. Name the types of muscle fibre.
✍ Striated, smooth and cardiac.
Q22. Why are cardiac muscles tireless?
✍ They contract and relax rhythmically without fatigue due to special structure and intercalated discs.
Q23. What is a neuron? Name its parts.
✍ Structural unit of nervous tissue: cell body, dendrites, axon.
Q24. What is the function of cartilage?
✍ Smoothens joints and gives flexible support (ear, nose).

Long Answer Questions

Q1. Describe the types of meristematic tissue with their locations and functions.
✍ See Section 2, table and Fig 1. Apical meristem – tips, growth in length. Lateral – cambium, growth in girth. Intercalary – at base of leaves/internodes.
Q2. Differentiate parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma.
✍ See Section 3 and Fig 2: parenchyma – living, thin wall, loose, storage; collenchyma – living, corners thick, flexible; sclerenchyma – dead, lignin, hard.
Q3. Describe the structure and function of stomata.
✍ Stomata are pores in the epidermis bounded by two bean-shaped guard cells. They open for gaseous exchange and transpiration and close to prevent water loss (Fig 3). Guard cells contain chloroplasts.
Q4. Differentiate between xylem and phloem with a diagram.
✍ See table in Section 4 and Fig 4.
Q5. Describe the epithelial tissues with types and examples.
✍ Section 5 table and Fig 5: squamous, stratified, cuboidal, columnar, ciliated, glandular.
Q6. Describe connective tissues: blood, bone, cartilage, ligament, tendon, areolar, adipose.
✍ See Section 6 table and Fig 6. Blood has plasma and cells; bone has hard matrix; cartilage is flexible; ligaments and tendons are fibrous.
Q7. Describe the three types of muscular tissue with their structure, location and function.
✍ Section 7 and Fig 7: striated – voluntary, limbs; smooth – involuntary, gut; cardiac – heart.
Q8. Draw and describe a neuron. How does it transmit impulses?
✍ Fig 8: dendrites receive impulse, pass to cell body, axon carries it to the nerve ending; myelin sheath speeds up transmission at nodes of Ranvier.
Q9. How is plant tissue different from animal tissue?
✍ See table in Section 1: plant tissues support, mostly dead, growth in meristems; animal tissues need energy, mostly living, uniform growth.
Q10. What is the importance of the division of labour in multicellular organisms?
✍ Cells specialise in different tasks, so complex bodies work efficiently – tissues form organs and organ systems.
