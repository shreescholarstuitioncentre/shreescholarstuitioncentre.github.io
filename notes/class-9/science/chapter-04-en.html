1. Charged Particles in Matter

Rubbing glass rod with silk shows matter has charges, so atoms must be divisible (contrary to Dalton).

Electron (e⁻)

Discovered by J.J. Thomson (1897) in cathode ray tubes. Charge = −1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C (−1 unit), mass ≈ 1/1837 of H atom (9.1×10⁻³¹ kg).

Proton (p⁺)

Goldstein (1886) discovered canal rays (anode rays) in a discharge tube with perforated cathode – positively charged radiations. Proton: charge +1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, mass 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg ≈ 1 u. H atom has 1 proton.

Neutron (n)

Discovered by James Chadwick (1932). No charge, mass ≈ 1 u (same as proton). All atoms have neutrons except hydrogen (¹H).

2. Thomson’s Model of Atom (1898)

Atom = a positively charged sphere with electrons embedded in it, like seeds in a watermelon or plums in a pudding. Total negative charge = total positive charge, so atom is electrically neutral.

Called the plum pudding / watermelon / Christmas pudding model. Drawback: could not explain the results of Rutherford’s scattering experiment and the stability of atom.

3. Rutherford’s Model of Atom (1911)

Experiment: A beam of fast-moving α-particles (He²⁺: +2 charge, 4 u mass) from a radioactive source was directed at a very thin gold foil (~1000 atoms thick, 0.0001 mm) (Fig 2). A fluorescent ZnS screen around the foil showed where particles landed. Gold was chosen because it is highly malleable and can be made extremely thin.

Observations and Conclusions

ObservationConclusion
Most α-particles passed straight throughMost of the atom is empty space
Some deflected by small anglesPositive charge is concentrated, repels α
1 in 12000 rebounded 180°Very small, dense, positive nucleus (size ~10⁻¹⁵ m vs atom ~10⁻¹⁰ m)
Rutherford’s model: (i) Atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus holding almost all mass. (ii) Electrons revolve around it in circular paths. (iii) Size of nucleus ≪ size of atom.

Drawbacks: A revolving electron is accelerating and would radiate energy, spiral and fall into the nucleus, making atom unstable – but atoms are stable.

4. Bohr’s Model and Distribution of Electrons

Bohr’s postulates (1913)

1) Only certain special orbits (discrete orbits) of electrons are allowed inside the atom. 2) While revolving in these orbits electrons do not radiate energy (stationary orbits). 3) These orbits or shells are called energy levels, K, L, M, N (n = 1, 2, 3, 4) – see Fig 3. Electrons absorb or emit energy only when jumping between shells.

Bohr–Bury scheme

1) Max electrons in a shell = 2n²: K=2, L=8, M=18, N=32.
2) Outermost shell holds at most 8 electrons; penultimate (second last) at most 18.
3) Electrons fill a shell only after the inner shells are full.

Eg: Na (11): K=2, L=8, M=1 → 2,8,1. Cl (17): 2,8,7. Ca (20): 2,8,8,2.

5. Valency, Atomic Number and Mass Number

Valency: combining capacity of an atom. Atoms with 8 electrons in the outermost shell (octet) are stable (inert). If outer electrons ≤ 4, valency = number of outer electrons; if > 4, valency = 8 − outer electrons. Eg: Na=1, Mg=2, Al=3, Si=4, P=3, S=2, Cl=1, He, Ne, Ar = 0 (completely filled outer shell; He has duplet).

Atomic number (Z) = number of protons = number of electrons in a neutral atom. Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons (nucleons). Notation: ᴬ_Z X, e.g. ²³₁₁Na (A = 23, Z = 11, n = 12). Neutrons = A − Z.

Atomic number identifies an element; it is its unique ‘identity card’. Number of electrons of an ion: Na⁺ = 10, Cl⁻ = 18, Mg²⁺ = 10, O²⁻ = 10.

6. Isotopes and Isobars

Isotopes: atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass number (different neutrons). Same chemical properties, different physical properties. Eg: protium, deuterium, tritium (Fig 4); ¹²C, ¹⁴C; ³⁵Cl, ³⁷Cl.

Average atomic mass of Cl = (75×35 + 25×37)/100 = 35.5 u. Because mass numbers of isotopes are whole numbers while atomic masses of elements are often fractional.

Uses of isotopes

Uranium-235 – fuel in nuclear reactors; Cobalt-60 – radiotherapy for cancer; Iodine-131 – treatment of goitre; Carbon-14 – carbon dating of fossils; Sodium-24 – blood circulation study.

Isobars: atoms of different elements with the same mass number but different atomic numbers. Eg: ⁴⁰₂₀Ca and ⁴⁰₁₈Ar; ¹⁴₆C and ¹⁴₇N. Different chemical properties.

Short Answer Questions

Q1. Who discovered the electron and neutron?
✍ Electron – J.J. Thomson (1897); neutron – James Chadwick (1932).
Q2. What are canal rays?
✍ Positively charged rays from the anode side in a discharge tube, discovered by Goldstein.
Q3. Why did Thomson’s atom stay neutral?
✍ Positive charge of the sphere equals the total negative charge of the electrons.
Q4. Why was gold foil used in Rutherford’s experiment?
✍ Gold is highly malleable and can be beaten into extremely thin foil.
Q5. What did the α-rebound show?
✍ The atom has a very small, dense, positively charged nucleus.
Q6. What is the drawback of Rutherford’s model?
✍ It could not explain the stability of the atom.
Q7. State Bohr’s main postulate.
✍ Electrons revolve in stationary orbits without radiating energy.
Q8. What is the maximum number of electrons in M shell?
✍ 2n² = 18.
Q9. Define valency. What is the valency of Mg?
✍ Combining capacity; Mg = 2,8,2 → valency 2.
Q10. Define atomic number and mass number.
✍ Z = number of protons; A = protons + neutrons.
Q11. Define isotopes with examples.
✍ Same Z, different A: ¹²C and ¹⁴C.
Q12. What are isobars? Give example.
✍ Same A, different Z: ⁴⁰Ca and ⁴⁰Ar.
Q13. Write electronic configuration of Cl.
✍ 2, 8, 7.
Q14. Why do isotopes have same chemical properties?
✍ They have the same number of electrons (same configuration).
Q15. Give two uses of isotopes.
✍ Co-60 in cancer treatment; U-235 as nuclear fuel.
Q16. Calculate the number of neutrons in ²⁷₁₃Al.
✍ 27 − 13 = 14.

Long Answer / Numerical Questions

Q1. Describe Rutherford’s α-scattering experiment with observations and conclusions.
✍ The experiment, table of observations/conclusions and model are in Section 3 and Fig 2. α-particles from a source were directed on gold foil; most went straight, few deflected, very few rebounded, giving the nuclear model.
Q2. List the postulates of Bohr’s model and rules for distribution of electrons.
✍ Postulates and Bohr–Bury rules (2n², outer ≤ 8, penultimate ≤ 18, fill inner first) are given in Section 4.
Q3. Compare the models of Thomson and Rutherford.
✍ Thomson: solid positive sphere with electrons embedded, no nucleus. Rutherford: tiny dense nucleus with electrons revolving in space around it; explains α-scattering but not stability.
Q4. What are isotopes? Why is the atomic mass of chlorine 35.5 u? Give uses of isotopes.
✍ Isotopes have same Z but different A. Cl has ³⁵Cl (75%) and ³⁷Cl (25%): average = 35.5 u. Uses: U-235, Co-60, I-131, C-14.
Q5. An element has 17 protons and 18 neutrons. Find its atomic number, mass number, electron configuration and valency.
✍ Z = 17; A = 17+18 = 35; configuration 2,8,7; valency 1 (chlorine, ³⁵Cl).
Q6. Find the number of protons, electrons and neutrons in Na⁺ (²³₁₁Na).
✍ Protons 11, electrons 11−1 = 10, neutrons 23−11 = 12.
Q7. Write the distribution of electrons in carbon and sodium atoms (draw Bohr diagrams).
✍ Carbon (Z=6): K=2, L=4. Sodium (Z=11): K=2, L=8, M=1. See Fig 3.
Q8. Why are Na and Cl reactive but Ne and Ar inert?
✍ Na (2,8,1) and Cl (2,8,7) have incomplete outer shells; they lose/gain electrons to achieve an octet. Ne (2,8) and Ar (2,8,8) already have a complete octet.
