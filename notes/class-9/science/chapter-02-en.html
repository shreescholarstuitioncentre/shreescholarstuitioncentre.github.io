Class 9 Science – Chapter 2: Is Matter Around Us Pure?

UP Board / NCERT • Notes with Q&A

1. Pure Substance and Mixture

Pure substance: contains only one kind of particle (element or compound), fixed composition and properties. Eg: gold, water, sugar, NaCl.
Mixture: two or more substances mixed in any ratio, retain their own properties. Eg: air, sea water, soil, milk, brass.

In everyday life “pure” (pure milk, pure ghee) means no adulteration; in science pure means only one kind of substance.

Homogeneous mixtureHeterogeneous mixture
Uniform compositionNon-uniform composition
One phase; parts not visibleDifferent phases; parts visible
Salt in water, alloys, airSand in water, oil in water, soil

Alloy = homogeneous mixture of metals (or metal + non-metal) that cannot be separated by physical methods. Brass (Cu+Zn), bronze (Cu+Sn), steel (Fe+C), solder (Pb+Sn).

2. Solutions

Solution = homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. Solvent = component in larger amount that dissolves the other; Solute = component dissolved.

Properties of a true solution

• Homogeneous • particle size < 1 nm (cannot be seen) • does not scatter light (no Tyndall effect) • particles do not settle on standing • cannot be separated by filtration.

Types: solid-in-liquid (sugar in water), gas-in-liquid (soda water), liquid-in-liquid (vinegar), solid-in-solid (alloys), gas-in-gas (air). Aerated drinks contain CO₂ in water; fish live on dissolved O₂.

Concentration of solution

Mass % = (mass of solute ÷ mass of solution) × 100
Volume % = (volume of solute ÷ volume of solution) × 100
Mass by volume % = (mass of solute ÷ volume of solution) × 100

Eg: 40 g salt in 320 g water → mass % = 40/360 × 100 = 11.11%.

Unsaturated solution can dissolve more solute at a temperature; saturated cannot; solubility = max solute (g) in 100 g solvent at a temperature, usually increases with temperature. Supersaturated holds more than saturated limit.

3. Suspension and Colloid

Suspension (heterogeneous)

Particles > 100 nm, visible to the eye, scatter light, settle on standing, separated by filtration. Eg: muddy water, chalk in water, flour in water.

Colloid (appears homogeneous, actually heterogeneous)

Particle size 1–100 nm; cannot be seen but scatter light (Tyndall effect) – see Fig 1; do not settle; cannot be filtered but separated by centrifugation. Particles show zig-zag Brownian motion because of collisions with molecules of the dispersing medium. Parts: dispersed phase + dispersion medium.

Tyndall effect: scattering of a light beam by colloidal particles making its path visible. Seen: sunlight through mist/forest canopy, torch light in smoky/dusty room, headlight in fog, light entering a dark room through a gap.

4. Separating the Components of a Mixture

(a) Heterogeneous mixtures

• Handpicking/Sieving/Winnowing – stones from rice; chaff from grain.
• Filtration – sand from water.
• Evaporation – dissolved salt from water; also cream/dye.
• Centrifugation – cream from milk; blood in labs. Denser particles move to bottom, lighter stay on top.
• Separating funnel – two immiscible liquids (oil and water): denser layer is drained first.
• Sublimation – NH₄Cl, camphor, naphthalene from salt/sand.
• Magnetic separation – iron from sand.

(b) Homogeneous mixtures

Crystallisation: pure copper sulphate/alum/sugar crystals from saturated solution on cooling; better than evaporation as it leaves impurities and does not decompose.

Chromatography: separates solutes dissolving in same solvent (inks, dyes, pigments) based on different solubility in solvent, see Fig 3. Result is a chromatogram. Uses: dyes, drugs in blood, pigments from natural colours.

Simple distillation: two miscible liquids with boiling point difference > 25 K, or solvent from solution – Fig 2. Eg: water from salt solution; acetone (329 K) from water (373 K).

Fractional distillation: miscible liquids with boiling point difference < 25 K. A fractionating column with glass beads gives repeated vaporisation-condensation. Eg: petroleum fractions, air separation (liquid air: nitrogen 77 K, argon 87 K, oxygen 90 K).

5. Physical and Chemical Changes

Physical change: only physical properties change, no new substance, usually reversible. Melting ice, dissolving sugar, stretching rubber.

Chemical change: new substance forms, usually irreversible, with energy, colour, gas or precipitate changes. Rusting, burning paper, curdling milk, cooking food.

Burning of candle has both: wax melting (physical) + wax burning (chemical).

6. Types of Pure Substances

Element: simplest form, cannot be broken into simpler substances by chemical means (Lavoisier). Over 118 known. Metals: lustre, malleable, ductile, conduct heat and electricity, sonorous (Fe, Cu, Au). Non-metals: dull, brittle, poor conductors (C, S, O). Metalloids: intermediate properties (B, Si, Ge). Mercury – liquid metal; Bromine – liquid non-metal.

Compound: two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio, with entirely new properties. Eg: H₂O (H:O = 1:8 by mass), CO₂, NaCl.

Short Answer Questions

Q1. What is a pure substance?
✍ A substance made of only one kind of particle with fixed properties.
Q2. Differentiate solute and solvent.
✍ Solute is dissolved component (less); solvent dissolves it (more).
Q3. Why does a colloid show Tyndall effect but a true solution does not?
✍ Colloid particles are large enough (1–100 nm) to scatter light; solution particles are too small.
Q4. What is Brownian motion?
✍ Zig-zag random movement of colloidal particles due to collisions with medium particles.
Q5. Name the method to separate cream from milk.
✍ Centrifugation.
Q6. What is an alloy?
✍ A homogeneous mixture of metals, or metals with a non-metal.
Q7. Calculate mass % of 10 g sugar in 90 g water.
✍ 10/(10+90) × 100 = 10%.
Q8. Why is crystallisation better than evaporation?
✍ Impurities stay in solution and solid does not decompose or char.
Q9. Which technique separates different colours of ink?
✍ Chromatography.
Q10. What are metalloids? Give two examples.
✍ Elements with properties of both metals and non-metals: boron, silicon.
Q11. Why is air a mixture?
✍ It has varying composition of N₂, O₂, CO₂ etc., components keep their properties and can be separated physically.
Q12. Give two uses of fractional distillation.
✍ Separating gases of air and petroleum fractions.

Long Answer Questions

Q1. Differentiate between solution, colloid and suspension.
✍ Solution: homogeneous, <1 nm, no Tyndall effect, no settling, passes through filter. Colloid: appears homogeneous, 1–100 nm, shows Tyndall effect, stable, passes filter paper, separated by centrifugation. Suspension: heterogeneous, >100 nm, visible, scatters light, settles, filtered.
Q2. Describe simple distillation with a labelled diagram.
✍ See Fig 2. The mixture is heated in a flask; the component with lower boiling point vaporises first, is read on the thermometer, then passes into a water-cooled condenser (cold water enters from bottom, exits at top), liquefies and is collected as distillate in the receiver.
Q3. How can you separate a mixture of common salt, sand, and ammonium chloride?
✍ 1) Heat: NH₄Cl sublimes and is collected by cooling. 2) Add water to residue and stir. 3) Filter: sand remains. 4) Evaporate filtrate to get salt.
Q4. Explain the difference between a compound and a mixture with an example.
✍ See the table in Section 6. Eg: iron + sulphur powder mix can be separated by a magnet, but heating forms iron sulphide, a compound with new properties not attracted by the magnet.
Q5. Explain how chromatography works and its applications.
✍ A spot of ink is placed on a baseline on paper and its end dipped in solvent. The solvent rises and carries the colours with different speeds depending on solubility, thus separating them. Used to separate dyes, drugs, pigments.
