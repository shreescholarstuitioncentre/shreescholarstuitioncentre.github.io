Class 10 Science – Chapter 9: Light – Reflection and Refraction

Sources covered: NCERT, Balaji, Rajeev Publications, UP Board pattern

1. Reflection of Light

Light travels in a straight line in a transparent medium (rectilinear propagation). When light falls on a polished surface it is thrown back into the same medium: this is reflection. Polished metal surfaces and mirrors reflect most of the light falling on them.
Terms: incident ray (ray falling), reflected ray (ray returning), normal (perpendicular to the surface at the point of incidence), angle of incidence i and angle of reflection r (both measured from the normal).
Laws of reflection: (1) angle of incidence = angle of reflection (i = r); (2) the incident ray, the normal and the reflected ray all lie in the same plane. These laws hold for all types of reflecting surfaces, including curved ones.
Regular and diffuse reflection: a smooth surface gives regular reflection (parallel rays stay parallel, image is formed); a rough surface scatters light in all directions (diffuse reflection), so no image is seen, though each ray still obeys the laws.

2. Image Formed by a Plane Mirror

Properties: (1) virtual and erect; (2) the same size as the object; (3) formed as far behind the mirror as the object is in front; (4) laterally inverted (left becomes right), which is why AMBULANCE is written reversed in front of vehicles so that it reads correctly in a rear-view mirror. Real vs virtual image: a real image is formed when reflected (or refracted) rays actually meet; it can be obtained on a screen and is inverted. A virtual image is formed when rays only appear to meet (back-extensions); it cannot be obtained on a screen and is erect.

3. Spherical Mirrors

A spherical mirror is a part of a hollow sphere whose one surface is polished. If the inner (curved-in) surface reflects, it is a concave mirror; if the outer (bulging) surface reflects, it is a convex mirror. Focus: in a concave mirror, parallel rays converge at a real principal focus F in front; in a convex mirror they diverge and appear to come from a virtual focus F behind the mirror. f = R/2 (for small aperture).
Rules for ray diagrams (any two rays are enough): (1) A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through F after reflection (or appears to come from F in a convex mirror). (2) A ray passing through F (or directed towards F) becomes parallel to the axis after reflection. (3) A ray through C (or directed to C) falls normally and retraces its path. (4) A ray hitting the pole P is reflected making equal angles with the axis.

4. Sign Convention (New Cartesian)

Rules: (1) The object is always placed on the left of the mirror/lens, so light falls from the left. (2) All distances are measured from the pole P (mirror) or optical centre O (lens). (3) Distances measured along the direction of the incident light (to the right of the origin) are positive; against it (left) are negative. (4) Heights measured upwards, perpendicular to the principal axis, are positive; downwards are negative.

5. Image Formation by a Concave Mirror (all six cases)

The position, size and nature of the image depend on the position of the object. Watch each animation: the rays are drawn one by one, then the image appears. Solid arrow = real image, dashed arrow = virtual image.
Parallel rays from a distant object (the Sun) reflect through the focus F; ray 1 and ray 2 meet there, so the image is a bright point. Use: solar furnaces; finding the focal length by the Sun's image on a screen.
Ray 1 reflects through F, ray 2 hits the pole and reflects at an equal angle, ray 3 (through F) reflects parallel. They meet between F and C: a small, real, inverted image. Use: large concave mirrors of reflecting telescopes.
Object at C: the image forms at C itself, the same size and inverted (v = u = 2f, m = −1). It can be caught on a screen.
Object between C and F: the image forms beyond C, real, inverted and enlarged. Use: projection arrangements that need enlarged real images.
Object at F: the reflected rays are parallel, so the image is at infinity, highly enlarged. Use: torches, car headlights and searchlights, where the bulb is kept at the focus to send a strong parallel beam.
Object between P and F: reflected rays diverge, but their dashed back-extensions meet behind the mirror, giving a virtual, erect, enlarged image. Use: shaving and make-up mirrors, dentists' mirrors.

6. Image Formation by a Convex Mirror

A convex mirror always forms a virtual, erect, diminished image behind the mirror, whatever the position of the object.
Parallel rays diverge after reflection; their back-extensions appear to start from F behind the mirror: a virtual point image at F.
For every position of the object, the image is virtual, erect and diminished, between P and F behind the mirror. Use: rear-view mirrors of vehicles (wide field of view with an erect image), shop security mirrors.

7. Mirror Formula and Magnification

Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f    (R = 2f)
Magnification: m = h'/h = −v/u
Notes: m is negative for real inverted images and positive for virtual erect images; |m| > 1 means enlarged, |m| < 1 means diminished, |m| = 1 means same size. Always substitute u, v and f with proper signs. A convex mirror has f positive and m always between 0 and 1. See solved numericals in the Q&A below.

8. Refraction of Light

Refraction is the bending of light when it passes obliquely from one transparent medium to another, because the speed of light changes. Examples: a pencil partly in water looks bent; a pool looks shallower; a coin in a cup appears raised when water is poured.
Optical density: a medium with a higher refractive index is optically denser; the speed of light is lower in it. Light bends towards the normal when it goes from a rarer to a denser medium and away from the normal from a denser to a rarer medium. Along the normal (i = 0) light goes undeviated.
Laws of refraction: (1) the incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane; (2) Snell's law: for light of a given colour and a given pair of media, sin i / sin r = constant (the refractive index).
Refractive index n = sin i / sin r   ;   absolute n = c / v   (c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum)
Refractive index of medium 2 with respect to 1: n₂₁ = v₁ / v₂
It has no unit. Typical values:

Glass slab: the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray because the bending on entry is undone on exit, but it is laterally displaced. The displacement increases with thickness and angle of incidence and with the refractive index.

9. Spherical Lenses

A lens is a transparent material bounded by two surfaces, at least one of them spherical. A convex (converging) lens is thicker in the middle and bulges outwards; a concave (diverging) lens is thinner in the middle.
Terms: optical centre O (central point; a ray through it goes undeviated); principal axis; principal foci F₁ and F₂ (one on each side); focal length f (distance OF); centres of curvature (centres of the two spheres; 2F₁ and 2F₂ are marked at twice the focal length); aperture (effective diameter).
Rules for ray diagrams: (1) A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through F₂ after refraction in a convex lens (appears to come from F₁ in a concave lens). (2) A ray through the optical centre O passes straight without deviation. (3) A ray passing through F₁ (convex) emerges parallel to the axis; in a concave lens a ray directed towards F₂ emerges parallel.

10. Image Formation by a Convex Lens (all six cases)

Rays are drawn one by one; the dashed lines are virtual back-extensions.
Parallel rays converge at F₂ after refraction: a real, point-sized image. Use: finding focal length with the Sun; burning paper with a lens.
Ray 1 goes through F₂, ray 2 passes straight through O, ray 3 through F₁ emerges parallel. They meet between F₂ and 2F₂: real, inverted and diminished. Use: camera lens, human eye.
Object at 2F₁: image at 2F₂, real, inverted and the same size (v = u = 2f, m = −1). Use: photocopiers making same-size copies.
Object between F₁ and 2F₁: image beyond 2F₂, real, inverted and enlarged. Use: film and slide projectors, objective of a compound microscope.
Object at F₁: refracted rays are parallel, so the image is at infinity, highly enlarged. Use: searchlights and lighthouse lamps.
Object between F₁ and O: refracted rays diverge, but their dashed back-extensions meet on the same side as the object: a virtual, erect, enlarged image. Use: magnifying glass, watch repairers, reading fine print.

11. Image Formation by a Concave Lens

A concave lens always forms a virtual, erect and diminished image on the same side as the object, between F₁ and O.
Parallel rays diverge after refraction; back-extensions meet at F₁: a virtual point image.
For every position of the object, a concave lens forms a virtual, erect and diminished image between F₁ and O. Use: spectacles for myopia (short-sightedness), door peepholes.

12. Lens Formula, Magnification and Power

Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f
Magnification: m = h'/h = v/u
Power: P = 1/f (f in metres), unit dioptre (D)
Combination: P = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …
1 dioptre is the power of a lens of focal length 1 metre. Power of a convex lens is positive, of a concave lens negative. A lens of short focal length bends light more (greater power). Opticians use the total power of lens combinations (for example in spectacles and cameras).

13. Important Activities (NCERT)

1. Focal length of a concave mirror: face a distant object (the Sun) and move a paper screen until a sharp bright image forms; the distance from the mirror to the screen is f.
2. Focal length of a convex lens: focus the Sun on a paper with the lens; the distance lens-paper is f (do not look at the Sun; paper may burn).
3. Plane mirror: place a pin and find its image by parallax; image distance equals object distance.
4. Refraction in water: put a coin at the bottom of a cup and move away until it just disappears; pour water slowly and it reappears.
5. Glass slab: trace the incident and emergent rays with pins and measure the lateral displacement.
6. Image formation by a convex lens: place a candle at positions beyond 2F, at 2F, between F and 2F, at F and inside F; get the image on a screen each time.

Short Answer Questions

Q1. State the laws of reflection of light.(1) The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. (2) The incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence and the reflected ray lie in the same plane.
Q2. What is lateral inversion? Give an example.The reversal of left and right in a plane mirror image. The word AMBULANCE is written reversed on the vehicle so that it reads correctly in a rear-view mirror.
Q3. State four properties of the image formed by a plane mirror.Virtual, erect, same size as the object, formed behind the mirror at the same distance as the object, and laterally inverted.
Q4. Define the principal focus of a concave mirror.The point on the principal axis at which rays parallel to the axis meet after reflection from the mirror.
Q5. What is the relation between focal length and radius of curvature?f = R/2, i.e. focal length is half the radius of curvature.
Q6. Name the mirror used by dentists and give the reason.A concave mirror, because when the tooth is within the focal length it forms an enlarged, erect, virtual image.
Q7. Why is a convex mirror used as a rear-view mirror?It always gives an erect, diminished image and covers a wide field of view, so the driver sees more traffic behind.
Q8. Why do we use a concave mirror in torches and headlights?The bulb is placed at the focus, so the reflected rays form a strong, parallel beam that travels far.
Q9. What kind of image does a concave mirror form when the object is at C?A real, inverted image of the same size, formed at C.
Q10. Where should an object be placed in front of a concave mirror to get a virtual, enlarged and erect image?Between the pole P and the focus F.
Q11. What is refraction of light? Why does it occur?Bending of light when it passes from one transparent medium to another; it occurs because the speed of light changes in different media.
Q12. State Snell's law.For a given colour of light and given pair of media, the ratio sin i / sin r is a constant, called the refractive index.
Q13. Define refractive index. Does it have a unit?The ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to the speed in the medium (n = c/v). It has no unit as it is a ratio.
Q14. How does light bend when it passes from air to glass?Towards the normal, since glass is optically denser.
Q15. Light travels fastest in which medium: water, glass or diamond? Why?In water. It has the lowest refractive index (1.33) among the three, and speed v = c/n.
Q16. What is meant by optical centre of a lens?The central point of a lens through which a ray passes without any deviation.
Q17. Why is a convex lens called a converging lens?It converges parallel rays to a point (principal focus) after refraction.
Q18. Define 1 dioptre of power.The power of a lens whose focal length is 1 metre.
Q19. Which lens is used as a magnifying glass? Where should the object be placed?A convex lens of small focal length, with the object between F₁ and O (within the focal length).
Q20. What is the nature of image formed by a concave lens?Always virtual, erect and diminished, on the same side as the object, between F₁ and O.

Long Answer Questions (with numericals)

Q1. Describe the image formation by a concave mirror for all positions of the object (with nature, position and size).Object at infinity: image at F, real, inverted, highly diminished. Beyond C: between F and C, real, inverted, diminished. At C: at C, real, inverted, same size. Between C and F: beyond C, real, inverted, enlarged. At F: at infinity, real, inverted, highly enlarged. Between P and F: behind the mirror, virtual, erect, enlarged. These come from tracing a ray parallel to the axis (through F), a ray through F (parallel after reflection) and a ray via C or P.
Q2. Describe the image formed by a convex mirror and write its uses.For every position of the object the convex mirror forms a virtual, erect and diminished image behind the mirror. At infinity the image is at F and point-sized; for an object anywhere else the image lies between P and F. Uses: rear-view mirrors of vehicles (erect image and wide field of view), security mirrors in shops and on road bends.
Q3. State the laws of refraction. Draw the path of a ray through a rectangular glass slab and explain lateral displacement.Laws: incident ray, refracted ray and normal lie in one plane; sin i / sin r = constant (Snell's law). In a glass slab the ray bends towards the normal on entering (r < i) and away from the normal on leaving, so the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray (e = i) but shifted sideways. The perpendicular distance between the emergent ray and the extended incident ray is the lateral displacement; it grows with the slab thickness, the angle of incidence and the refractive index.
Q4. Describe image formation by a convex lens for all positions of the object.At infinity: at F₂, real, inverted, highly diminished. Beyond 2F₁: between F₂ and 2F₂, real, inverted, diminished. At 2F₁: at 2F₂, real, inverted, same size. Between F₁ and 2F₁: beyond 2F₂, real, inverted, enlarged. At F₁: at infinity, real, inverted, highly enlarged. Between F₁ and O: same side as the object, virtual, erect, enlarged (magnifying glass). Rays used: parallel to the axis, through the optical centre, through F₁.
Q5. What happens when light passes through a concave lens? Give its nature of images and uses.A concave lens diverges parallel rays so that they appear to come from F₁. For any object position the image is virtual, erect and diminished, between F₁ and O. Uses: spectacles for myopia, door peepholes, combinations in optical instruments.
Q6. Define power of a lens. Find the power of a lens of focal length −25 cm, and of a combination of +2 D and −4 D lenses.Power P = 1/f (metres), unit dioptre. For f = −25 cm = −0.25 m: P = 1/(−0.25) = −4 D (concave). Combination: P = +2 + (−4) = −2 D; its focal length f = 1/P = −0.5 m = −50 cm, so the combination acts as a concave lens.
Q7. Numerical. An object 4 cm high is placed 15 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 10 cm. Find the image position, nature and size.u = −15 cm, f = −10 cm, h = 4 cm. 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = −1/10 + 1/15 = −1/30, so v = −30 cm (30 cm in front of the mirror, real). m = −v/u = −(−30)/(−15) = −2. h' = m·h = −8 cm. The image is real, inverted, 8 cm tall (enlarged), at 30 cm from the mirror.
Q8. Numerical. A 4 cm object is 25 cm from a convex mirror of focal length 20 cm. Find the image distance and height.u = −25 cm, f = +20 cm. 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = 1/20 + 1/25 = 9/100, so v = +11.1 cm (behind the mirror). m = −v/u = −(11.1)/(−25) = +0.44. h' = 0.44 × 4 = +1.78 cm. The image is virtual, erect and diminished (1.78 cm).
Q9. Numerical. A concave mirror has focal length 15 cm. Where should an object be placed to get an erect image three times as large?f = −15 cm; erect enlarged image means m = +3 = −v/u, so v = −3u. Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f gives −1/(3u) + 1/u = 2/(3u) = −1/15, so u = −10 cm. The object must be 10 cm in front of the mirror (within f); then v = +30 cm behind the mirror.
Q10. Numerical. A convex lens of focal length 10 cm has an object 15 cm from it. Find v and the magnification.u = −15 cm, f = +10 cm. 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/10 − 1/15 = 1/30, so v = +30 cm (real image on the other side). m = v/u = 30/(−15) = −2: the image is inverted and twice the size.
Q11. Numerical. A concave lens of focal length 15 cm has an object 30 cm from it. Where is the image and what is its magnification?u = −30 cm, f = −15 cm. 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = −1/15 − 1/30 = −3/30 = −1/10, so v = −10 cm (virtual, on the object side). m = v/u = (−10)/(−30) = +1/3: erect and one-third the size of the object.
Q12. Numerical. The speed of light in glass is 2 × 10⁸ m/s. Find the refractive index of glass. Also find the speed of light in diamond (n = 2.42) and the angle of refraction for light entering glass (n = 1.5) at 30°.n(glass) = c/v = 3×10⁸ / 2×10⁸ = 1.5. In diamond v = c/n = 3×10⁸/2.42 = 1.24×10⁸ m/s. Snell's law: sin r = sin i / n = sin 30° / 1.5 = 0.5/1.5 = 0.333, so r ≈ 19.5° (towards the normal).
Q13. Explain why a pencil partly dipped in water appears bent and a pool appears shallower.Light from the part under water comes out of the denser water into rarer air and bends away from the normal. To the eye the rays appear to come from a point higher than the actual one, so the immersed part looks raised and the pencil looks bent at the water surface. For the same reason the apparent depth of a pool is less than the real depth (about real depth / 1.33).
