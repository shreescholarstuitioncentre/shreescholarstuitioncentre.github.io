Class 10 Science – Chapter 10: The Human Eye and the Colourful World

Sources covered: NCERT, Balaji, Rajeev Publications, UP Board pattern

1. The Human Eye

The human eye is like a camera. Light enters through the cornea and pupil, is focused by the eye lens and forms a real, inverted image on the retina. The retina's light-sensitive cells (rods for dim light, cones for bright light and colour) send electrical signals through the optic nerve to the brain, which interprets the image as erect.

Working: most of the refraction occurs at the outer surface of the cornea; the eye lens only fine-tunes the focal length so that the image of objects at different distances forms on the retina. The distance from lens to retina is fixed, so the lens changes its focal length instead of the image distance.
Pupil and iris: the iris controls the size of the pupil: in bright light the pupil contracts, in dim light it dilates, protecting the retina and helping vision. Persistence of vision: the image on the retina stays for about 1/16 second after the object is removed. Therefore a series of still pictures shown quickly (as in a film) seems to move. Two eyes give a wider field of view (about 150° with one eye, about 180° with two) and help to judge distance.

2. Power of Accommodation

The ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length (by the ciliary muscles changing the lens curvature) so as to focus both distant and near objects on the retina is the power of accommodation. Distant object: ciliary muscles relax, lens becomes thinner, focal length increases. Near object: ciliary muscles contract, lens becomes thicker, focal length decreases.
Near point: the minimum distance at which the eye can see an object clearly without strain, about 25 cm for a young adult (least distance of distinct vision). Far point: the farthest point the eye can see clearly: infinity for a normal eye. So the range of normal vision is 25 cm to infinity. A normal eye cannot see objects closer than 25 cm clearly because the ciliary muscles cannot make the lens thick enough, and trying causes eye strain.

3. Defects of Vision and Their Correction

Eye defects arise when the eye loses its power of accommodation or when the eyeball shape/lens power is wrong. Three common refractive defects:
(a) Myopia (near-sightedness): a person sees near objects clearly but distant objects are blurred. Cause: excessive curvature of the cornea/lens (short focal length) or elongation of the eyeball. The image of a distant object forms in front of the retina. The far point is nearer than infinity. Correction: a concave lens of suitable power (negative). (b) Hypermetropia (far-sightedness): distant objects are seen clearly but near objects are blurred. Cause: eye lens focal length too long or eyeball too small. The image of a near object forms behind the retina; the near point is farther than 25 cm. Correction: a convex lens of suitable power (positive). (c) Presbyopia: with age the ciliary muscles weaken and the lens becomes less flexible, so the near point recedes; some people have both myopia and hypermetropia. Correction: bifocal lenses (concave upper part for distance, convex lower part for reading). Cataract: the eye lens of old people becomes milky and cloudy, giving partial or complete loss of vision; it is treated by surgery (replacing the lens by an artificial one).
Power of the corrective lens: P = 1/f (in metres). Myopia: use f = −(far point distance). Hypermetropia: use the lens formula with u = −25 cm and v = −(near point of the person). Eye care: avoid reading in poor light, rest the eyes, eat vitamin A rich food, and get regular check-ups; donate eyes after death, since corneal transplant can restore vision to blind persons.

4. Refraction of Light through a Prism

A triangular glass prism has two triangular bases and three rectangular lateral surfaces inclined to each other; the angle between two refracting surfaces is the angle of prism A. A ray entering one face bends towards the normal, and on leaving the second face bends away from the normal, so it is deviated towards the base. The angle between the direction of the incident ray and the emergent ray is the angle of deviation δ. Terms: incident ray, refracted ray, emergent ray, angle of incidence i, angle of refraction r, angle of emergence e. Unlike a slab, the emergent ray is not parallel to the incident ray.

5. Dispersion of White Light

The splitting of white light into its component colours on passing through a prism is called dispersion; the band of colours is the spectrum: Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, Red (VIBGYOR). Why does dispersion occur? Different colours travel at different speeds in glass, so they have different refractive indices and bend by different amounts: red bends the least (highest speed, least deviation) and violet the most. In vacuum all colours travel at the same speed.
Newton's experiment: Newton used a second identical prism, inverted, to recombine the spectrum into white light. This proves that sunlight is made of seven colours and that the prism does not add colour.

6. Rainbow

A rainbow is a natural spectrum in the sky after a rain shower, caused by dispersion of sunlight by tiny water droplets in the atmosphere. Each drop acts like a small prism: sunlight is refracted and dispersed on entering, internally reflected inside, and refracted again on leaving, so the colours emerge at slightly different angles.
Conditions: the Sun must be behind the observer and water drops in front. Red appears at the outer edge (about 42°) and violet at the inner edge (about 40°). Rainbow is an arc, because the drops at the same angle from the line through the Sun and the observer form a circle (circular arc); each drop sends a different colour to the eye from different heights.

7. Atmospheric Refraction

The earth's atmosphere has layers of air with different optical densities (denser nearer the ground), so light from a source bends again and again towards the normal as it travels through the atmosphere. This is atmospheric refraction.
(a) Twinkling of stars: as the apparent position of the star keeps changing slightly because of changing air density (turbulent hot and cold air), the light reaching the eye varies in amount; the star seems to flicker. Stars are point sources of light, so even small changes have a large effect. Why do planets not twinkle? Planets are nearer, so they are extended sources made of many point sources; the variations in light from these points average out, giving steady light.
(b) Advance sunrise and delayed sunset: because of atmospheric refraction the Sun is visible about 2 minutes before the actual sunrise and about 2 minutes after the actual sunset. The apparent flattening of the solar disc at sunrise and sunset is due to the same reason.

8. Scattering of Light

When light falls on very small particles it is scattered (sent in different directions).
Tyndall effect: the scattering of light by colloidal particles makes the path of a light beam visible. Examples: a sunbeam through a room with dust or smoke; sunlight through a canopy of a dense forest with mist. A true solution does not show it, because its particles are too small.
Colour of scattered light depends on the size of the scattering particles: very fine particles scatter mainly blue light (shorter wavelength) while larger particles scatter longer wavelengths; if particles are large enough, scattered light may even look white (as in clouds).
Colour of the sky: air molecules and fine particles are smaller than the wavelength of visible light and scatter blue (short wavelength) light much more than red (scattering is proportional to 1/λ⁴). So the sky appears blue. At the sunrise and sunset the Sun is near the horizon and light travels a long path through the atmosphere, so most of the blue and shorter wavelengths are scattered away, and the red reaches us, making the Sun and sky red. Other points: (1) To an astronaut the sky appears dark because there is no atmosphere in space to scatter light. (2) Danger signals are red, because red is scattered the least by fog or smoke and therefore can be seen from far away. (3) Clouds look white because water droplets are large and scatter all colours equally.

9. Important Activities (NCERT)

1. Prism spectrum: pass a narrow beam of sunlight through a glass prism and catch the spectrum on a white screen.
2. Recombination: place a second prism upside down next to the first; the spectrum combines into white light.
3. Artificial rainbow: stand with your back to the Sun and spray water from a hose or use a fine mist; a small rainbow appears.
4. Tyndall effect: shine a torch beam through a glass of water mixed with a few drops of milk; the beam is seen; with clear water it is not.
5. Scattering at sunset: add a pinch of sodium thiosulphate and a little dilute acid to water and pass light; the colour changes from blue to orange-red as particles grow.
6. Near and far points: bring a printed page slowly towards your eye until it becomes blurred to find your near point.

Long Answer Questions (with numericals)

Q1. Describe the structure of the human eye with a labelled description and explain how it works.The eye is a spherical organ. Light enters through the transparent cornea (which does most of the refraction), passes through the aqueous humour and the pupil (whose size is controlled by the iris), and the flexible eye lens focuses it through the vitreous humour on the retina. The retina has rods (dim light) and cones (colour, bright light) which send electrical signals through the optic nerve to the brain. Ciliary muscles change the focal length of the lens to focus objects at different distances. The image on the retina is real and inverted and the brain interprets it as erect.
Q2. What is the power of accommodation? Explain how the eye focuses near and distant objects.The adjustment of the eye lens to focus objects at different distances is called accommodation. For distant objects the ciliary muscles relax, the lens becomes thin and its focal length is large. For near objects the ciliary muscles contract, the lens becomes thicker and the focal length decreases. The lens-to-retina distance remains fixed. The least distance of distinct vision is about 25 cm; the far point is infinity.
Q3. Explain myopia: its causes, nature of the defect, ray diagram and correction.Myopia (near-sightedness) occurs when the cornea/lens is too curved (short focal length) or the eyeball is too long. Parallel rays from a distant object are focused in front of the retina, so distant objects look blurred while near objects are seen clearly. A concave lens of suitable focal length placed before the eye diverges the rays slightly, so that the eye lens forms the image on the retina. The power of the lens is negative: f = −(far point distance).
Q4. Explain hypermetropia: its causes, ray diagram and correction.Hypermetropia (far-sightedness) occurs when the eye lens has a long focal length or the eyeball is too short. Rays from a near object would converge behind the retina, so near objects are blurred while distant objects are seen. A convex lens of suitable focal length forms a virtual image of the near object at the person's near point, so the eye can focus it on the retina. The power is positive.
Q5. What is presbyopia? How do cataract and presbyopia differ? Suggest care for the eyes.Presbyopia is the age-related weakening of the ciliary muscles and loss of flexibility of the lens, so the near point recedes and near objects are blurred; it is corrected by bifocal lenses. Cataract is the clouding of the eye lens, treated by surgery. Eye care: do not read in dim light, avoid strain, eat vitamin A rich food, wash eyes, regular check-ups, donate eyes.
Q6. Explain refraction of light through a prism and define angle of deviation.When light enters one face of the glass prism it bends towards the normal and on leaving the second face it bends away from the normal. The emergent ray is therefore deviated towards the base. The angle between the direction of the incident ray and the emergent ray is the angle of deviation. Unlike a rectangular slab, the emergent ray is not parallel to the incident ray.
Q7. What is dispersion of white light? Describe Newton's experiment.Dispersion is the splitting of white light into VIBGYOR on passing through a prism, because different colours have different speeds in glass, hence different refractive indices; red bends the least and violet the most. Newton placed a second prism, inverted, in the path of the spectrum; it recombined all colours into white light, showing the prism does not create colours but only separates them.
Q8. How is a rainbow formed? State the conditions for seeing a rainbow.Raindrops act like small prisms. Sunlight entering a drop is refracted and dispersed, internally reflected at the back of the drop, and refracted again as it emerges, so each colour leaves at a slightly different angle. The observer sees red from drops at a higher position and violet from lower ones, giving a coloured arc with red outside and violet inside. Conditions: Sun behind the observer, drops in the air in front, low Sun angle.
Q9. Explain atmospheric refraction with twinkling of stars and advance sunrise.The atmosphere has layers of increasing optical density downward, so light bends towards the normal repeatedly and the apparent position of a star is slightly higher than the actual one. As the air density keeps varying, the apparent position and the amount of light received keep changing, so stars twinkle. Because of this refraction the Sun is seen about 2 minutes before the actual sunrise and about 2 minutes after the actual sunset.
Q10. What is the Tyndall effect? Why is the sky blue and the Sun red at sunrise?Tyndall effect is the scattering of light by colloidal particles which makes the beam visible. Scattering is greater for shorter wavelengths (proportional to 1/λ⁴): blue is scattered much more than red by the small air particles, so the sky appears blue. At sunrise or sunset the light travels a long path through the atmosphere, most of the blue is scattered away and red reaches the observer, so the Sun and the sky near it look red.
Q11. Numerical. The far point of a myopic person is 80 cm in front of the eye. What is the nature and power of the lens required to correct the problem?Parallel rays must appear to come from the far point: u = −∞, v = −80 cm. 1/f = 1/v − 1/u = −1/80 − 0 = −1/80 so f = −80 cm = −0.8 m. P = 1/f = −1.25 D. A concave lens of power −1.25 D is required.
Q12. Numerical. The near point of a hypermetropic eye is 1 m. What is the focal length and power of the lens required to correct this defect? (Near point of a normal eye = 25 cm.)The lens must form a virtual image at 1 m of an object at 25 cm: u = −25 cm, v = −100 cm. 1/f = 1/v − 1/u = −1/100 + 1/25 = 3/100, so f = +33.3 cm = 0.333 m. P = 1/f = +3.0 D. A convex lens of focal length 33.3 cm (power +3 D) is needed.
Q13. Numerical. A person needs a lens of power −5.5 D for distant vision and +1.5 D for near vision. Find the focal lengths of the two lenses.(i) Distant vision: f = 1/P = 1/(−5.5) = −0.1818 m = −18.2 cm (concave). (ii) Near vision: f = 1/(+1.5) = +0.667 m = +66.7 cm (convex). Such a person needs a bifocal lens.
Q14. Numerical. A myopic person has a far point at 50 cm. Which lens, and of what power, will let him see distant objects?The lens must form the image of a distant object at his far point: v = −50 cm, u = −∞. f = −50 cm = −0.5 m. P = −2.0 D. A concave lens of power −2 D is required.
