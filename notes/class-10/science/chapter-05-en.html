Class 10 Science – Chapter 5: Life Processes

Sources covered: NCERT, Balaji, Rajeev Publications, UP Board pattern

1. What are Life Processes?

Basic functions performed by living organisms to maintain life and repair, growth and energy: nutrition, respiration, transport and excretion (plus control, coordination and reproduction later). Living things need energy from outside sources (food) to prevent molecular movement from stopping and the body from breaking down.

2. Nutrition

Autotrophic nutrition: organisms (green plants) make their own food from CO2 and water using sunlight and chlorophyll by photosynthesis:
6CO2 + 12H2O → (chlorophyll, sunlight) → C6H12O6 + 6O2 + 6H2O
Steps: (1) absorption of light by chlorophyll; (2) conversion of light energy to chemical energy and splitting of water into H and O; (3) reduction of CO2 to carbohydrates. Extra glucose is stored as starch. Sunlight, chlorophyll, CO2 and water are essential. Desert plants fix CO2 at night.

Stomata: tiny pores on leaves for gas exchange and transpiration. Each is surrounded by guard cells: when water flows in, they swell and the pore opens; when water leaves, it closes.
How it works (watch the animation): guard cells are bean-shaped and have chloroplasts. When water flows in by osmosis they swell and curve apart, so the pore opens: CO2 enters for photosynthesis, while O2 and water vapour leave. When water leaves they become flaccid and the pore closes (night or water shortage), saving water.

Heterotrophic nutrition: depend on others for food. Holozoic: ingestion → digestion → absorption → assimilation → egestion (humans, Amoeba). Saprophytic: break down dead and decaying matter outside the body (fungi: bread mould, yeast, mushrooms). Parasitic: live on a host and take food (Cuscuta, tapeworm, lice, Plasmodium).
Amoeba engulfs food with pseudopodia (false feet) forming a food vacuole, digests with enzymes, absorbs, and expels waste by rupturing the cell surface. Paramecium takes food with cilia from a specific spot.
Steps shown: (1) pseudopodia stretch out around the food particle; (2) the food is engulfed in a food vacuole; (3) enzymes digest it (the particle shrinks); (4) digested food diffuses into the cytoplasm; (5) undigested part is thrown out when the cell surface ruptures. The contractile vacuole pumps out excess water. Amoeba has no fixed mouth.

Path: mouth → pharynx → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine → anus. Food moves by peristalsis (rhythmic contraction). Teeth chew; tongue mixes saliva. Oesophagus has no digestive role.

Small intestine is the longest part (about 6.5 m), the site of complete digestion and absorption. Its inner wall has finger-like villi that increase surface area for absorption and have blood vessels. Large intestine absorbs water; the rest passes out by the anus. Dental caries: bacteria act on sugar, produce acid that dissolves enamel; brush after meals to prevent it.

Colour key: liver brown, gall bladder green, stomach red, pancreas yellow, small intestine orange, large intestine grey-brown. Digestion ends in the small intestine; only water is absorbed in the large intestine.

4. Respiration

Breakdown of food (glucose) to release energy, stored as ATP (energy currency) that is used for muscle contraction, protein synthesis, conduction of nerve impulses.
Aerobic: Glucose (6C) → Pyruvate (3C) in cytoplasm → CO2 + H2O + energy (in mitochondria, 38 ATP)
Anaerobic (yeast): Glucose → Pyruvate → Ethanol + CO2 + energy (2 ATP)
Anaerobic (muscle cells): Glucose → Pyruvate → Lactic acid + energy (cramps)
Reading the chart: glycolysis in the cytoplasm splits glucose (6C) into pyruvate (3C) and releases a little energy. If oxygen is present, pyruvate goes to the mitochondria and is broken down fully to CO2 and water (38 ATP in all). Without oxygen it is changed to ethanol and CO2 in yeast (fermentation) or to lactic acid in tired muscles (cramps). The dots show the flow of each path.
Human respiratory system: nostrils → nasal cavity (hair, mucus filter air) → pharynx → larynx → trachea (cartilage rings keep it open) → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli (balloon-like, thin, rich blood supply; exchange of gases). Blood carries O2 by haemoglobin (high affinity for O2); CO2 is carried dissolved in plasma as it is more soluble.
Left: air travels nostrils → nasal cavity → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli (blue dots in, orange dots out). Cartilage rings keep the trachea open. Right (zoomed alveolus): O2 diffuses from the alveolus into blood in the capillary, and CO2 diffuses from blood into the alveolus to be breathed out. Millions of thin-walled alveoli give a huge surface area for exchange.
Breathing: Inhalation: ribs lift up and out, diaphragm flattens (moves down) → chest volume increases, pressure falls → air rushes in. Exhalation: ribs go down and in, diaphragm domes up → volume decreases → air pushed out. Lungs always have residual air to give time for exchange.
Other organisms: aquatic animals (fish) have gills with a large surface area for dissolved O2; insects have tracheae; plants exchange gases through stomata (leaves), lenticels (stems), and root surface.

5. Transport in Human Beings

Blood: plasma (fluid, transports food, CO2, nitrogenous waste), RBC (haemoglobin carries O2), WBC (fight infection), platelets (clot blood). Blood vessels: arteries (thick, elastic walls, high pressure, away from heart), veins (thin walls, valves, towards heart), capillaries (one cell thick, exchange of materials).
Heart: muscular organ, four chambers: two atria (receive blood) and two ventricles (pump blood; thick walls). Left side has oxygenated blood, right side deoxygenated; they are separated by a septum to avoid mixing, which helps in high-energy needs of warm-blooded animals (birds, mammals).
Double circulation: Blood passes twice through the heart in one cycle. Pulmonary circulation: right ventricle → lungs → left atrium. Systemic circulation: left ventricle → body → right atrium. Fishes and amphibians (2 or 3 chambers) have single or incomplete double circulation.
Blood pressure: force of blood on vessel walls; normal 120 mm Hg (systolic) / 80 mm Hg (diastolic), measured by sphygmomanometer. Hypertension can burst vessels.
Lymph: colourless fluid from tissue spaces, carrying digested fats and filtering germs; flows from tissues to blood.
Heart facts: atria contract first and push blood into ventricles; ventricles then pump blood out. Valves between atria and ventricles and at the vessel exits ensure one-way flow (the white bars in the diagram). The right ventricle wall is thinner than the left, which pumps blood to the whole body. Resting heart beats about 72 times a minute. Veins in the legs also have valves.

6. Transport in Plants

Xylem (dead tubes: vessels and tracheids) carries water and minerals from roots to leaves upward. Driven by transpiration pull: loss of water from stomata creates suction (main force in tall trees); at night root pressure pushes water up. Phloem (living sieve tubes + companion cells) carries food (sucrose) from leaves to all parts, up and down: translocation, using energy (ATP). Transpiration also cools the plant and helps absorb water.
Reading the diagram: blue dots rise in xylem from root hairs to leaves (transpiration pull + root pressure), light blue dots leave the stomata as vapour; orange dots move in phloem carrying sucrose from leaves to roots, fruits and growing tips (translocation, needs ATP). Xylem cells are dead tubes; phloem has living sieve tubes with companion cells.

7. Excretion

Path of urine: kidney → ureter → urinary bladder (muscular, stretches) → urethra → outside. The renal artery brings blood with urea; the renal vein takes away cleaned blood. Urine leaves when the sphincter relaxes under nervous control. Remember: a man at rest forms about 1–1.5 litres of urine daily.
Removal of harmful metabolic wastes. Human excretory system: pair of kidneys → ureters → urinary bladder → urethra. Nephron (functional unit): glomerulus (capillary cluster) + Bowman's capsule + tubule. Steps: filtration (nitrogenous waste: urea, uric acid, plus glucose, amino acids, salts, water) → selective reabsorption of useful substances by tubule → urine collected in collecting duct → ureter → bladder (stored, then released through urethra). Water reabsorption depends on how much excess water is in the body and how much waste is dissolved.
Nephron steps: (1) Blood enters the glomerulus through the wide afferent arteriole at high pressure, so water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea filter into Bowman's capsule. (2) In the PCT useful substances are reabsorbed (blue dots return to the capillary). (3) Loop of Henle and DCT reabsorb more water as the body needs. (4) What remains is urine, which collects in the collecting duct and flows to the ureter. Each kidney has about a million nephrons.

Artificial kidney (haemodialysis): for kidney failure, blood is drawn from an artery, passed through tubes with dialysing fluid, urea and waste diffuse out, and cleaned blood is returned to a vein. Unlike a kidney, there is no reabsorption.
Working: blood from the patient's artery, with heparin (an anticoagulant), flows through a long coiled cellophane tube (semi-permeable) kept in a tank of dialysing fluid that has the same osmotic pressure as blood but no urea. Urea and wastes diffuse out (yellow dots), and cleaned blood is returned to a vein. A session lasts 3 to 4 hours.

Plants: excess water by transpiration; waste stored in leaves that fall, as resins and gums, and in bark; excreted in soil around roots.

8. Important Activities (NCERT)

1. Test for starch: boil a leaf in water, then in alcohol to remove chlorophyll, rinse and add iodine solution. Blue-black colour shows starch (photosynthesis took place).
2. Light is necessary: cover part of a destarched leaf with black paper and keep in sunlight. Only the uncovered part turns blue-black.
3. CO2 is necessary: keep one destarched plant in a bell jar with KOH (absorbs CO2) and another with water. Only the control leaf shows starch.
4. Chlorophyll is necessary: in a variegated leaf (croton) only the green parts show starch.
5. Fermentation: yeast and sugar in a flask with a delivery tube into lime water; the lime water turns milky due to CO2.
6. CO2 in exhaled air: blow through a tube into lime water; it turns milky faster than when air is pumped in.

Short Answer Questions

Q1. What are life processes?Basic processes (nutrition, respiration, transport, excretion) needed to maintain life.
Q2. Why is the small intestine longer in herbivores than carnivores?Herbivores eat cellulose-rich plants, which take longer to digest, so they need a longer small intestine.
Q3. What is the role of saliva?It moistens food, and salivary amylase breaks starch into sugar.
Q4. Why is oxygenated blood separated from deoxygenated blood in birds and mammals?They need high energy to maintain body temperature. Complete separation supplies more oxygen, so energy is released efficiently.
Q5. What is peristalsis?Rhythmic contraction and relaxation of muscles of the alimentary canal that pushes food forward.
Q6. Name the pigment which carries oxygen in human blood. Where is it found?Haemoglobin; present in red blood cells.
Q7. Why does a stomach not digest itself?Mucus released by the stomach wall protects its lining from HCl and pepsin.
Q8. What is the role of guard cells?They open and close stomatal pores, which controls gas exchange and water loss.
Q9. What is transpiration? State two functions.Loss of water as vapour from aerial parts, mainly through stomata. It creates a suction (transpiration pull) that draws water up the xylem and it cools the plant.
Q10. Why are alveoli thin-walled and surrounded by capillaries?A thin wall and rich blood supply let O2 and CO2 diffuse quickly between air and blood.
Q11. Why do arteries have thick elastic walls while veins have valves?Arteries carry blood at high pressure from the heart; veins carry low-pressure blood, so valves stop it flowing backwards.
Q12. What is the role of bile?Bile from the liver makes food alkaline for pancreatic enzymes and emulsifies fats into small globules.
Q13. Why does the trachea have cartilage rings?They keep the air passage open and stop it from collapsing when air pressure falls.
Q14. Name the substances selectively reabsorbed in the nephron.Glucose, amino acids, salts and water, in amounts the body needs.
Q15. What is the function of villi?Finger-like projections of the small intestine that increase surface area for absorption of digested food.
Q16. What are the products of anaerobic respiration in yeast and in muscle?Yeast: ethanol and CO2. Muscle: lactic acid.

Long Answer Questions

Q1. Describe the process of photosynthesis. What are its raw materials?Raw materials are CO2 (from air via stomata), water (from soil via roots), sunlight and chlorophyll. Steps: light absorption by chlorophyll; light energy converted to chemical energy and water split into H2 and O2; CO2 reduced to carbohydrate. Equation: 6CO2 + 12H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 + 6H2O. Glucose is stored as starch; O2 is released.
Q2. Describe digestion in the human alimentary canal.Mouth: saliva amylase breaks starch to sugar. Oesophagus: peristalsis pushes food. Stomach: gastric glands release HCl, pepsin and mucus; proteins partially digested. Small intestine: bile from the liver emulsifies fats, pancreas supplies amylase, trypsin and lipase, intestinal juice completes digestion into glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, which are absorbed by villi. Large intestine absorbs water; undigested food is egested through the anus.
Q3. Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration.Aerobic needs oxygen; occurs in cytoplasm and mitochondria; end products CO2 and H2O; releases 38 ATP. Anaerobic occurs without oxygen, only in cytoplasm; end products ethanol + CO2 (yeast) or lactic acid (muscles); releases only 2 ATP. Lactic acid build-up causes cramps.
Q4. Describe double circulation in humans with its significance.Blood passes through the heart twice in one complete circuit. Right atrium → right ventricle → lungs (oxygenated) → left atrium → left ventricle → body → right atrium. Significance: complete separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood, efficient oxygen supply, high energy and constant body temperature in birds and mammals.
Q5. Explain the structure and function of the nephron.The nephron has glomerulus (capillary cluster), Bowman's capsule and a long tubule. Blood is filtered at the glomerulus into the capsule. Useful substances (glucose, amino acids, salts, water) are selectively reabsorbed in the tubule. Remaining wastes (urea, excess water and salt) form urine, collected in the collecting duct, ureter and bladder.
Q6. Compare transport of water and food in plants.Xylem: dead cells, carries water and minerals, only upward (roots to leaves), driven mainly by transpiration pull, no energy needed. Phloem: living cells, carries food made in the leaves (sucrose), upward and downward (translocation), needs ATP energy.
Q7. Distinguish between breathing and cellular respiration.Breathing is a physical process of taking air in and out of the lungs and exchanging gases; it releases no energy. Cellular respiration is a chemical process in which glucose is broken down inside cells (cytoplasm and mitochondria) with enzymes to release energy as ATP. Breathing supplies the oxygen that cellular respiration uses.
Q8. Describe the structure of the human heart and the path of blood.The heart has four chambers: right and left atria (receive blood) and right and left ventricles (pump blood; thicker walls). A septum separates right and left sides. Valves prevent backflow. Path: body → vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body.
Q9. How do stomata open and close? Why are they important?Each stoma is bounded by two guard cells. When water enters by osmosis the guard cells swell and the pore opens; when water leaves they shrink and the pore closes. Stomata allow exchange of CO2 and O2 and loss of water vapour (transpiration), and they close at night or in water scarcity to save water.
Q10. Explain how water rises in tall trees.Water enters root hairs by osmosis and moves into the xylem. Root pressure pushes it up for a short distance. In tall plants the main force is transpiration pull: water lost by stomata creates suction, which pulls a continuous water column up the xylem vessels and tracheids because water molecules cling together (cohesion).
Q11. Describe nutrition in Amoeba.Amoeba is a holozoic unicellular organism. It spots food with pseudopodia, which surround and engulf it into a food vacuole. Digestive enzymes break the food down in the vacuole, the digested food diffuses into the cytoplasm for energy and growth, and the undigested remainder is expelled by rupture of the cell surface.
Q12. What is dialysis? Explain the working of an artificial kidney.Dialysis is the cleaning of blood of a patient whose kidneys have failed. Blood from an artery, mixed with heparin, passes through a coiled cellophane tube in a tank of dialysing fluid. Urea and wastes diffuse out through the semi-permeable membrane, and the purified blood is returned to a vein. Unlike a natural kidney, it has no reabsorption.
Q13. Describe gas exchange in the alveoli.Each alveolus is a thin-walled air sac wrapped in blood capillaries. Blood arriving has more CO2 and less O2 than alveolar air. O2 diffuses from the alveolus into the blood and is carried by haemoglobin; CO2 diffuses from the blood into the alveolus and is exhaled.
