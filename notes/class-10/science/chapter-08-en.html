Class 10 Science – Chapter 8: Heredity

Sources covered: NCERT, Balaji, Rajeev Publications, UP Board pattern

1. Accumulation of Variation During Reproduction

Reproduction makes a copy of the DNA, but the copying is never perfectly accurate. Asexual reproduction gives only small variations (copying errors). Sexual reproduction gives much more variation because the DNA of two parents combines in new ways. Variations are inherited by the next generation and accumulate over generations.
Example (beetles): in a colony of red beetles on green bushes, a green variant appears by chance. Crows can see and eat the red beetles more easily, so the green beetles survive and reproduce. As the green trait is inherited, the proportion of green beetles increases generation after generation. A variation that helps survival is thus selected.
Important: different species have different traits; within a species, individuals differ slightly; variations that help in survival become common.

2. Heredity: Inherited Traits

Heredity is the transmission of characters (traits) from parents to offspring through genes. The study of heredity and variation is called genetics. Offspring of the same species look alike (similarity) but are not identical to parents (variation).
Equal contribution of both parents: a child gets half the DNA from the father and half from the mother, so each trait is controlled by two copies (one paternal, one maternal) and both parents contribute equally to the child's traits.
DNA, gene, chromosome: DNA in the nucleus is a double helix. A gene is a segment of DNA that gives the information to make a particular protein, and the protein controls a trait. DNA together with proteins is packed into thread-like chromosomes.
Each cell has pairs of chromosomes (human: 23 pairs = 46). Gametes (sperm and egg) have only one set of 23, and at fertilisation the zygote receives 23 from each parent, restoring 46. Thus the chromosome number of a species stays constant.

3. Mendel and His Experiments

Gregor Johann Mendel (Austrian monk, mid-1800s) is called the father of genetics. He worked on the garden pea (Pisum sativum) and counted offspring of crosses.
Why the pea plant? (1) many contrasting, easily visible traits; (2) short life cycle (a generation in one season); (3) produces many seeds; (4) flowers are bisexual and can self-pollinate, but cross-pollination is easy too.
Mendel selected pairs of contrasting characters and studied them one or two at a time. He found that traits are not blended; one form appears in all F1 plants and the other reappears in F2 in a fixed ratio.

4. Monohybrid Cross (one trait)

A cross involving one pair of contrasting traits, e.g. tall (T) and dwarf (t) pea plants.
Steps: (1) Cross a pure tall (TT) plant with a pure dwarf (tt) plant: all F1 plants are Tt and tall; the dwarf trait seems to disappear. (2) Self-pollinate F1: in F2, three-fourth are tall and one-fourth are dwarf (3 : 1), so the dwarf trait reappears. Genotype ratio: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.

Inference: the trait that appears in F1 (tall) is dominant; the hidden trait (dwarf) is recessive. Each gamete carries only one allele (they separate during gamete formation), and the two alleles meet again at fertilisation.

5. Dihybrid Cross (two traits)

A cross involving two pairs of traits, e.g. seed shape (round R, wrinkled r) and seed colour (yellow Y, green y).
Steps: (1) Cross round yellow seeds (RRYY) with wrinkled green seeds (rryy): all F1 are RrYy: round and yellow, so round and yellow are dominant. (2) Self-pollinate F1: F2 shows four types in the ratio 9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green.

Inference: round green and wrinkled yellow are new combinations not seen in parents. Therefore the traits (shape and colour) are inherited independently of each other. Mendel concluded that factors for different traits assort independently (independent assortment).

6. How Do Traits Get Expressed?

Cellular DNA gives the information for making proteins. Proteins act as enzymes or hormones and control body features. For example, the gene for height controls the amount of a plant hormone: a gene that makes an efficient enzyme produces more hormone and the plant is tall; an altered gene makes less efficient enzyme, less hormone, and the plant is dwarf. In this way genes control characteristics.
Acquired and inherited traits: Inherited traits come from changes in the DNA of germ (reproductive) cells and are passed on. Acquired traits are developed during life (e.g. thin body by starvation, muscle by exercise, loss of a tail) and change only body (somatic) cells, not the DNA of germ cells, so they are not inherited.

7. Human Inheritance and Sex Determination

Many human traits (eye colour, free or attached earlobe, tongue rolling, hair type) show dominant and recessive forms. For example, brown eye colour (B) is dominant over blue (b), so Bb parents can have a blue-eyed (bb) child, which cannot be seen in them.
Sex determination: humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes: 22 pairs autosomes and 1 pair of sex chromosomes. Females have XX (both same), males have XY (one short Y). Every egg carries an X. A sperm carries either X or Y, so the father's sperm decides the sex: X sperm + egg gives XX (girl), Y sperm + egg gives XY (boy). The chance is 50 % each, so a mother cannot be blamed for the sex of the child.
Other animals: in some reptiles (e.g. turtles) the incubation temperature of the egg decides sex; in some animals like snails the individual can change sex.

Short Answer Questions

Q1. What is heredity?The transmission of characters from parents to offspring through genes.
Q2. What is a gene?A segment of DNA that carries the information to make a particular protein and controls a trait.
Q3. Why did Mendel choose the pea plant?It has many contrasting visible traits, a short life cycle, many seeds, and can both self- and cross-pollinate.
Q4. Define dominant and recessive traits.The trait that appears in F1 is dominant; the one hidden in F1 and reappearing in F2 is recessive.
Q5. What is the genotypic ratio of a monohybrid F2?1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.
Q6. What is the phenotypic ratio of a dihybrid F2?9 : 3 : 3 : 1.
Q7. What do 'round green' and 'wrinkled yellow' seeds in F2 show?They are new combinations, showing that shape and colour are inherited independently.
Q8. What are acquired traits? Are they inherited?Traits developed during life (e.g. muscle by exercise). They do not change the germ-cell DNA, so they are not inherited.
Q9. How many chromosomes do human gametes and zygote have?Gametes 23 (one set); zygote 46 (23 pairs).
Q10. What are sex chromosomes? Name them in a male and a female.Chromosomes that decide sex: female XX, male XY.
Q11. Who decides the sex of a child, mother or father? Why?Father, because all eggs carry X while sperms carry X or Y.
Q12. What is the probability of a girl or boy at each birth?50 % each (1 : 1).
Q13. How is the number of chromosomes kept constant in a species?Gametes have half the number (23) and fertilisation restores the full number (46).
Q14. Why do variations arise in sexual reproduction?Because DNA of two parents combines in new ways and the copying of DNA is not perfectly accurate.
Q15. Give two human traits showing dominance and recessiveness.Eye colour (brown dominant, blue recessive) and earlobes (free commonly dominant over attached).

Long Answer Questions

Q1. State Mendel's experiment on monohybrid cross with a Punnett square and conclusions.Pure tall (TT) × pure dwarf (tt): gametes T and t; F1: all Tt, tall. Self-cross of F1: gametes T, t from each parent; Punnett square gives TT, Tt, Tt, tt. F2: 3 tall : 1 dwarf (phenotype), 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt (genotype). Conclusions: tall is dominant, dwarf is recessive; traits do not blend; alleles separate in gametes and reunite at fertilisation.
Q2. Explain the dihybrid cross of Mendel with its results and conclusion.Round yellow (RRYY) × wrinkled green (rryy): F1 all RrYy (round yellow). F1 produces four gametes RY, Ry, rY, ry. In F2 (16 combinations): 9 round yellow, 3 round green, 3 wrinkled yellow, 1 wrinkled green. Round green and wrinkled yellow are new combinations, proving that the traits are inherited independently.
Q3. How are traits inherited? Explain the equal contribution of mother and father.Each cell has a double set of chromosomes, one from each parent. A child receives 23 chromosomes from the sperm and 23 from the egg, so each trait is controlled by two alleles, one paternal and one maternal. Both parents therefore contribute equally. The dominant allele is expressed when the two differ.
Q4. Explain how the sex of a child is determined in humans.Humans have 22 pairs of autosomes and 1 pair of sex chromosomes. Females have XX and produce eggs with X; males have XY and produce X and Y sperms in equal numbers. An X sperm fertilising the egg gives XX (girl); a Y sperm gives XY (boy). So the father determines the sex, with a 50 % chance each.
Q5. Distinguish between acquired and inherited traits with examples.Inherited traits arise from changes in germ-cell DNA and pass to offspring (eye colour, skin colour, tallness). Acquired traits develop during life through the environment or use (muscles from exercise, a thin body from starvation, a cut tail) and change only body cells, so they are not inherited.
Q6. How do genes control traits? Explain with tallness in pea plants.Genes carry the code to make proteins. Proteins work as enzymes and hormones that control features. The gene for height makes an enzyme that controls the amount of a plant hormone. An efficient gene makes more hormone and the plant is tall; an altered gene makes less efficient enzyme and less hormone, so the plant is dwarf.
Q7. What is a gene, DNA and a chromosome? How are they related?DNA is the double-helix molecule in the nucleus carrying genetic information. A gene is a segment of DNA that codes for one protein. Chromosomes are thread-like structures made of DNA and proteins. So many genes lie on a DNA molecule, and DNA is packed into a chromosome.
Q8. What is accumulation of variation? Explain with the beetle example.In sexual reproduction, new variations arise and are inherited generation after generation. In a beetle colony, if a green variant appears among red beetles and crows eat red ones more easily, green beetles survive and reproduce, so the green trait spreads while the red decreases. Thus useful variations accumulate.
Q9. Draw and explain the pedigree of eye colour for Bb × Bb parents.Both parents are Bb with brown eyes (B dominant). Gametes B and b from each give BB, Bb, Bb and bb. Three children have brown eyes and one has blue (bb), which was hidden in the parents. The ratio is 3 brown : 1 blue and the genotype ratio 1 : 2 : 1.
Q10. List Mendel's contributions and conclusions about inheritance.Mendel chose the pea plant and counted offspring of crosses. He found that F1 shows only one of two contrasting traits (dominance), that the hidden trait reappears in F2 in a 3 : 1 ratio (segregation of alleles), and that two traits are inherited independently giving a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio (independent assortment). He showed that traits are controlled by units now called genes.
