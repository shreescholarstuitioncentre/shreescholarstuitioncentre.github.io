Class 10 Science – Chapter 7: How do Organisms Reproduce?

Sources covered: NCERT, Balaji, Rajeev Publications, UP Board pattern

1. Why Reproduce? Importance of Variation

Reproduction is the production of new individuals similar to the parent. It is not needed for the survival of an individual, but it keeps the species going.
DNA copying: DNA in the cell nucleus carries the information to make proteins, which decide body design. During reproduction cells copy their DNA by chemical reactions and make extra cell machinery, so the new cell has the same body design. The copying is not perfectly accurate, so small variations arise.
Why variations matter: (1) They help a species survive changes in the environment, e.g. if a heat wave kills most bacteria, the few heat-resistant variants survive and multiply. (2) They are the basis of evolution. (3) Reproduction keeps the population stable in its niche. Some variations are useful, some harmful, and others have no effect.

2. Asexual Reproduction

A single parent produces new individuals which are identical (clones), without gametes; variations are only small copying errors.
(a) Fission (unicellular organisms): binary fission gives two cells: in Amoeba the division can be in any plane, while in Leishmania it happens at a fixed position near the whip-like flagellum. Multiple fission gives many daughter cells at once, e.g. Plasmodium (malaria parasite) divides inside a cyst.
(b) Fragmentation: a multicellular organism with simple body (e.g. Spirogyra) breaks into pieces on maturing, and each piece grows into a new individual.
(c) Regeneration: if an organism is cut or broken into pieces, each piece may regrow the missing parts, e.g. Hydra and Planaria. It is done by specialised cells that divide and form tissues. Regeneration is not the same as reproduction, as most organisms cannot regenerate any body part from any cut.
(d) Budding: a bud grows out of the parent body due to repeated cell division at one site, develops into a tiny individual and detaches (Hydra, yeast).
(e) Vegetative propagation: new plants from the vegetative parts (root, stem, leaf). Natural: roots (sweet potato, dahlia), stems (potato tuber, ginger rhizome, runner grass), leaves (Bryophyllum produces buds in leaf notches that grow into plantlets). Artificial: cutting (rose, sugarcane), layering (jasmine, strawberry), grafting (mango) and tissue culture (a few cells from the growing tip are placed on a nutrient medium to form a callus, then plantlets; used for orchids and ornamental plants).
Advantages of vegetative propagation: plants that cannot make viable seeds (banana, orange, rose, jasmine) can be grown; new plants are genetically identical to the parent; they bear flowers and fruits earlier; they are produced faster.
(f) Spore formation: many fungi (Rhizopus, bread mould) have upright hyphae with tiny round sporangia full of spores. Spores are covered by a thick protective wall and can survive harsh conditions; in moist and warm conditions they germinate into new hyphae.

3. Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

Two parents (or two kinds of gametes) are involved, giving more variations, which help a species to adapt and evolve. Flower is the reproductive organ of angiosperms.
Flower parts: sepals (protect the bud), petals (attract pollinators), stamen (male: anther + filament; anther makes pollen grains containing male gametes), pistil/carpel (female: stigma (sticky top), style (tube), ovary (with ovules, each having an egg cell)). Bisexual flowers have both stamens and pistil (Hibiscus, mustard); unisexual flowers have only one (papaya, watermelon).
Pollination: transfer of pollen from anther to stigma. Self-pollination: within the same flower or between flowers of the same plant. Cross-pollination: to a flower of another plant of the same species (by wind, water, insects, birds, animals).
Fertilisation: after landing on the stigma, the pollen grain grows a pollen tube through the style to the ovule. The male gamete travels down the tube and fuses with the egg cell to form the zygote. The zygote divides to form the embryo.
After fertilisation: the zygote → embryo; the ovule → seed (embryo with stored food in the cotyledons, covered by seed coat); the ovary → fruit; the petals, sepals, stamens, style and stigma wither and fall off.
Seed germination: the seed stays dormant until it gets water, air and suitable temperature. Then the embryo grows: the radicle becomes the root and the plumule becomes the shoot; the cotyledons feed the seedling.

4. Reproduction in Human Beings

Humans reproduce sexually: sperm (male gamete: tiny, motile, with a tail) and egg/ovum (female gamete: large, with stored food). Their fusion is fertilisation, forming a zygote.
Puberty (about 10–12 years onwards): Both: thick hair in armpits and genital area, skin becomes oily (pimples). Girls: breast size increases, dark nipple skin, menstruation starts. Boys: thick facial hair, voice breaks, penis occasionally enlarges. These are controlled by hormones (testosterone, oestrogen).
Male reproductive system: testes lie outside the abdomen in the scrotum because sperm formation needs a temperature lower than body temperature; they make sperms and the hormone testosterone (controls sperm production and secondary sexual features). Sperms pass through the vas deferens, joined by secretions of the seminal vesicle and prostate gland that nourish sperms and help them move (this fluid with sperms is semen). The urethra is the common passage for semen and urine and runs through the penis.
Female reproductive system: two ovaries make eggs (thousands of immature eggs are present at birth; from puberty one egg matures each month, alternately from either ovary) and produce hormones. The egg is caught by the funnel of the oviduct (fallopian tube) where fertilisation occurs. The two oviducts join in the uterus (womb), which opens through the cervix into the vagina.
If fertilisation occurs: the zygote divides to form an embryo that implants in the thick, blood-rich uterus lining. The placenta, a disc-like tissue embedded in the uterine wall with villi, lets the mother's blood supply glucose and oxygen to the embryo and remove wastes (they do not mix directly). The embryo is connected by the umbilical cord. Development (gestation) takes about nine months (about 40 weeks), ending in childbirth by rhythmic contractions of uterine muscles.
If fertilisation does not occur: the egg lives for about one day, the thickened uterus lining is not needed and is shed with blood and mucus through the vagina: menstruation (2–8 days). The cycle repeats about every 28 days.

5. Reproductive Health

Reproductive health means a state of physical, mental and social well-being in all matters of reproduction. Important points:
Contraception (to avoid pregnancy): barrier methods (condom, diaphragm, cervical cap) stop sperm reaching the egg; chemical methods (oral pills) change hormonal balance and stop egg release (may have side effects); IUCD (loop, Copper-T) placed in the uterus; surgical methods: vasectomy (vas deferens cut/tied) and tubectomy (oviducts cut/tied), which are permanent.
Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs): bacterial (gonorrhoea, syphilis), viral (warts, HIV-AIDS). Spread by sexual contact; condoms give some protection. HIV also spreads via infected blood and from mother to child.
Social issues: illegal sex-determination and female foeticide reduce the sex ratio; medical termination of pregnancy (MTP) is allowed only legally for medical reasons; small family norms and spacing control population growth; the mother's health needs care during pregnancy.

6. Important Activities (NCERT)

1. Yeast budding: put yeast in sugar solution, keep warm and view a drop under a microscope: buds appear on yeast cells.
2. Bread mould: keep moist bread in a damp warm place for 3–4 days; thread-like hyphae and round sporangia appear; touch a sporangium to see spores.
3. Spirogyra: view a filament from a pond under a microscope; it breaks into fragments.
4. Bryophyllum and potato: keep a leaf on moist soil; buds from notches grow into plantlets; potato eyes sprout when kept moist.
5. Pollen germination: put pollen in dilute sugar solution and view it; a pollen tube grows out.
6. Flower dissection: take a bisexual flower (hibiscus), separate its parts and identify stamens and pistil.
7. Seed structure: soak chickpea seeds overnight, peel the seed coat and open them to see cotyledons, radicle and plumule.

Short Answer Questions

Q1. Why is variation important for a species?It helps some individuals survive environmental changes, so the species continues, and it is the basis of evolution.
Q2. What is binary fission? Give examples.A cell divides into two daughter cells: Amoeba (any plane) and Leishmania (fixed plane).
Q3. How is multiple fission different from binary fission?In multiple fission one parent forms many daughter cells at once (Plasmodium inside a cyst); in binary fission only two form.
Q4. What is regeneration? Give two examples.The ability of a body to regrow lost parts using specialised dividing cells, as in Hydra and Planaria.
Q5. What is budding? Name two organisms showing it.A bud grows from the parent body by cell division, develops and detaches: Hydra and yeast.
Q6. Why is spore formation useful to Rhizopus?Spores have a thick protective wall, survive harsh conditions and germinate when moisture and warmth return.
Q7. Name two plants produced by vegetative propagation and the part used.Potato (stem tuber eyes) and Bryophyllum (leaf notch buds).
Q8. What are the advantages of vegetative propagation?Plants without viable seeds can be grown, offspring are identical to the parent, and they flower and fruit earlier.
Q9. What is tissue culture?Growing a few cells of a plant on a nutrient medium to make a callus and then plantlets, e.g. for orchids.
Q10. Differentiate between pollination and fertilisation.Pollination is transfer of pollen from anther to stigma; fertilisation is the fusion of male and female gametes to form the zygote.
Q11. What happens to the ovary and ovule after fertilisation?The ovary becomes the fruit and the ovule becomes the seed.
Q12. Why are the testes located outside the abdomen?Sperm production needs a temperature lower than body temperature, which the scrotum provides.
Q13. What is the role of the placenta?It transfers oxygen and nutrients from the mother to the embryo and removes the embryo's wastes.
Q14. Why does menstruation occur?If the egg is not fertilised, the thickened uterine lining is not needed, so it breaks down and leaves with blood.
Q15. Name two sexually transmitted diseases, one bacterial and one viral.Gonorrhoea (bacterial) and HIV-AIDS (viral).

Long Answer Questions

Q1. Describe the different modes of asexual reproduction with examples.Fission: Amoeba (binary), Plasmodium (multiple). Fragmentation: Spirogyra breaks into pieces that grow into filaments. Regeneration: Hydra and Planaria regrow lost parts from specialised cells. Budding: Hydra and yeast grow buds that detach. Vegetative propagation: roots (sweet potato), stems (potato), leaves (Bryophyllum), or by cutting, layering, grafting and tissue culture. Spore formation: Rhizopus releases spores from sporangia.
Q2. Explain the advantages and disadvantages of vegetative propagation.Advantages: grows plants that cannot produce seeds (banana, orange, rose, jasmine); offspring identical to the parent with desired traits; they mature, flower and fruit early; quick and cheap. Disadvantage: no variation, so a disease or a change in the environment can kill all the plants.
Q3. Explain the structure of a flower with a labelled description.A bisexual flower has sepals (protect the bud), petals (attract insects), stamens (male: filament + anther making pollen) and a pistil (female: stigma, style, ovary with ovules). The stigma receives pollen, the style forms the path for the pollen tube, and each ovule contains an egg cell.
Q4. Describe pollination and fertilisation in flowering plants. What changes occur afterwards?Pollen transfers from anther to stigma by wind, water or animals (self or cross). The pollen grain grows a pollen tube through the style into the ovule. The male gamete fuses with the egg to form a zygote, which divides to form an embryo. Afterwards the ovule becomes the seed, the ovary becomes the fruit, and the other floral parts wither.
Q5. Describe the male reproductive system with functions of each part.Testes (in the scrotum) produce sperms and testosterone. The vas deferens carries sperms. The seminal vesicle and prostate gland add fluid to nourish and carry sperms (semen). The urethra carries semen (and urine) through the penis to the outside.
Q6. Describe the female reproductive system and the process of fertilisation and implantation.Ovaries produce eggs and hormones; one egg is released monthly and is caught by the oviduct, where sperm may fertilise it. The zygote divides into an embryo, which travels to the uterus and implants in its thick, blood-rich lining. The placenta develops and supplies the embryo through the umbilical cord. The cervix and vagina form the birth canal.
Q7. What is the menstrual cycle? Explain its phases.A monthly cycle of about 28 days: days 1–5 menstruation (lining sheds), days 6–13 the lining rebuilds and an egg matures, day 14 ovulation, days 15–28 the lining stays thick for a possible embryo. If no fertilisation occurs the lining sheds again.
Q8. Explain the role of the placenta in the development of the embryo.The placenta is a disc embedded in the uterine wall, with villi that increase surface area and bring the embryo’s blood close to the mother’s. Glucose and oxygen pass to the embryo and wastes pass to the mother, without direct mixing of blood. The cord connects it to the embryo.
Q9. Describe various methods of contraception and the importance of reproductive health.Barrier: condom, diaphragm. Chemical: oral pills. IUCD: Copper-T. Surgical: vasectomy and tubectomy. Reproductive health prevents STDs (gonorrhoea, syphilis, warts, HIV-AIDS), controls population growth, protects mother’s health and avoids female foeticide that disturbs the sex ratio.
Q10. Compare sexual and asexual reproduction.Asexual: one parent, no gametes, rapid, offspring identical, little variation (Amoeba, Hydra). Sexual: two types of gametes, fusion forms zygote, slower, offspring show more variation, which helps adaptation and evolution (humans, flowering plants).
