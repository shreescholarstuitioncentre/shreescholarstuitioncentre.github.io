Class 10 Science – Chapter 6: Control and Coordination

Sources covered: NCERT, Balaji, Rajeev Publications, UP Board pattern

1. Introduction

Living organisms respond to changes in surroundings called stimuli (light, heat, touch, smell). The working together of organs in response is coordination. In animals this is done by the nervous system (electrical impulses) and hormones (chemical messengers, endocrine system). Plants have no nervous system but use chemical (hormonal) coordination.

2. Receptors and the Neuron

Receptors (sense organs) detect stimuli: photoreceptors (eye – light), phonoreceptors (ear – sound and balance), olfactory receptors (nose – smell), gustatory receptors (tongue – taste), thermoreceptors/touch receptors (skin – heat, cold, touch).
Neuron is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system. Parts: dendrites (receive information), cell body with nucleus, axon (long fibre that conducts impulse) and nerve endings.
Path of impulse: information acquired at the dendrite tip → sets off a chemical reaction that creates an electrical impulse → travels to the cell body → along the axon → at the nerve ending, the impulse releases chemicals that cross the gap called the synapse and start a new impulse in the dendrite of the next neuron. Finally, impulses go to muscles or glands. This arrangement ensures one-way travel of the impulse.
Watch the synapse: (1) the impulse reaches the axon terminal; (2) vesicles release a chemical neurotransmitter into the tiny synaptic gap; (3) it diffuses across and binds receptors on the dendrite of the next neuron; (4) a new electrical impulse starts there. Only the first neuron has vesicles, so the signal can pass in one direction only.

3. Reflex Action and Reflex Arc

Reflex action: sudden, automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus, e.g. pulling the hand away from a hot object, blinking, sneezing, knee jerk. It is controlled by the spinal cord, not by the brain, so response is quick and avoids damage (the brain takes time to decide because many neurons are involved).
Reflex arc: Receptor → Sensory neuron → Spinal cord (relay neuron) → Motor neuron → Effector (muscle)
Why a reflex arc? In the spinal cord, the sensory and motor neurons connect in the path of a reflex. Information also goes to the brain, so we later become aware that we touched something hot. Reflex arcs also help where thinking is not needed, like controlling heartbeat or breathing, whereas thinking-based actions (reading) need the brain.

4. Human Nervous System

Central Nervous System (CNS): brain and spinal cord. Peripheral Nervous System (PNS): nerves from the brain (cranial, 12 pairs) and spinal cord (spinal, 31 pairs).
Brain has three parts:
1) Fore-brain (cerebrum): largest, centre of thinking, memory, intelligence, voluntary actions, hearing, smell, sight, touch and the hunger centre.
2) Mid-brain: involuntary actions in eye movement and pupil size.
3) Hind-brain: cerebellum (balance, posture, precision of voluntary actions like picking a pen), pons (regulates breathing), medulla (involuntary actions: blood pressure, salivation, vomiting).

How do muscles move? Nerve impulses reach muscle cells, where special protein molecules change their shape and arrangement, so the cells become short, and the muscle contracts.

5. Coordination in Plants

Plants respond to stimuli by tropic movements (directional growth) and by nastic movements (non-directional).
Phototropism (light): shoots bend towards light (positive), roots away (negative). Geotropism (gravity): roots grow down (positive), shoots up (negative). Hydrotropism (water): roots grow towards water. Chemotropism (chemicals): pollen tube grows towards the ovule.
Reading the diagram: a seed placed sideways still sends its root down (positive geotropism) and its shoot up (negative geotropism). A root also bends towards moist soil (positive hydrotropism). Thigmotropism: a tendril coils round a support because cells on the side away from the support grow faster than the cells touching it.

Touch-me-not (Mimosa pudica): leaves fold and droop when touched due to change in turgor pressure (water leaves the cells and they shrink). It is not growth; the plant uses special electrical-chemical means.
Reading the animation: the touch sets off an electrical-chemical signal that reaches the pulvinus (swollen leaf base). Water moves out of its cells, they lose turgor and shrink, so leaflets fold and the leaf droops. Water returns slowly and the leaf reopens. It is a nastic movement: the response does not depend on the direction of the stimulus and it is not growth.
Plant hormones: (1) Auxin: made at the shoot tip, helps cells to grow longer; when light is on one side, auxin moves to the shaded side, making cells there longer so the shoot bends toward light. (2) Gibberellins: stem growth. (3) Cytokinins: cell division, high in fruits and seeds. (4) Abscisic acid: growth inhibitor; causes wilting of leaves, stress response, closing of stomata.

6. Hormones in Animals

Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands directly into the blood in small amounts, carried to target organs.
Where are they? pituitary at the base of the brain (the master gland, as it controls other glands), thyroid in the neck, adrenals on top of the kidneys, pancreas behind the stomach (it is both a digestive gland and an endocrine gland), ovaries in the female and testes in the male. Adrenaline: secreted in emergencies, heart beats faster, breathing becomes rapid, blood is diverted to skeletal muscles, digestion slows. Feedback mechanism: hormone levels are regulated, e.g. when blood sugar rises, pancreas releases more insulin; as sugar falls, insulin release decreases. Iodine deficiency causes goitre (swollen neck). Diabetic patients are treated with insulin injections.
Negative feedback: after a meal blood sugar rises → pancreas releases insulin → cells and liver take up glucose (stored as glycogen) → sugar returns to normal → insulin release falls. Thus levels of hormones are kept steady. Insulin is given by injection (not as tablets) because it is a protein that would be digested in the stomach.

7. Important Activities (NCERT)

1. Knee-jerk reflex: let a person sit with legs hanging and tap just below the knee; the leg jerks without conscious thought.
2. Touch-me-not: touch the leaves of Mimosa pudica; the leaflets fold and the leaf droops within seconds.
3. Phototropism: grow seedlings in a box with a small hole on one side; they bend towards the light.
4. Geotropism: place germinating gram seeds on moist cotton on a board, then turn the board by 90°; roots again bend downward.
5. Hydrotropism: plant seedlings and keep a porous pot of water on one side; roots grow towards the water.
6. Tendrils: a tendril coils round a support after contact (thigmotropism).

Short Answer Questions

Q1. What is a stimulus? Give two examples.Any change in surroundings to which an organism responds, e.g. light and touch.
Q2. What is a synapse? Why is it important?The gap between the nerve ending of one neuron and the dendrite of the next. Impulse crosses it by chemicals, ensuring one-way transmission.
Q3. What is a reflex action? Give two examples.A sudden, involuntary response to a stimulus controlled by the spinal cord, e.g. withdrawing a hand from fire and blinking.
Q4. Which part of the brain controls balance and posture?Cerebellum in the hind-brain.
Q5. Why is the brain protected by fluid?The cerebrospinal fluid in the meninges acts as a shock absorber and prevents injury.
Q6. What is phototropism?Growth of plant parts in response to light, positive in shoots, negative in roots.
Q7. Name the hormone responsible for the growth of the stem and the one for cell division.Gibberellin for stem growth; cytokinin for cell division.
Q8. Why is iodised salt necessary in our diet?Iodine is needed to make thyroxine. Deficiency causes goitre.
Q9. Why do plants not have nervous tissue?They do not move from place to place, so they use hormones and changes in cell water (turgor) instead.
Q10. Name the three types of neurons.Sensory (receptors to CNS), relay/association (inside CNS) and motor (CNS to effectors).
Q11. Why is reflex action faster than a thinking response?The impulse passes only through the spinal cord, not the brain, so fewer neurons and less time are involved.
Q12. What is thigmotropism? Give an example.Directional growth in response to touch, e.g. a tendril coiling around a support.
Q13. Why is the pituitary called the master gland?It secretes hormones that control the working of other endocrine glands, and growth hormone.
Q14. What happens if the cerebellum is damaged?The person cannot keep balance or perform precise voluntary actions and walks unsteadily.
Q15. Why is insulin given as an injection?It is a protein, which would be digested in the stomach if taken as a tablet.
Q16. What is the role of adrenaline?It prepares the body for emergency: faster heartbeat and breathing and more blood to muscles.
Q17. Where is the hunger centre of the brain?In the cerebrum (fore-brain).

Long Answer Questions

Q1. Describe the structure and working of a neuron.A neuron has dendrites (receive information), a cell body with nucleus, a long axon and nerve endings. The information received at the dendrites triggers an electrical impulse, which travels through the cell body and the axon to the nerve ending. Here chemicals cross the synapse and set a new impulse in the next neuron. The impulse then passes to muscles or glands.
Q2. What is a reflex arc? Explain with an example.The pathway followed in a reflex action: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord (relay neuron) → motor neuron → effector. E.g. on touching a hot object, the skin receptor sends the impulse to the spinal cord via the sensory neuron; the relay neuron transmits it to the motor neuron, which makes the muscles of the hand contract, so the hand is withdrawn quickly before the brain registers the pain.
Q3. Describe the parts of the human brain and their functions.Fore-brain (cerebrum): thinking, memory, voluntary actions, senses, hunger centre. Mid-brain: involuntary actions such as pupil size. Hind-brain: cerebellum controls balance and precision, pons regulates breathing, medulla controls involuntary actions like blood pressure, salivation and vomiting. The brain is protected by cranium, meninges and CSF.
Q4. Explain how auxin causes a shoot to bend towards light.Auxin is made at the shoot tip. When light falls from one side, auxin diffuses to the shaded side. Cells on the shaded side receive more auxin and elongate more than cells on the lit side, so the shoot bends towards light.
Q5. Name the hormones of the endocrine glands with functions and disorders.Thyroid: thyroxine controls metabolism; deficiency causes goitre. Pituitary: growth hormone; dwarfism (less), gigantism (more). Pancreas: insulin controls blood sugar; deficiency causes diabetes. Adrenal: adrenaline helps in emergencies. Testes: testosterone for male puberty. Ovary: oestrogen for female puberty.
Q6. Compare nervous and hormonal coordination.Nervous: electrical impulses via nerves, very fast, short-lived, specific organs. Hormonal: chemicals through the blood, slow, long-lasting, affects many cells over a wide area. Both are essential, as some functions need quick response while growth needs long-term regulation.
Q7. Explain the synapse and the transmission of an impulse across it.A synapse is the gap between the axon terminal of one neuron and the dendrite of the next. When the impulse reaches the terminal, vesicles release a neurotransmitter, which diffuses across the gap and binds receptors on the next dendrite, starting a new impulse. Because only the first neuron releases chemicals, transmission is one-way.
Q8. Describe the endocrine glands of the human body with hormones and disorders.Pituitary: growth hormone (dwarfism, gigantism). Thyroid: thyroxine (goitre from iodine deficiency). Pancreas: insulin (diabetes). Adrenal: adrenaline (emergency response). Testes: testosterone. Ovaries: oestrogen. These glands release hormones directly into the blood.
Q9. What is feedback mechanism? Explain with the help of insulin.It is the regulation of hormone release by the effect it produces. After a meal blood sugar rises; the pancreas senses this and releases insulin. Insulin makes cells and the liver take up glucose, so blood sugar falls to normal, and the pancreas then releases less insulin. This keeps blood sugar near a steady level.
Q10. Explain tropic movements in plants with examples.Directional growth movements: phototropism (shoots toward light), geotropism (roots down, shoots up), hydrotropism (roots toward water), chemotropism (pollen tube toward ovule), thigmotropism (tendrils coil on contact). Plant hormones such as auxin distribute unevenly and cause cells on one side to grow longer.
Q11. Distinguish between voluntary, involuntary and reflex actions.Voluntary actions are controlled by the cerebrum and need thinking (writing). Involuntary actions are controlled by the medulla and other parts and occur without will (heartbeat, digestion). Reflex actions are sudden, automatic responses controlled by the spinal cord (blinking, withdrawing the hand from fire).
Q12. How does Mimosa respond to touch? How is this different from phototropism?On touch, signals reach the pulvinus, water leaves its cells, turgor falls and the leaf droops. This is a quick nastic movement without growth. Phototropism is slow, directional growth of shoots towards light caused by auxin.
