Class 10 Science – Chapter 2: Acids, Bases and Salts

Sources covered: NCERT, Balaji, Rajeev Publications, UP Board pattern

1. Acids, Bases and Indicators

Acids are sour in taste, turn blue litmus red (e.g. HCl, H2SO4, HNO3, citric acid, acetic acid). Bases are bitter and soapy to touch, turn red litmus blue (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)2). Bases soluble in water are called alkalis. Never taste chemicals in the lab.
Indicators: substances that show different colours in acid and base.
Litmus: acid red / base blue. Phenolphthalein: acid colourless / base pink. Methyl orange: acid red / base yellow. Turmeric: acid yellow / base red. Red cabbage extract also works.
Olfactory indicators change smell: onion, vanilla and clove lose their smell in a base.

2. Chemical Properties of Acids and Bases

(a) Acid + Metal → Salt + H2
Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl2 + H2↑
2NaOH + Zn → Na2ZnO2 + H2↑ (base + metal also gives H2, sodium zincate)
Test: H2 burns with a pop sound (see Diagram 2).
(b) Acid + Metal carbonate/hydrogencarbonate → Salt + CO2 + H2O
Na2CO3 + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H2O + CO2
NaHCO3 + HCl → NaCl + H2O + CO2
CO2 turns lime water milky: Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3↓ + H2O. On passing excess CO2, milkiness disappears: CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 → Ca(HCO3)2 (soluble).
(c) Neutralisation: Acid + Base → Salt + Water
NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H2O
(d) Acid + Metal oxide → Salt + Water (metal oxides are basic): CuO + 2HCl → CuCl2 + H2O (blue-green solution).
(e) Base + Non-metal oxide → Salt + Water (non-metal oxides are acidic): Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3 + H2O

3. Acids and Bases in Water

Acids give H+ ions (in water they form hydronium H3O+); bases give OH- ions. Dry HCl gas does not change dry litmus, because without water no H+ is produced. Glucose and alcohol have hydrogen but are not acids, since they do not ionise.
Dilution: mixing acid/base with water is highly exothermic. Always add acid to water, slowly, with stirring, never water to acid (it may splash and burn). Dilution lowers the concentration of H3O+ per unit volume.

4. Strength of Acids and Bases: pH Scale

pH = potential of Hydrogen. The scale runs 0 to 14: pH < 7 acidic, pH = 7 neutral, pH > 7 basic. As H+ concentration increases, pH decreases. Universal indicator shows different colours at different pH. Strong acids (HCl, H2SO4) ionise fully; weak acids (acetic acid, citric acid) ionise partly.
pH in daily life: (1) Our body works at pH 7.0–7.8. (2) Acid rain pH below 5.6 harms rivers and aquatic life. (3) Tooth decay starts when mouth pH falls below 5.5; use basic toothpaste. (4) Stomach HCl (pH ~1.5) helps digestion; acidity relieved by antacids (milk of magnesia). (5) Soil pH decides crops. (6) Bee/ant sting (methanoic acid) is relieved by baking soda; nettle sting is treated with dock plant.

5. More about Salts

Salts are products of neutralisation. Family: salts with the same positive or negative ion, e.g. NaCl and Na2SO4. pH: strong acid + strong base gives neutral salt (pH 7); strong acid + weak base gives acidic (pH < 7); strong base + weak acid gives basic (pH > 7).
Common salt (NaCl) by chlor-alkali process: 2NaCl + 2H2O → 2NaOH + Cl2 + H2 Cl2 (anode) is used for bleach and PVC; H2 (cathode) for fuels and margarine; NaOH for soaps and paper.
Bleaching powder CaOCl2: Ca(OH)2 + Cl2 → CaOCl2 + H2O. Uses: bleaching, disinfecting drinking water, oxidiser.
Baking soda NaHCO3: NaCl + H2O + CO2 + NH3 → NH4Cl + NaHCO3. On heating: 2NaHCO3 → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2 Uses: antacid, baking powder (baking soda + tartaric acid; CO2 makes cakes fluffy), soda-acid fire extinguisher.
Washing soda Na2CO3·10H2O: from Na2CO3 + 10H2O. Used in glass, soap, paper, and to remove permanent hardness of water.
Water of crystallisation: fixed water molecules in a crystal: CuSO4·5H2O (blue) → CuSO4 (white) on heating; adding water restores blue. Na2CO3·10H2O has 10; gypsum CaSO4·2H2O has 2.
Plaster of Paris CaSO4·½H2O: CaSO4·2H2O → (373 K) CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O Sets into gypsum on adding water: CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O. Heat only at 373 K, otherwise it loses all water. Stored in moisture-proof containers. Used for plastering broken bones, toys, decoration.

Short Answer Questions

Q1. Why does an aqueous solution of an acid conduct electricity?Acid produces H+ (H3O+) ions in water. These moving ions carry current.
Q2. Why should curd and sour substances not be kept in brass or copper vessels?Acids in them react with the metal, forming harmful salts that spoil the food and may poison it.
Q3. What happens when a solution of an acid is mixed with a solution of a base in a test tube? Is the temperature of the mixture raised or lowered?Neutralisation takes place and salt and water form. The temperature rises as the reaction is exothermic.
Q4. Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of dry litmus paper?It does not release H+ without water, so the litmus paper does not detect acidity.
Q5. Why is Plaster of Paris stored in a moisture-proof container?It absorbs moisture from air and sets into hard gypsum, becoming useless.
Q6. Name the acid present in (i) ant sting (ii) tomato (iii) tamarind (iv) vinegar.(i) Methanoic (formic) acid (ii) Oxalic acid (iii) Tartaric acid (iv) Acetic acid.

Long Answer Questions

Q1. What is the pH scale? Why is pH important for our life?The pH scale (0–14) measures the hydrogen ion concentration in a solution. pH 7 is neutral, below 7 acidic, above 7 basic. Importance: living bodies work in a narrow pH range (7.0–7.8); acid rain (pH < 5.6) harms aquatic life; tooth decay starts below pH 5.5; stomach acid (pH 1.5) aids digestion, but excess is treated by antacids; soil pH decides which crops grow; stings are treated with a suitable acid/base.
Q2. How is Plaster of Paris made? Write the equations and uses.It is made by heating gypsum at 373 K: CaSO4·2H2O → CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O. It is a white powder. On mixing with water it hardens into gypsum: CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O. Uses: setting fractured bones, making toys and statues, decorative material, smoothing surfaces.
Q3. Describe the activity to show that all acids contain hydrogen. Also explain the preparation of baking soda and its uses.Take Zn granules in a flask with dil. H2SO4/HCl, pass the gas through soap solution and bring a candle near the bubble: it burns with a pop sound (H2). Baking soda: NaCl + H2O + CO2 + NH3 → NH4Cl + NaHCO3. Uses: antacid, baking powder, fire extinguisher, cooking (fluffy cakes).
Q4. Explain the chlor-alkali process and the uses of its products.On passing electricity through brine (aqueous NaCl): 2NaCl + 2H2O → 2NaOH + Cl2 + H2. Cl2 forms at the anode and H2 at the cathode, and NaOH remains near the cathode. Cl2: water treatment, bleach, PVC. H2: fuel, margarine, ammonia. NaOH: soap, paper, artificial fibres. Cl2 + NaOH gives bleach, and Cl2 + H2 gives HCl.
