1Core Practical Art – What and How to Choose

What is it? In the practical part of the Class 10 Drawing & Painting paper you must show your own skill with hand, eye and colour. You are given a choice of three core practical arts, and you attempt only one of them:
  1. Prakritik Drishya Chitran – Nature (landscape) scene painting in water colours or pastels.
  2. Alekhan – Design: a decorative, rhythmic arrangement of motifs with colour harmony.
  3. Pravidhik Kala – Technical art: accurate geometrical constructions with instruments.

Besides these, the practical paper also has Smriti Chitran (memory drawing in pencil), which is treated in the last topic of these notes.

OptionWhat you doMedium / toolsMain skillsChoose it if you…
LandscapePaint a natural scene (e.g. village sunset) inside a rectangleWater colours or pastels, brushes, pencilComposition, perspective, colour mixing, observationenjoy colour and nature
Design (Alekhan)Make an original decorative pattern in a given shapePencil, compass, poster/water coloursImagination, symmetry, rhythm, colour harmonylike patterns and rangoli
Technical artSolve geometrical construction problemsPencil, scale, compass, set squares, protractorAccuracy, neatness, instrument handlinglike exact, rule-based work
How to decide Choose according to your strength and practice. Technical art is the most predictable: if you practise the standard constructions, full marks are possible. Landscape rewards good colour sense but is less forgiving of messy washes. Design needs a good original idea plus clean finishing. Whatever you choose, practise at least 10 full-size sheets before the exam.
Paper pattern In past UP Board papers the core option appeared in the compulsory section; memory drawing (pencil, picture not smaller than 15 cm, two parts, one object chosen in each) and a theory section on Indian painting were in other sections, and the time allowed was about 3 hours 15 minutes. The exact layout, marking and instructions change from session to session – always check the latest model paper.

2Prakritik Drishya Chitran – Nature (Landscape) Painting

Definition Painting a view of nature – sky, land, water, hills, trees, huts, people and animals – from observation or imagination, using colour to create depth, light and mood, is landscape (nature scene) painting.

In exams you are usually given a theme such as a village scene, sunrise or sunset, a riverside or lake, a hill scene, a rainy day or a forest, with instructions about size (often a rectangle drawn in the middle of the sheet) and medium (water colours or pastels).

Explanation of the figure: The numbers in the picture match the list on the right. Notice how the picture is divided into background (hills, sky), middle ground (lake, huts) and foreground (tree, path, farmer). The sun, the birds and the water ripples move gently in this animated diagram.

1. Parts of a landscape

  • Sky – sets the mood: warm for sunset, pale blue for noon, grey for rain.
  • Land – hills, fields, paths; gives the base of the composition.
  • Water – lake, river; reflects the sky colours.
  • Trees and plants – vertical accents and greens in many shades.
  • Buildings – huts, temple, bridge; show human life and scale.
  • Figures – people, animals, birds; give life and a focal point.

2. Composition

  • Three grounds – foreground (large, dark, detailed), middle ground, background (small, pale, soft). This creates depth.
  • Horizon line – the line where earth and sky appear to meet; it is at your eye level.
  • Focal point – one main interest (e.g. farmer or hut) placed off-centre, near a "third" of the picture.
  • Balance – a big tree on one side balanced by hills or a hut on the other.
  • Leading lines – a path or river guides the eye into the picture.

Explanation of the figure: A high horizon gives more ground and is good for fields and roads; a middle horizon divides sky and land equally (avoid it unless the design needs it as it can look static); a low horizon gives a large sky, good for sunsets and clouds. Objects below the horizon line are seen from above, those above it from below.

3. Perspective in a landscape

  • Size – nearer objects are bigger, farther ones are smaller.
  • Overlapping – the front tree hides part of the hut behind it.
  • Linear perspective – roads, rivers and fences narrow towards a vanishing point on the horizon.
  • Aerial perspective – distant things look paler, bluer and less detailed (see figure).

Explanation of the figure: Each hill layer is a different colour. As the distance increases, colours become lighter and bluish-grey, edges softer and details fewer. Near objects are darker, warmer and sharper. The legend on the right summarises this.

4. Colour in landscape

Time / weatherColour idea
MorningLight yellow, pink, pale blue; soft, cool shadows
NoonClear blue sky, bright greens, short dark shadows
SunsetOrange, red, yellow in sky; violet and blue-green shadows; silhouettes
NightDeep blue, violet; small warm lights (lamp, moon) as contrast
RainGreys with blue; wet-on-wet clouds; slanting lines of rain
  • Use warm colours in the foreground and cool colours in the distance (Chapter 1: warm advance, cool recede).
  • Make many greens: yellow + blue, adding a little red or brown to dull it. Do not use one flat green for everything.
  • Use complementary accents – a red turban in a green field.

5. Techniques

TechniqueWater colour
Flat washEven colour over an area (sky)
Graded washColour changes from dark to light (sky near horizon is lighter)
Wet-on-wetPaint on a damp surface – soft, blended clouds and water

Pastels: layer light over dark, blend with a finger or tissue for sky and water, use the side of the stick for broad areas and the tip for details; fix the final work lightly if allowed.

6. Step by step

Explanation of the figure: The same scene is built in four stages. Never start with details – always go from large to small and light to dark.

  1. Draw a margin or the required rectangle with a light pencil.
  2. Fix the horizon, then sketch the main shapes lightly (hills, lake, huts, tree, path). No heavy lines.
  3. Paint the sky first (graded wash), then water with similar but softer colours.
  4. Paint distant hills in pale bluish colour.
  5. Paint middle ground: huts, banks, trees in medium tones.
  6. Paint foreground in darker, warmer colours; add path and grass.
  7. Add details: figures, birds, windows, branches, shadows and reflections.
  8. Check colour harmony; add final dark accents; clean the border.
Checklist for marks Theme followed • good composition • proper perspective • pleasing colour harmony • clean washes • neat finish.
Common mistakes Using a lot of water so colours bleed • starting with dark colours • all trees equal size • horizon exactly in the middle • no foreground detail • heavy pencil lines showing through.

3Alekhan – Design

Definition Alekhan (design) is the decorative arrangement of lines, shapes and colours into a rhythmic, balanced pattern within a given shape. It is not a realistic picture; natural forms are simplified and stylised.

Terms to know

TermMeaning
Motif (Rupankan)Basic decorative form – a petal, leaf, bird, geometric shape
Unit (Ikai)The smallest repeating part of the design
Repetition (Avritti)Using the unit again and again
AxisThe line (vertical, horizontal or diagonal) about which parts are balanced
GroundBackground of the design
Border (Patti)A decorative band that runs around or along a surface

Types of design

Explanation of the figure:

  • Geometric – made of circles, triangles, hexagons, squares; constructed with compass and scale.
  • Floral / natural – flowers, leaves, creepers, birds simplified into patterns.
  • Alpana / Rangoli – traditional floor art, mostly symmetrical, made of dots, lines and lotus-like forms.
  • Border – a motif repeated in a straight band.

Traditional floor decorations from different regions, which can inspire your motifs, include Chowk Purna (Uttar Pradesh), Alpana (Bengal), Rangoli, Kolam (Tamil Nadu), Aipan (Uttarakhand) and Mandana (Rajasthan).

Principles of a good design

  • Balance – symmetrical (mirror), asymmetrical or radial.
  • Rhythm – regular repetition or progression gives the eye movement.
  • Unity and harmony – all parts look like one family.
  • Contrast – light-dark, large-small to avoid dullness.
  • Proportion – sizes of motifs fit the shape.
  • Originality – your own arrangement, not a copy of a printed pattern.

Ways of repeating a unit

  • Translation – the same unit moved in a straight line (borders).
  • Rotation – the unit turned around a centre (radial designs).
  • Reflection – a mirror image across an axis (symmetrical designs).
  • Alternation – two units or two colours used one after the other.

Step by step

Explanation of the figure: A single unit (one petal) is drawn in one sector; the same unit is rotated 8 times around the centre (the animation shows each petal appearing); finally colours are applied alternately and a border of dots is added.

  1. Draw the shape (circle, square, hexagon, etc.) in the size given.
  2. Divide it with light construction lines into 4, 6 or 8 equal parts.
  3. Draw your original unit in one part.
  4. Repeat it by tracing or measuring in the other parts, keeping it identical.
  5. Add a centre motif and a border to finish the composition.
  6. Fix a colour scheme of 3–4 colours and apply flat colours evenly.
  7. Outline motifs with a darker colour if suitable; clean up the construction lines.

Geometric design: construction

Explanation of the figure: Draw a circle (e.g. radius 4 cm in the exam, 2.5 cm here). Without changing the compass opening, step round the circle with arcs to get 6 equally spaced points. Joining alternate points gives two triangles (a hexagram); an inner circle touches the hexagon sides; semicircles on the six sides give petals. This is the base for many geometrical designs.

  1. Draw a circle with centre O; mark A on it.
  2. With the compass set to the radius, put the point on A and cut the circle on both sides; repeat from each new point until six points are found.
  3. Join alternate points to make the two triangles.
  4. Draw an inner circle touching the hexagon sides.
  5. Take the midpoint of each hexagon side and draw a semicircle (diameter = side) outwards for petals.
  6. Colour with a harmonious or complementary scheme.
Colour harmony tips Use the colour schemes of Chapter 1: analogous for calm designs, complementary for bold ones. Keep one colour dominant, repeat colours symmetrically, and keep the background plain so that the motifs stand out.
Points on which designs are judged Originality of the unit • Composition • Colour harmony • General effect and neatness.

4Pravidhik Kala – Technical Art

Definition Technical art is drawing with instruments to construct exact geometrical figures and drawings. It is the foundation of engineering, architectural and machine drawing.

Instruments

Explanation of the figure: Two set squares (45°-45°-90° and 30°-60°-90°), a protractor with a 0°–180° scale, a ruler in centimetres and millimetres, and a compass whose one leg has a needle and the other a pencil lead.

Rules of good technical drawing

  • Keep the pencil sharp (H or 2H for construction, HB for result lines).
  • Use three kinds of lines: given lines (as in the question – firm and dark), construction lines (thin and light, arcs and helping lines) and result/required lines (dark and bold).
  • Do not erase construction lines – the examiner checks the method by them.
  • Name points with capital letters (A, B, C…).
  • Fix the paper firmly, set the compass carefully, and do not change its opening during one step.
  • Be neat: no smudges, no repeated lines, no overwriting.
Note In earlier board papers the technical art question had several construction problems from which three had to be attempted, with the instruction that given, construction and required lines must be clear and that construction lines must not be rubbed out. Practise all constructions below.
About the animations In every construction figure the steps are drawn one by one (blue = construction, red = result). Use the ↻ button on a figure to replay it.

1. Bisecting a line and an angle

Steps – perpendicular bisector of AB:

  1. Draw the line AB.
  2. With centre A and a radius more than half of AB draw an arc above and below the line.
  3. With centre B and the same radius draw arcs cutting the first ones at P and Q.
  4. Join P and Q. PQ cuts AB at M; AM = MB and PQ ⟂ AB.

Steps – bisector of angle AOB:

  1. With centre O draw an arc cutting OA at C and OB at D.
  2. With centres C and D and the same radius draw two arcs meeting at E.
  3. Join O and E and extend it. OE bisects the angle: ∠AOE = ∠EOB.

2. Constructing standard angles

  1. 60°: draw OA, arc from O cutting it at C, arc of the same radius from C cutting the first arc at D; join OD.
  2. 30°: bisect the 60° angle as above.
  3. 90° at a point O on a line: cut equal parts OC and OD on both sides, draw arcs from C and D with a larger radius to meet at E; join OE.
  4. 45°: bisect a 90° angle.

Using these, other angles can be made, e.g. 120° = 60°+60°, 15° = bisect 30°, 75° = 60°+15°, 105° = 90°+15°.

3. Equilateral triangle and its incircle

  1. Draw AB = 6 cm.
  2. With centres A and B and radius 6 cm draw arcs meeting at C.
  3. Join AC and BC.
  4. Bisect angles A and B; the bisectors meet at I (incentre).
  5. Drop a perpendicular IF from I to AB.
  6. With centre I and radius IF draw the circle; it touches all three sides.

4. Triangle when three sides are given, and its circumcircle

  1. Draw AB = 6 cm.
  2. With centre A and radius 5 cm draw an arc; with centre B and radius 4 cm draw another arc meeting it at C.
  3. Join AC and BC to get the triangle.
  4. Draw perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC; they meet at O (circumcentre).
  5. With centre O and radius OA draw the circle; it passes through A, B and C.
Remember A triangle is possible only if the sum of any two sides is greater than the third side. Incentre = meeting point of angle bisectors (inside the triangle); circumcentre = meeting point of perpendicular bisectors of the sides.

5. Square and regular octagon

  1. Draw AB (5 cm); erect perpendiculars at A and B.
  2. With radius AB cut them at D (from A) and C (from B).
  3. Join DC – ABCD is a square.
  4. Draw diagonals AC and BD meeting at O.
  5. With centres A, B, C, D in turn and radius AO draw arcs cutting the sides.
  6. These eight points joined in order give a regular octagon.

6. Regular hexagon and pentagon in a circle

Hexagon: the side of a regular hexagon equals the radius of its circumscribed circle. Step round the circle with the radius six times and join the points.

Pentagon:

  1. Draw a circle with two perpendicular diameters; let C be the top end of the vertical one and B the right end of the horizontal one.
  2. Find the midpoint M of OB.
  3. With centre M and radius MC draw an arc cutting the horizontal diameter at N (on the other side of O).
  4. CN is the side of the pentagon. With this opening step round the circle from C to get the five points; join them.

7. Dividing a line into equal parts

  1. Draw AB. From A draw any line AC at a convenient angle.
  2. On AC mark five equal parts with the compass (points 1 to 5).
  3. Join point 5 to B.
  4. Draw lines through 4, 3, 2, 1 parallel to 5B (set square method) to meet AB at d, c, b, a.
  5. AB is divided into five equal parts: Aa = ab = bc = cd = dB.

8. Tangents to a circle

At a point P on the circle: join OP and draw a line through P perpendicular to OP – this is the tangent (a tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact).

From an outside point Q:

  1. Join OQ and bisect it at M.
  2. With centre M and radius MO draw a circle; it cuts the given circle at T₁ and T₂.
  3. Join QT₁ and QT₂ – these are the two tangents (angle in a semicircle is 90°, so OT ⟂ QT).

9. Scales

Scale A scale lets us draw big objects smaller (or tiny ones bigger) in proportion. Representative Fraction (R.F.) = length of the drawing ÷ actual length (both in the same unit). Because it is a ratio, R.F. has no unit. Example: 1 cm = 1 m gives R.F. = 1/100.
  1. Find the length of the scale: R.F. × maximum length (1/100 × 6 m = 6 cm).
  2. Draw a line a little longer (7 cm) and make a narrow rectangle on it.
  3. Divide the right 6 cm into 6 equal primary divisions (1 m each).
  4. Divide the extra left division into 10 equal sub-divisions (1 dm each).
  5. Number the divisions: 0 at the first primary mark, 1, 2… to the right; 10, 5 on the sub-divisions.
  6. To show 4.3 m, put the compass at 4 m (right of 0) and the other point at the 3rd sub-division left of 0.

10. Isometric drawing

Explanation: In isometric projection the three axes make equal angles of 120° with each other: one is vertical and two slope at 30° to the horizontal. Edges parallel to the axes are drawn along them; hidden edges are dotted. Strictly, lengths are shortened to 0.816 of true length on the isometric scale, but in an isometric drawing true lengths are used.

  1. Draw the three isometric axes from a point (vertical, 30° right, 30° left).
  2. Mark the edge length along each axis.
  3. Draw lines parallel to the axes to complete the three visible faces.
  4. Draw hidden edges with dotted lines (if needed).
Quick facts Interior angle of regular polygon = (n−2)×180°/n: triangle 60°, square 90°, pentagon 108°, hexagon 120°, octagon 135°. Angle in a semicircle = 90°. Tangent ⟂ radius.

5Smriti Chitran – Memory Drawing

Definition Drawing a familiar object in pencil from memory, after careful earlier observation, showing its correct shape, proportion and character, is memory drawing (Smriti Chitran).

In past papers the objects were everyday things such as a jug, surahi (water pot), open umbrella, cup and plate, open book, kite, cricket bat and ball. The drawing is done in pencil, normally not smaller than 15 cm, and is judged mainly on the similarity (anurupta) of forms and line quality (rekha saushthav).

How to prepare

  • Observe real objects from different sides; note the number of parts, their relative sizes and how they join.
  • Draw each object 5–10 times at home, first looking at it, later without looking.
  • Learn the construction method of each object (axis, boxes, ellipses).

Ellipse: the key to round objects

Explanation of the figure: A circle (such as the mouth of a jug or cup) looks like an ellipse when seen from the side. The higher your eye level, the wider (rounder) the ellipse; as your eye comes down to the level of the top, it narrows and finally becomes a straight line. The red dashed line is the minor axis (always along the axis of the object) and the blue dashed line is the major axis (always at right angles to it).

  • Ellipses have no corners – draw them smooth, not like an egg or a lemon.
  • The lower ellipse (base) is always a little rounder than the top one in a standing object.

Construction of common objects

Explanation of the figure: In each object first the blue construction lines (axis, box, diagonals, ellipses) are drawn, then the red outline is added.

ObjectMethod
JugCentral axis → rectangle for the body → ellipse for the mouth and base → curved sides narrowing at the neck → handle as two parallel curves.
SurahiAxis → large round body (circle) + narrow neck (cylinder) + small mouth ellipse → small handle on one side and spout on the other.
KiteRhombus (diamond) → two sticks (diagonals) crossing at the centre → string bows → curved tail with small cross bows.
Cup and plateWide ellipse for the plate, smaller one inside for the base ring → cup on the central axis with its own mouth ellipse → ring-shaped handle.

Open bookTwo slightly slanted four-sided pages meeting at the spine → curved top edges → page lines at the sides.
Cricket bat and ballLong rectangle for the blade with narrower handle; ball as a circle with curved seam lines.

Step by step

  1. Decide the position and size (not less than 15 cm); mark the central axis lightly.
  2. Draw the outer box, then the basic forms (circles, cylinders, ellipses).
  3. Check proportions: relative widths and heights of parts.
  4. Draw the final outline in smooth, confident lines.
  5. Add details – handle, patterns, ribs, folds.
  6. Use light shading only if it helps; never smudge. Do not erase the whole construction – lighten it.
Line quality Use continuous, sure strokes instead of tiny scratchy ones; vary pressure (thicker for near/shadow side, thinner for far/light side); keep curves smooth and corners crisp.

7Short Answer Questions (2–3 marks)

Click a question to open its answer.

What is meant by core practical art?

It is the practical part of the paper in which the student chooses one of three arts – landscape (nature scene) painting, design (Alekhan) or technical art – and shows personal skill in it.

Name the three choices in core practical art.

Prakritik Drishya Chitran (landscape), Alekhan (design) and Pravidhik Kala (technical art).

What is landscape painting?

Painting a natural scene – sky, land, water, trees, huts, figures – with colours, showing depth, light and mood.

What is the horizon line?

The line where earth and sky appear to meet; it lies at the artist's eye level and fixes the proportion of sky and land.

Name the three grounds of a landscape.

Foreground (near, dark, detailed), middle ground, and background (far, pale, soft).

What is aerial perspective?

The effect by which distant things appear lighter, bluer and less detailed because of the atmosphere between them and the viewer.

Why should a painter go from light to dark colours in a wash?

Water colour is transparent: a dark colour cannot be lightened later, but a light area can be darkened. So light washes are done first and darks last.

What is a graded wash?

A wash in which the colour gradually changes from dark to light by adding more water, e.g. a sunset sky lighter near the horizon.

What is wet-on-wet technique?

Painting on a damp surface so that colours spread and blend softly; used for clouds, water and misty hills.

What is Alekhan?

Alekhan is a decorative design: a rhythmic and balanced arrangement of motifs and colours in a given shape, not a realistic picture.

What is a motif?

The basic decorative form of a design, such as a petal, leaf, bird or geometric shape.

What is a unit in design?

The smallest part of a design which is repeated to build the whole.

Name the four types of design.

Geometric, floral (natural), alpana/rangoli and border (patti) designs.

What is a border design?

A motif repeated in a straight band to decorate the edge of a surface such as a cloth, wall or page.

Name the methods of repeating a motif.

Translation (shifting), rotation (turning around a centre), reflection (mirror image) and alternation.

On what points is a design judged?

Originality of the unit, composition, colour harmony and general effect/neatness.

What is technical art?

Drawing geometrical figures and constructions accurately with instruments such as scale, compass, set squares and protractor.

Name the three kinds of lines in a construction.

Given lines, construction lines and required (result) lines.

Why should construction lines not be erased?

Because they show the method used. The examiner checks them to confirm that the figure was constructed correctly and not drawn by guess.

How do you bisect a line?

Draw arcs of equal radius (more than half the line) from both ends so that they cross above and below; join the crossing points. This line is the perpendicular bisector.

How is a 60° angle constructed?

Draw a line OA; with centre O draw an arc cutting it at C; with centre C and the same radius cut the arc at D; join OD. ∠AOD = 60°.

What is the incentre of a triangle?

The point where the three angle bisectors meet; it is the centre of the inscribed circle.

What is the circumcentre of a triangle?

The point where the perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet; it is the centre of the circle through all three vertices.

What is the side of a regular hexagon in terms of its circle?

It is equal to the radius of the circumscribed circle.

What is the Representative Fraction?

The ratio of the length on the drawing to the actual length of the object, both in the same unit. It has no unit.

What is an isometric drawing?

A pictorial drawing in which the three axes are 120° apart (one vertical, two at 30° to the horizontal) and lengths along the axes are drawn to true size.

What is memory drawing?

Drawing an object in pencil from memory, based on earlier observation, with correct form, proportion and line quality.

Why does a circle look like an ellipse in a drawing?

Because it is seen at an angle. The lower the eye level, the narrower the ellipse; at eye level it becomes a straight line.

8Long Answer Questions (5–6 marks)

Detailed answers – add a neat diagram wherever possible.

Explain the three choices of core practical art and how a student should choose one.

1. Landscape painting – painting a natural scene with water colours or pastels; needs composition, perspective and colour sense.

2. Alekhan (design) – an original decorative pattern; needs imagination, balance, rhythm and colour harmony.

3. Technical art – geometrical constructions with instruments; needs accuracy and neatness.

Choice: pick the one that matches your strength and that you have practised most. Technical art is the most predictable; landscape rewards colour skill; design rewards imagination and clean finishing. Only one option is to be attempted in the paper.

What is landscape painting? Explain composition in a landscape.

Landscape painting depicts nature with colour to create depth and mood.

Composition:

  • Three grounds – foreground, middle ground, background.
  • Horizon line – high, middle or low; decides the share of sky and land.
  • Focal point – a main interest placed off-centre.
  • Balance – a heavy object on one side balanced by others on the other.
  • Leading lines – road/river leads the eye in.

(Draw a rectangular scene with labelled grounds.)

Explain perspective in a landscape painting with examples.

Linear perspective: parallel lines such as a road or river seem to meet at a vanishing point on the horizon.

Aerial perspective: distant hills are pale, bluish and soft, nearer ones darker, warmer and sharper.

Size and overlapping: nearer objects are larger and hide those behind.

Colour temperature: warm colours advance, cool recede. These together create the illusion of depth on a flat sheet.

Describe the step-by-step procedure for painting a landscape in water colours.
  1. Fix the paper and draw the required rectangle lightly.
  2. Fix the horizon and sketch main shapes lightly.
  3. Paint the sky with a graded wash; add clouds wet-on-wet.
  4. Paint the water in softer sky colours.
  5. Paint distant hills in pale blue-grey.
  6. Paint middle ground, then foreground in darker, warmer colours.
  7. Add details – figures, birds, branches, shadows.
  8. Finish with dark accents and clean edges.

Rule: large to small, light to dark.

What is Alekhan? Describe its types and principles.

Alekhan is a decorative, stylised design in a given shape.

Types: geometric, floral, alpana/rangoli, border.

Principles: balance, rhythm, unity/harmony, contrast, proportion and originality.

Repetition methods: translation, rotation, reflection, alternation.

Colours are applied flat, with a limited, harmonious scheme (3–4 colours).

Explain the steps in making a design in a circle. How is colour harmony achieved?
  1. Draw the circle of the given size.
  2. Divide it into equal parts (4, 6 or 8).
  3. Draw an original unit in one part.
  4. Repeat it in all parts.
  5. Add a centre motif and a border.
  6. Colour using a scheme and outline as needed.

Harmony: choose analogous colours for softness or complementary for contrast; keep one dominant colour; repeat colours symmetrically; use a plain background.

Construct a regular hexagon in a circle. Write the steps.
  1. Draw a circle with centre O and any radius r; mark point A on it.
  2. With the compass opening equal to r, put the point on A and cut the circle at B.
  3. From B cut at C, from C at D, from D at E, from E at F.
  4. Join A, B, C, D, E, F in order.

The side of a regular hexagon equals the radius of its circumscribed circle. (Keep all arcs.)

Construct an equilateral triangle of side 6 cm and draw its inscribed circle.
  1. Draw AB = 6 cm.
  2. With A and B as centres and radius 6 cm draw arcs meeting at C; join AC, BC.
  3. Bisect ∠A and ∠B; the bisectors meet at I.
  4. Draw IF perpendicular to AB.
  5. With centre I and radius IF draw the incircle, touching all sides.
Draw a triangle with sides 6 cm, 5 cm and 4 cm and construct its circumcircle.
  1. Draw AB = 6 cm.
  2. Arc from A radius 5 cm and arc from B radius 4 cm meet at C.
  3. Join AC and BC.
  4. Draw perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC; they meet at O.
  5. With centre O and radius OA draw the circle through A, B, C.
Construct tangents to a circle from an outside point.
  1. Draw the circle (centre O) and mark outside point Q; join OQ.
  2. Bisect OQ at M.
  3. With centre M and radius MO draw a circle cutting the first at T₁ and T₂.
  4. Join QT₁ and QT₂ – these are tangents.

Reason: ∠OT₁Q is an angle in a semicircle = 90°, so the tangent is perpendicular to the radius.

What is R.F.? Draw a plain scale of R.F. 1/100 to read up to 6 m in decimetres.

R.F. = drawing length / actual length (same units, no unit).

  1. Length of scale = 1/100 × 6 m = 6 cm; add 1 cm on the left (total 7 cm).
  2. Make 6 primary divisions of 1 cm each (1 m each).
  3. Divide the left extra part into 10 sub-divisions (1 dm each).
  4. Write 0 at the first primary mark, 1…6 to the right and 10, 5 on the left.
What is memory drawing? Explain how to draw a jug and a cup with plate.

Memory drawing is drawing an object from memory after observation.

Jug: axis → rectangle → mouth ellipse → body curves → base ellipse → handle.

Cup and plate: plate as a wide ellipse with an inner ring → cup on the axis with its own mouth ellipse → body narrowing to the base → ring-shaped handle.

Remember: ellipse minor axis lies along the object's axis; base ellipse is slightly rounder than the top. The work is judged on similarity of forms and line quality.

11True / False

Read the statement, then click the yellow strip to check.

  1. A student has to attempt all three core practical arts. False — Only one of the three is attempted.
  2. In a landscape the foreground is usually darker and more detailed. True — Near things are darker and sharper.
  3. A low horizon shows more sky. True — More of the sheet is sky.
  4. In water colours dark washes should be done first. False — Light washes first, dark last.
  5. Wet-on-wet gives soft blended edges. True — Colours spread on damp paper.
  6. Alekhan is a realistic portrait. False — It is a stylised, decorative design.
  7. Rotation of a unit makes a radial design. True — Turning about a centre.
  8. Construction lines are to be rubbed out at the end. False — They must remain visible.
  9. A 30° angle can be obtained by bisecting 60°. True — Half of 60° is 30°.
  10. The incentre is the meeting point of the perpendicular bisectors. False — That is the circumcentre; the incentre is from angle bisectors.
  11. The side of a regular hexagon is equal to the radius of its circumscribed circle. True — This is why six arcs of radius r step around the circle.
  12. R.F. is measured in centimetres. False — It is a ratio without unit.
  13. In isometric axes the angle between any two axes is 120°. True — One vertical, two at 30°.
  14. Memory drawing is done with water colours. False — It is a pencil drawing.
  15. A circle appears as a straight line when viewed at its own eye level. True — The ellipse closes to a line.
