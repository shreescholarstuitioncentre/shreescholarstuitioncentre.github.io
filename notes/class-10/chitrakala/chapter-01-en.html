1Art and the Elements of Art – Introduction

What is art? Art is a creative human activity in which a person expresses feelings, ideas, imagination and a sense of beauty through line, colour, form and other means.
Painting Creating forms with lines and colours on a flat surface (paper, canvas, wall, wooden panel) is called painting.

What are the elements of art?

A building is made of bricks, cement and iron; a meal is made of different ingredients. In the same way a picture is built from basic parts. These basic building blocks of a picture are called the Elements of Art.

Generally six elements are studied: Line, Form, Colour, Tone, Texture and Space.

Explanation of the figure: The elements of art sit in the centre with the six elements around it. They depend on each other: line creates form, tone gives the form depth, and colour brings out the feeling.

ElementMeaning in one line
LineThe path made by a moving point; the foundation of a picture
FormOuter shape of an object – shape (2D) and form (3D)
ColourThe element that creates mood and atmosphere
ToneLightness and darkness produced by light and shade
TextureSurface quality – rough or smooth
SpaceThe area in a picture – inside, around and between objects
Note Some books list the elements with slightly different names or numbers. In the exam, follow the list given in your own textbook; the concepts below are common to all.

2Line

Definition When a point moves in some direction, the path it traces is called a line. A line has length; its width is negligible.

Line is considered the most basic element of painting because the outline of every shape is made with it. Indian art, especially the Ajanta murals, is widely praised for its rhythmic use of line.

Qualities of line

  • Length – short or long.
  • Direction – horizontal, vertical, diagonal.
  • Thickness – thick (weight, strength) or thin (delicacy).
  • Character – straight (hard) or curved (soft).

Types of lines and their feelings

Explanation of the figure: In six panels the lines draw themselves. Below each panel are the line's name and the feeling it creates.

LineFeeling / effectExample in a picture
HorizontalCalm, rest, stabilityHorizon, still lake, a sleeping person
VerticalHeight, strength, dignityTree trunk, pillar, tower
DiagonalMovement, action, instabilityRunning player, slope, falling tree
CurvedSoftness, grace, flexibilityRainbow, petal, a dancer's pose
ZigzagTension, excitement, sudden movementLightning, hill road with sharp bends
WavyFlow, rhythm, motion of water or windRiver ripples, sand dunes

Uses of line in a picture

  1. Outline / contour – shows the boundary of an object.
  2. Expression – hard lines for anger, soft lines for love.
  3. Movement and direction – leads the viewer's eye through the picture.
  4. Depth / shading – parallel or crossed lines (hatching) build tone.
  5. Texture – short strokes suggest a surface.
Remember Horizontal = rest | Vertical = strength | Diagonal = movement | Curved = softness | Zigzag = tension | Wavy = flow

3Form and Shape

Definition The outer structure of an object is its form. When lines enclose an area, a shape is formed.

Explanation of the figure: The square, circle and triangle on top are flat (2D) shapes. When depth is added with light and shade they turn into solid (3D) forms – cube, sphere and cone.

BasisShapeForm
Dimensions2D – length, width3D – length, width, depth
ExamplesSquare, circle, triangleCube, sphere, cone, cylinder
How shownBy line or flat colourBy tone (light and shade)

Kinds of form

  • Geometric – regular, measurable: square, circle, cube, sphere.
  • Natural / organic – irregular, found in nature: leaf, cloud, human figure.
  • Abstract – not resembling real objects; from the artist's imagination.
Drawing tip Before drawing an object, break it into basic forms – a pot = cylinder + sphere, a glass = truncated cone, an orange = sphere, a box = cube. Proportion and structure then come out right.

4Colour as an Element

Definition The effect produced on the eye by light is called colour. It is the most attractive element of a picture: it identifies objects and creates feeling and mood.

Colour theory (colour wheel, primary-secondary-tertiary, complementary, warm-cool, properties, schemes) is studied in detail in the later sections on Colour Theory.

  • Red and orange suggest heat, energy and excitement.
  • Blue and green suggest coolness and calm.
  • Colour also shows distance – warm colours seem to come forward, cool colours recede.

5Tone (Value)

Definition The degree of lightness or darkness produced on an object by light and shadow is called tone. White is the lightest tone, black the darkest, with many greys between.

Explanation of the figure:

  • Top left: a 9-step scale from white to black. A picture mainly in light tones is high key; one mainly in dark tones is low key.
  • Bottom left: three shading methods – hatching (parallel lines), cross-hatching (lines in two directions, darker) and stippling (dots).
  • Right: the five parts of light and shade on a sphere.

Parts of light and shade on a sphere

PartMeaning
HighlightBrightest spot, facing the light source
Half-toneGradual darkening from highlight towards shadow
Core shadowDarkest part of the object, where light does not reach
Reflected lightFaint light bounced back from the surroundings, lightening the edge
Cast shadowShadow thrown on the ground or wall, away from the light

Importance of tone

  1. Gives volume and solidity to flat shapes.
  2. Creates the illusion of depth.
  3. Sets the mood – dark tones for seriousness, light tones for cheerfulness.
  4. Shows the direction of light.
Shading rules Start with a light hand; fix the light direction and put shadow on the opposite side; increase tone gradually so the blending stays smooth.

6Texture

Definition The surface quality of an object – rough, smooth, soft, hard – is called texture.

Explanation of the figure: Bark is rough, glass is smooth, a brick wall is hard and coarse, cloth is woven, and water shows a rippled texture.

Types of texture

  • Actual (tactile) texture – can be felt by touch: sandpaper, cloth stuck in a collage, thick paint (impasto).
  • Visual (simulated) texture – the surface is flat, but line, tone and colour create the illusion of roughness or smoothness.

How to show texture in a drawing

  1. Change pencil pressure (light – smooth, heavy – rough).
  2. Use dots, criss-cross lines or short strokes.
  3. Dry-brush or sponge painting.
  4. Frottage (rubbing) – place paper over a coin or leaf and rub with pencil.
  5. Collage – stick cloth, sand or leaves.
Remember Texture = surface quality. A smooth surface shows a sharp highlight; a rough surface scatters light.

7Space

Definition The empty or filled area around, between and within objects in a picture is called space.

Explanation of the figure:

  • Left: the dark vase is the positive space; the pale area around it is the negative space. Together they form a balanced composition.
  • Right: in perspective the road narrows and meets at the vanishing point on the horizon line. The poles look smaller as they go farther.

Kinds of space

  • Positive space – the area occupied by the object or shape.
  • Negative space – the empty area surrounding the object.

Ways to show depth

  1. Size – near objects large, far objects small.
  2. Overlapping – the front object hides part of the one behind.
  3. Linear perspective – parallel lines meet at the vanishing point.
  4. Colour / atmospheric effect – distant things are lighter, hazier, bluish; near things darker and sharper.
  5. Placement – far objects placed higher, near the horizon.
Note Indian miniature paintings (Rajasthani, Pahari, etc.) usually have flat colour fields and limited linear perspective; space is shown by overlapping and by stacking things higher or lower.

8Principles of Composition and the Six Limbs of Indian Painting

If the elements are the "ingredients", the principles are the rules for arranging them. Arranging elements pleasingly is called composition.

1. Balance

Distributing visual weight so that the picture feels stable and pleasing.

Explanation of the figure:

  • Symmetrical (formal): identical shapes on both sides of a centre line – calm, stable (e.g. temple gateway).
  • Asymmetrical (informal): different objects but equal weight – a big object near the centre balances a small one far away. More natural and lively.
  • Radial: everything spreads from a centre – flower, rangoli, wheel.

2. Rhythm and 3. Emphasis

Explanation of the figure: Rhythm – repetition or gradual change of shapes gives the eye a path to follow. Emphasis – one object is made to stand out through colour, size or tone so the viewer sees it first.

Other principles

  • Proportion – correct size relationship between parts.
  • Unity / Harmony – all parts work together to give one effect.
  • Contrast – light-dark or warm-cool opposition for interest.
  • Variety – avoids monotony.

Shadanga – six limbs of Indian painting

A commentary on Vatsyayana's Kamasutra (Yashodhara's Jayamangala) lists six limbs of painting:

LimbMeaning
RupabhedaKnowledge of the differences in forms
PramanaCorrect measurement and proportion
BhavaExpression of emotion
Lavanya YojanaInfusing grace and artistic charm
SadrishyaResemblance to the real form
Varnika-bhangaArtistic use of colours and brush
Memory trick Ru-Pra-Bha-La-Sa-Va : Rupabheda, Pramana, Bhava, Lavanya, Sadrishya, Varnikabhanga.

9Colour Theory – 1: What is Colour? Light and Pigment

Around 1666 Sir Isaac Newton passed sunlight through a glass prism and found that white light splits into seven colours – violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red (VIBGYOR). The rainbow is a natural example.

Memory trick VIBGYOR = Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, Red.

Why does an object look coloured?

An object absorbs some colours of the light falling on it and reflects others. The reflected colour reaching our eye is the colour we see. A leaf looks green because it reflects green and absorbs the rest.

Two systems of primaries

SystemPrimary coloursUse
Light (additive)Red, green, blue (RGB)TV and mobile screens; all three make white light
PigmentRed, yellow, blue (traditional in art)Paints; mixing all gives a dark/grey tone
Note In Class 10 Drawing & Painting the pigment primaries – red, yellow, blue – are studied.

Achromatic colours

Black, white and grey have no hue, so they are called achromatic. They are added to other colours to change value – white lightens, black darkens.

10Colour Theory – 2: Colour Wheel and Classification

Colour wheel Arranging colours in a circle according to their relationships is called a colour wheel. It is the map for mixing colours and planning colour schemes.

Explanation of the figure: The wheel has 12 colours – 3 primary, 3 secondary, 6 tertiary. The small circles on the inner ring show P / S / T for each colour. Starting with yellow at the top, colours change clockwise.

GroupColoursHow made
PrimaryRed, yellow, blueCannot be made by mixing; parents of all other colours
SecondaryOrange, green, violetEqual mix of two primaries
TertiaryYellow-orange, red-orange, red-violet, blue-violet, blue-green, yellow-greenA primary + the neighbouring secondary
  • Naming rule: in a tertiary colour the primary is named first (e.g. yellow-green).
  • Achromatic colours (black, white, grey) are not on the wheel.

Colour mixing

Explanation of the figure: Where two primary circles overlap, a secondary colour appears – yellow + red = orange, yellow + blue = green, red + blue = violet. The common centre of all three gives brown/grey.

Formula Red + Yellow = Orange  |  Yellow + Blue = Green  |  Blue + Red = Violet

11Colour Theory – 3: Complementary, Warm and Cool Colours

Complementary colours Colours placed exactly opposite each other on the colour wheel are complementary colours.

Explanation of the figure: Three pairs are placed side by side (they seem to vibrate – side by side they make each other look vivid). Below each pair is the dull grey-brown they produce when mixed.

  • Red ↔ Green, Yellow ↔ Violet, Blue ↔ Orange. (Tertiary pairs also exist – yellow-orange ↔ blue-violet, etc.)
  • The complement of a primary is the secondary made from the other two primaries (red's complement green = yellow + blue).
  • Side by side they intensify each other.
  • Mixed they neutralise each other into grey/brown.
  • To dull a colour, add a little of its complement.

Warm and cool colours

Explanation of the figure: An imaginary line divides the wheel. Colours on the right (yellow to red side) are warm (like sun and fire); colours on the left (green-blue-violet) are cool (like ice and water).

Warm coloursCool colours
ColoursRed, orange, yellowBlue, green, violet
FeelingHeat, energy, excitement, nearnessCoolness, calm, sadness, distance
In depthSeem to come forwardSeem to recede
ExamplesSun, fire, sunsetSky, sea, grass, ice

12Colour Theory – 4: Properties of Colour

Every colour has three properties: hue, value and intensity.

Explanation of the figure: Three strips show the three properties.

PropertyMeaningExample
HueThe basic name or identity of a colourRed, yellow, blue
ValueLightness or darkness of a colourPink (light red), maroon (dark red)
Intensity (Chroma)Purity, brightness or dullnessPure blue – high; greyed blue – low

Tint, Shade and Tone

  • Tint = pure colour + white → lighter (red + white = pink).
  • Shade = pure colour + black → darker (red + black = maroon).
  • Tone = pure colour + grey → duller.
Remember White added → Tint | Black added → Shade | Grey added → Tone (intensity falls)

13Colour Theory – 5: Colour Schemes

Colour scheme A planned way of choosing and combining colours so that a picture looks beautiful and balanced.

Explanation of the figure: In each mini wheel the chosen colours are bright and the rest are faded. The dashed line (line / triangle / rectangle) joining them shows the shape of the scheme.

SchemeMeaningEffect / example
MonochromaticDifferent values of one hueCalm, unified; many tints of blue
Analogous2–3 neighbouring coloursSoft harmony; yellow, yellow-green, green
ComplementaryTwo opposite coloursStrong contrast; blue-orange
Split complementaryOne colour + the two beside its complementContrast, less harsh
TriadicThree equally spaced coloursLively and balanced; red-yellow-blue
TetradicTwo complementary pairs (4)Rich, but hard to handle – let one colour dominate
Tip When painting a scheme, use one colour as dominant (60%), another as supporting (30%), and a third as accent (10%).

15Practical Help: Drawing a 12-Colour Wheel (Step by Step)

  1. Draw a circle of 10–12 cm radius and a small circle (about 3 cm radius) at its centre.
  2. With a protractor mark every 30° to divide the circle into 12 equal parts; draw straight lines from the centre to the marks.
  3. Paint yellow in the top part, then red in the 4th part (120° on) and blue in the 8th – these are the primaries.
  4. In the part midway between two primaries, paint the secondary – orange between yellow and red, violet between red and blue, green between blue and yellow.
  5. Fill the 6 remaining parts with tertiary colours, mixing the two neighbours.
  6. Keep colours flat, clean and inside the lines; write each name at the rim (mark P for primary, S for secondary).
  7. Write "Colour Wheel" in the centre circle and add title, name and roll number.
Caution Use thick poster colour; wash the brush each time so colours stay clean; let a colour dry before painting its neighbour.

17Short Answer Questions (2–3 marks)

Click a question to open its answer.

What do you understand by art?

Art is the creative expression of a person's feelings, ideas and sense of beauty through line, colour, form and similar means. Painting is the art form made on a flat surface.

Name the elements of art.

Line, form, colour, tone, texture and space – the six elements from which a picture is built.

What is a line?

The path traced by a moving point is a line. It has length only. It is the most basic element because the boundary of every shape is drawn with it.

How do horizontal and vertical lines differ in feeling?

Horizontal lines give calm, rest and stability (horizon, a person lying down). Vertical lines give height, strength and dignity (tree, pillar).

What feeling does a diagonal line give?

Movement, action and instability, like a running player or a slope.

What is the effect of a curved line?

Softness, grace and flexibility – a petal, a rainbow or a dancer's pose.

Differentiate between shape and form.

Shape is flat (2D: length and width), e.g. square, circle. Form is solid (3D: length, width, depth), e.g. cube, sphere. Form is shown through tone.

Differentiate between geometric and natural forms.

Geometric forms are regular and mathematical (square, cube, sphere); natural forms are irregular and taken from nature (leaf, cloud, human).

What is tone?

The amount of lightness or darkness produced on an object by light and shadow. White is the lightest and black the darkest tone.

What are high key and low key?

A picture dominated by light tones is high key (cheerful); one dominated by dark tones is low key (serious or mysterious).

What is texture? Name its types.

Texture is the surface quality of an object. Actual texture is felt by touch; visual texture is only an illusion created by line, tone and colour.

Define space in art.

Space is the area around, between and within objects in a picture. It can be positive (occupied by the object) or negative (surrounding it).

Explain positive and negative space with an example.

In a picture of a vase, the vase itself is positive space and the background around it is negative space. A good composition balances both.

What is a colour wheel?

An arrangement of colours in a circle according to their relationships. It has 12 colours: 3 primary, 3 secondary and 6 tertiary.

What are primary colours? Name them.

Colours that cannot be produced by mixing others and from which all other colours are made: red, yellow and blue.

What are secondary colours? How are they made?

Equal mixtures of two primaries – red + yellow = orange, yellow + blue = green, blue + red = violet.

What are tertiary colours? Give two examples.

Made by mixing a primary with its neighbouring secondary, e.g. yellow-green and blue-violet.

What are complementary colours? Give three pairs.

Colours opposite each other on the wheel: red-green, yellow-violet, blue-orange. Side by side they look brighter; mixed they give grey/brown.

Name the warm and cool colours.

Warm: red, orange, yellow. Cool: blue, green, violet. Warm colours seem to advance, cool ones recede.

Name the three properties of colour.

Hue (identity), value (lightness/darkness), intensity (purity/brightness).

Differentiate between tint and shade.

Adding white to a colour makes a tint (red + white = pink). Adding black makes a shade (red + black = maroon).

Which colours are achromatic?

Black, white and grey, because they have no hue. They are not part of the colour wheel.

What is a monochromatic colour scheme?

A scheme using different values (lights and darks) of a single hue, giving a calm, unified effect.

What is an analogous colour scheme?

Use of two or three neighbouring colours on the wheel, e.g. yellow, yellow-green, green – giving soft harmony.

What is Shadanga? Name the six limbs.

The six limbs of Indian painting: Rupabheda, Pramana, Bhava, Lavanya Yojana, Sadrishya and Varnika-bhanga.

Name the three types of balance.

Symmetrical (formal), asymmetrical (informal) and radial.

18Long Answer Questions (5–6 marks)

Detailed answers for the exam – do not forget to draw the diagram.

Describe the elements of art.

The basic parts of a picture are the elements of art. There are six:

  1. Line – path of a moving point; shows boundary and feeling.
  2. Form – outer structure; shape (2D) and form (3D).
  3. Colour – creates mood, atmosphere and distance.
  4. Tone – lightness/darkness from light and shade; gives volume and depth.
  5. Texture – surface quality (rough/smooth).
  6. Space – area around and between objects; positive and negative.

A beautiful combination of these makes a picture effective. (Draw the hexagon diagram of the six elements.)

What is a line? Explain the types of lines with the feelings they express.

Definition: the path traced by a moving point.

  • Horizontal – calm, rest (horizon).
  • Vertical – height, strength (tree, pillar).
  • Diagonal – movement, action (runner).
  • Curved – softness, grace (flower, petal).
  • Zigzag – tension, excitement (lightning).
  • Wavy – flow, rhythm (river).

Thickness also matters – thick lines give weight, thin lines delicacy. (Draw six panels showing the lines.)

Differentiate between shape and form and describe the kinds of form.

Shape is a flat two-dimensional area (square, circle, triangle); form is a three-dimensional solid (cube, sphere, cone) that has depth. Tone is used to show form.

Kinds: (1) Geometric – regular (cube, cylinder); (2) Natural/organic – irregular (leaf, human); (3) Abstract – from imagination.

When drawing an object, breaking it into basic geometric forms makes the work easy and proportionate.

What is tone? Explain the parts of light and shade on a sphere with a diagram.

Tone: degree of lightness or darkness produced by light and shade.

Five parts on a sphere:

  1. Highlight – brightest, facing light.
  2. Half-tone – gradually darkening.
  3. Core shadow – darkest part.
  4. Reflected light – faint light bounced from surroundings at the edge.
  5. Cast shadow – shadow on the ground opposite the light.

Shading methods: hatching, cross-hatching, stippling and blending. (Draw a sphere and label the five parts.)

What is texture? How can texture be shown in a drawing?

Surface quality – rough, smooth, soft – is texture. Actual texture is felt by touch; visual texture is only seen.

Methods: varying pencil pressure; dots, lines and strokes; dry brush or sponge; frottage (rubbing); collage; thick paint (impasto). Draw a sharp highlight on smooth surfaces and broken short strokes on rough ones.

What is space? Explain positive-negative space and the ways of showing depth.

Space is the area within, around and between objects. The object's area is positive space; the area around it is negative space.

Showing depth: (1) near large, far small; (2) overlapping; (3) linear perspective – lines meeting at the vanishing point; (4) colour – distant things lighter and bluish, near things darker; (5) far objects placed higher near the horizon. (Draw a road in perspective.)

Draw a colour wheel and explain primary, secondary and tertiary colours.

The 12-colour wheel, starting with yellow at top and moving clockwise: yellow, yellow-orange, orange, red-orange, red, red-violet, violet, blue-violet, blue, blue-green, green, yellow-green.

  • Primary: red, yellow, blue – cannot be made by mixing.
  • Secondary: orange, green, violet – equal mixes of two primaries.
  • Tertiary: primary + adjacent secondary, e.g. yellow-green.

(Draw a circle, divide it into twelve 30° parts, paint and label.)

What are complementary colours? Describe their properties and effects.

Colours opposite each other on the wheel: red-green, yellow-violet, blue-orange.

  1. Side by side they intensify each other.
  2. Mixed, they form neutral grey/brown.
  3. A primary's complement is the secondary made from the other two primaries.
  4. A little complement added to a colour reduces its intensity.
  5. Useful in posters and paintings for contrast and attraction.
Explain the properties of colour. Also explain tint and shade.

Hue: the name of the colour.

Value: lightness/darkness; white added gives a tint, black added gives a shade.

Intensity: purity; reduced by adding grey or the complement; pure colour has the highest intensity.

Example: red (hue) → pink (light value) → maroon (dark value) → greyed red (low intensity).

What are colour schemes? Describe any four.

The planned way of choosing and combining colours is a colour scheme.

  • Monochromatic: values of one hue – calm.
  • Analogous: 2–3 neighbours – soft harmony (yellow, yellow-green, green).
  • Complementary: two opposites – strong contrast (blue-orange).
  • Triadic: three equally spaced colours – lively balance (red-yellow-blue).

Keep one colour dominant and others supporting.

Explain balance and rhythm with diagrams. Also write the Shadanga.

Balance: even distribution of visual weight – symmetrical, asymmetrical, radial.

Rhythm: repetition or gradual change of shapes giving the eye a flowing path.

Shadanga: Rupabheda, Pramana, Bhava, Lavanya Yojana, Sadrishya, Varnika-bhanga.

21True / False

Read the statement, then click the yellow strip to check.

  1. Line is regarded as the basic element of painting. True — Shape boundaries are drawn with lines.
  2. A diagonal line gives a feeling of calm. False — Diagonal gives movement; calm comes from horizontal.
  3. A shape is three-dimensional. False — A shape is 2D; form is 3D.
  4. Black is the darkest tone. True — Tone deepens from white to black.
  5. Texture can only be known by touch. False — Visual texture is perceived by sight.
  6. Negative space is the area around an object. True — That is its definition.
  7. Green is a primary colour. False — Green is secondary (yellow + blue).
  8. Red and green are complementary colours. True — They are opposite on the wheel.
  9. Mixing complementary colours makes them brighter. False — Mixing gives grey/brown; they look brighter only side by side.
  10. Blue is a cool colour. True — Blue suggests coolness.
  11. Black is part of the colour wheel. False — Black is achromatic.
  12. Adding black to a colour makes a shade. True — Black darkens a colour.
  13. Value means the name of a colour. False — The name is hue; value is lightness/darkness.
  14. The Shadanga has six limbs. True — Rupabheda, Pramana, Bhava, Lavanya, Sadrishya, Varnikabhanga.
  15. Warm colours seem to recede in a picture. False — Warm colours advance; cool colours recede.
