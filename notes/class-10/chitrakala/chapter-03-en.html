1Natural Landscape Drawing – Overview

Definition Natural landscape drawing (Prakritik Drishya Chitran) is the painting of a scene from nature – sky, land, hills, water, trees, huts, people, animals and birds – in which the artist arranges these parts into a pleasing picture and uses colour to show distance, light, season and mood.

It is Option 1 of the core practical art. You attempt it instead of Design (Alekhan) or Technical art. It is done in water colours or pastels (as stated in the question) on a rectangle of the size given, often about 20 cm × 15 cm, drawn on the sheet.

Why is landscape painting important?

  • It trains observation – you learn to see shapes, colours and light.
  • It combines all the elements of art (line, form, colour, tone, texture, space) and the principles (balance, rhythm, emphasis).
  • It develops control of the medium – washes, blending and brush work.
  • It expresses your feelings about nature – calm, joy, freshness, storm.

Typical themes

A village scene • sunrise or sunset • riverside or lake • a hill or mountain scene • a rainy day • a garden or orchard • a forest • a fair or market in the open • a seashore with a boat • a farm during harvest.

2Materials and Medium

Explanation of the figure: The palette holds the colours (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet, brown, white). A round brush draws details and a flat brush lays washes. The jar holds clean water; the sponge or tissue controls moisture and lifts colour. Pencil and eraser are for the first light sketch.

Paper

  • Use a drawing sheet that does not curl when wet; thick paper (cartridge/water-colour paper) is best.
  • Fix the sheet on a board with tape or clips so that it stays flat.
  • Draw the picture frame lightly with an HB pencil and a scale.

Brushes

  • Round brushes (small to medium, e.g. No. 2–6) – details, branches, figures.
  • Flat or large round brush (e.g. No. 10–12) – sky and large washes.
  • Wash brushes after every colour; store with the bristles up; never leave them standing in water.

3Colour in Landscape

1. Mixing the landscape colours

Explanation of the figure: The chart shows common landscape colours and an approximate recipe for each. The colours on your own palette will look a little different, so always test a mix on scrap paper first.

  • Do not use one flat green. Make a family of greens: lighter and yellower for sunlit grass, bluer and darker for shadows and distant trees.
  • Dull a colour by adding a touch of its complement (Chapter 1) – a little red to green, or a little blue to orange.
  • Never use pure black for shadows; use blue + red (violet) or blue + burnt sienna for livelier shadows.

2. Warm and cool colours

  • Warm colours (red, orange, yellow) advance; cool colours (blue, violet, blue-green) recede.
  • Foreground: warmer, darker, stronger. Background: cooler, paler, softer.
  • Light parts are generally warm and shadows cool (or the reverse at sunset).

4. Seasons

Explanation of the figure: The tree has the same structure but the colours of leaves, sky and ground change with the season; leaves fall in autumn and rain lines fall in the monsoon.

  • Summer: strong sun, bright greens, dry yellow ground, deep blue sky.
  • Monsoon: heavy grey clouds, dark rich greens, puddles reflecting the sky.
  • Autumn: orange, yellow and brown leaves; golden light; fallen leaves on ground.
  • Winter: bare branches, pale cool sky, mist, muted colours.
Harmony tip Choose a main colour scheme before you start: analogous (e.g. yellow-orange-red for sunset) for calm, or complementary (e.g. blue sky with orange sun) for drama. Keep one colour dominant and use the other as an accent.

4Composition and Layout

1. Layout on the sheet

Explanation of the figure: A frame (here 20 cm × 15 cm) is centred on the sheet with equal side margins. Light lines divide it into thirds; the four crossing points are power points – good places for the main object. The horizon is placed on one of the third lines, not in the middle.

  1. Measure and draw the frame with a pencil and scale; keep it light so it can be neatly finished.
  2. Divide the width and height into three parts with light lines.
  3. Place the horizon on the lower third (large sky) or upper third (large land).
  4. Place the main object (hut, tree, boat) near a power point.

2. Useful compositions

Explanation of the figure:

  • Rule of thirds: the tree stands on a power point; the horizon follows a third line.
  • Leading lines: the road narrows towards a hut, leading the viewer's eye into the picture.
  • S-curve: a river winds into the distance, giving rhythm and depth.
  • Framing: overhanging branches and a trunk form a natural frame around the distant view.

3. Perspective in practice

Explanation of the figure: The horizon is at the artist's eye level and the road edges meet at the vanishing point (VP). The six trees on each side are equally spaced in real life, but on paper they become smaller and closer as they go farther. Lines drawn through all the tree bases and through all the tree tops meet at the same VP – use this to check your drawing.

  • All objects that stand on level ground and are as tall as your eye level have their tops at the horizon line.
  • Things below the horizon are seen from above; things above it, from below.
  • Overlap: a nearer object always covers part of a farther one.

4. Light and shadow

Explanation of the figure: The sun is at the left, so every object has a bright left side and a cast shadow to its right. In the animation the shadows lengthen and shorten, as they do through the day.

  • Decide the direction of light first and keep it the same for every object.
  • Shaded sides and cast shadows are darker and usually cooler.
  • Cast shadows follow the shape of the ground (they bend over a slope).

5Drawing the Elements of a Landscape

1. Sky and clouds

Explanation of the figure:

  • Cumulus – puffy with a flat, shaded base; typical of fair weather.
  • Stratus – flat grey layers; dull day.
  • Cirrus – high, thin wisps; paint with dry or lifted strokes.
  • Nimbus – dark, heavy rain cloud with rain streaks.
  • Sky is darker at the top and lighter near the horizon (graded wash).
  • Clouds get smaller and flatter near the horizon (perspective).
  • Paint clouds while the sky is still damp, or lift them out with tissue.

2. Hills and mountains

Explanation of the figure: Light comes from the top-left. The sunlit face is warm (orange-pink), the shaded face cool (blue-violet). The snow cap is white with blue-grey shadows. Below it is the tree line in dark green. A band of mist softens the valley and the far range is paler and bluer than the main mountain.

  • Start with the farthest range in the palest colour and move to the nearest in the darkest.
  • Use angular strokes for rocky mountains, rounded ones for soft hills.
  • Leave white paper (or lift colour) for snow and mist.

3. Trees

Explanation of the figure: Every tree can be reduced to a simple shape: the mango/neem as a circle, the palm as a straight trunk with fan leaves, the conifer as stacked triangles and the willow as a dome with hanging strands. The red dashed outlines show these basic shapes; the trees sway gently.

Explanation of the figure: Stage 1 – draw the skeleton (trunk tapering upward, branches splitting). Stage 2 – block in the big mass shapes. Stage 3 – add light on the top-left and dark on the underside and inner parts. Stage 4 – add leaf dabs and a cast shadow on the ground.

  • Trunks are thicker at the base and taper; branches never leave the trunk at the same height on both sides.
  • Do not paint every leaf; suggest foliage with groups of dabs.
  • Distant trees are small, pale, bluish and simple; near trees large, dark, detailed.

4. Water and reflections

Explanation of the figure: The shoreline is a mirror line. The reflection of the tree and hut is turned upside down, as tall as the object, but a little darker and softer. Ripples (moving white strokes) break the reflection into horizontal bands.

  • Water takes the colour of the sky, lighter near the horizon and darker near you.
  • Use horizontal strokes; calm water has long, smooth ones; rough water has short, broken strokes.
  • Leave small white gaps for sparkle.

5. Huts, buildings and paths

  • Build a hut from simple boxes and prisms; follow the same vanishing point as the rest of the picture.
  • Roof tops are darker; the wall in light is warm, the wall in shade is cool.
  • A path or river should be narrow in the distance and wide near you; it leads the eye to the focal point.

6. Birds, animals and people

Explanation of the figure: Birds are two curved wings and a small body; the boat is a curved hull with a mast and a triangular sail; the cow is two ovals (body and head) with four legs; the person is about seven to eight heads tall (the ticks show each head-unit). Keep figures small and simple in a landscape.

  • Figures give scale and a focal point; a bright red or white colour on a figure draws the eye.
  • Figures standing on level ground are bigger near the viewer and smaller far away.

6Techniques of Painting

1. Water colour techniques

Explanation of the figure:

  • Flat wash – an even layer of one colour; tilt the board slightly and load the brush well.
  • Graded wash – start with strong colour at the top and add water at each stroke so that it fades; used for skies.
  • Wet-on-wet – touch colours onto a damp surface so they spread; for clouds, mist, water and soft backgrounds.
  • Dry brush – a nearly dry brush dragged over rough paper leaves a broken texture; for grass, bark, sparkle on water.
  • Lifting out – blot damp paint with clean tissue or a thirsty brush to make clouds or highlights.

2. Pastel techniques

Explanation of the figure: The side of the stick covers broad areas (sky, ground); the tip draws lines and details; blending with a finger or tissue makes smooth sky and water; layering puts light colours over dark ones to build rich colour.

3. Common problems and remedies

7Step-by-Step Landscape: A River-Bank Morning

Explanation of the figure: Six stages build one painting. The dashed blue lines in stage 1 are the thirds and the red line is the horizon on the lower third; later stages add colour from the background to the foreground.

  1. Layout: draw the frame (e.g. 20 cm × 15 cm), the thirds and the horizon; decide where the sun, boat and huts will go.
  2. Pencil sketch: lightly outline the mountains, river, banks, huts, boat, trees and reeds. Keep lines thin; no shading.
  3. Sky wash: wet the sky area and paint a graded wash from blue at the top to pale yellow at the horizon; keep the sun area light; add a faint warm glow around it.
  4. Water and far hills: paint the distant mountains pale blue-violet; paint the river with the sky colours, lighter in the distance; leave small gaps for sparkle.
  5. Middle ground: paint bank greens, hut walls and roofs, the boat and sail, and the trees in medium tones.
  6. Foreground and details: darker, warmer greens for grass and reeds, rocks, birds, small figures, shadows and reflections; add final dark accents and clean the border.
StageSuggested share of working time

Plan before you paint Write down three things on a rough sheet: (1) time of day and colour scheme, (2) horizon position, (3) main object and where it goes. Doing this takes two minutes and avoids most mistakes.

9Evaluation, Mistakes and Revision

Checklist before submitting

  • Is the theme clear?
  • Are there three clear grounds and a focal point?
  • Is the horizon placed sensibly?
  • Do colours suit the time/season and look harmonious?
  • Are sky, water, trees painted with suitable techniques?
  • Is the light direction consistent?
  • Is the sheet clean with a neat border?

Common mistakes

  • Painting details first or using very dark colours early.
  • Pure black outlines or black shadows.
  • Same green and same tree size everywhere.
  • Horizon exactly at the centre; no foreground.
  • Reflections upright or lighter than the object.
  • Shadows in different directions.
  • Too much water causing patches and buckling.

10Short Answer Questions (2–3 marks)

Click a question to open its answer.

What is natural landscape drawing?

It is the painting of a scene from nature – sky, hills, water, trees, huts, people and animals – arranged into a good composition, using colour to show distance, light and mood.

In which colours can landscape painting be done in the exam?

As stated in the question – usually water colours or pastels.

What is composition in a landscape?

The arrangement of all parts of the scene so that the picture looks balanced and pleasing, with a clear focal point and sense of depth.

Name the three grounds of a landscape.

Foreground, middle ground and background.

What is the horizon line?

The line where the earth and sky seem to meet. It is at the eye level of the artist.

Why should the horizon not be placed in the exact middle?

It divides the picture into two equal parts and makes it dull. Placing it on a third gives a more interesting balance of sky and land.

What is the rule of thirds?

Dividing the picture into three equal parts both ways; the crossing points are strong places for the main object, and the horizon is placed on a third line.

What is a focal point?

The main centre of interest in the picture, e.g. a hut, boat or figure, to which the eye is drawn first.

What are leading lines?

Roads, rivers or fences that guide the viewer's eye into the picture towards the focal point.

Explain the S-curve composition.

A river or path shaped like the letter S winds from foreground to background, giving rhythm and depth to the picture.

What is perspective?

The method of showing depth and distance on a flat surface, so that near things look large and far things look small.

What is a vanishing point?

The point on the horizon at which parallel lines (road edges, rows of trees) appear to meet.

What is aerial perspective?

The effect by which distant objects look paler, bluer and less detailed than near ones.

Why are warm colours used in the foreground?

Warm colours appear to advance and cool colours to recede; so warm colours bring the foreground forward and cool colours push the distance back.

Why should we not use pure black for shadows?

Black looks dead and heavy. Mixtures such as blue + red or blue + burnt sienna give lively, coloured shadows.

Name the sequence of working in a water colour painting.

Large to small, light to dark, far to near. Sky first, then distance, middle ground, foreground and finally details.

What is a flat wash?

An even layer of one colour spread over an area without variations of tone.

What is a graded wash and where is it used?

A wash that gradually changes from dark to light. It is used for skies, which are darker at the top and lighter near the horizon.

What is wet-on-wet technique?

Painting on a damp surface so that colours blend softly; used for clouds, mist and water.

What is dry brush technique?

Using a brush with very little water so that the paint breaks over the paper's grain, giving a rough texture, e.g. grass or bark.

What is lifting out?

Removing wet paint with tissue or a damp brush to make clouds or highlights.

Name two pastel techniques.

Blending (smudging with finger or tissue) and layering (light colours over dark). Using the side of the stick for broad areas is another.

How are clouds painted?

Wet the sky and drop in cloud colours so that they blend, or lift out cloud shapes with tissue. Clouds get smaller near the horizon.

How is the light side of a hill painted differently from the shaded side?

The sunlit side uses warm, lighter colours; the shaded side uses cool, darker colours.

How should reflections in water look?

Inverted, the same height as the object, but slightly darker and softer, broken into horizontal bands by ripples.

Why are figures included in a landscape?

They give a sense of scale and life, and can act as the focal point.

How tall is a human figure in terms of heads?

About seven to eight heads, taking one head as the unit.

Name four common themes of landscape painting.

Village scene, sunrise/sunset, river bank and hill scene (also rainy day, garden, seashore).

Name the colours you mix for a dull green.

Yellow + blue gives green; adding a touch of brown or red (its complement) makes it dull.

Name some common mistakes in landscape painting.

Starting with dark colours, using black outlines, all trees equal in size, horizon in the centre, inconsistent shadows, too much water, no foreground.

11Long Answer Questions (5–6 marks)

Detailed answers – add a neat diagram wherever possible.

Define landscape painting. Explain the importance of composition and the three grounds.

Landscape painting is the painting of natural scenes with colour to show depth, light and mood.

Composition is the arrangement of the parts so that the picture is balanced and has a clear centre of interest.

  • Foreground – nearest; large, darker, warm, detailed (grass, tree, figures).
  • Middle ground – medium tones (huts, water, trees).
  • Background – farthest; pale, bluish and soft (hills, sky).

Together these three grounds create the feeling of depth. Add a focal point near a power point and place the horizon on a third line. (Draw a rectangle with the grounds labelled.)

Explain the rule of thirds and the different types of composition used in landscape painting.

Rule of thirds: divide the frame into three equal parts horizontally and vertically; place the horizon on a third line and the main object near a crossing point (power point).

  • Leading lines: road/river leads the eye to the focal point.
  • S-curve: a winding river or path creates rhythm and depth.
  • Framing: trees or branches in the foreground frame the distant view.

(Draw the four small panels.)

Explain perspective in landscape drawing. How do you show depth?

Perspective shows depth on a flat sheet.

  1. Size: near objects larger, far objects smaller.
  2. Overlapping: nearer objects cover those behind.
  3. Linear perspective: road, river, fence narrow to a vanishing point on the horizon.
  4. Aerial perspective: distant hills paler, bluer, less detailed.
  5. Warm-cool colour: warm advance, cool recede.
  6. Spacing: equally spaced trees become closer together with distance.
Describe the step-by-step method of painting a river-bank morning scene in water colours.
  1. Layout: draw the frame, thirds and horizon.
  2. Sketch: light outline of mountains, river, huts, boat, trees.
  3. Sky: graded wash from blue to pale yellow; glow near the sun.
  4. Water and far hills: pale blue-violet hills; river in sky colours with sparkle.
  5. Middle ground: banks, huts, boat, trees in medium tones.
  6. Foreground: darker warm greens, reeds, rocks.
  7. Details: birds, figures, shadows, reflections, final dark accents.

Work large to small, light to dark, far to near.

Explain the colour schemes for different times of day and weather.
  • Morning: pale yellow, pink and light blue; cool soft shadows.
  • Noon: deep blue sky, bright greens, short dark shadows.
  • Sunset: orange, red and yellow sky; violet and blue shadows; silhouettes.
  • Night: deep blue and violet; moon and stars; a warm lamp or window as contrast.
  • Rain: greys with blue; dull greens; slanting rain lines.

Choose one dominant colour and one accent; keep warm and cool contrast between light and shadow.

Describe the techniques of water colour painting with examples.
  • Flat wash – even colour (a wall).
  • Graded wash – dark to light (sky).
  • Wet-on-wet – soft blending (clouds, mist, water).
  • Wet-on-dry – sharp edges (huts, trunks).
  • Dry brush – broken texture (grass, bark).
  • Lifting out – tissue removes colour (clouds, highlights).
Explain the use of pastel colours in landscape painting.

Pastels are dry, opaque colour sticks.

  • Side stroke for large areas such as sky and ground.
  • Tip for lines and details.
  • Blending with finger or tissue for smooth skies and water.
  • Layering light over dark for richness.

Take care not to press too hard, avoid smudging and keep a clean paper under your hand.

How do you paint trees in a landscape? Explain with the stages.
  1. Skeleton: trunk tapering upwards with branches.
  2. Mass: block the foliage as big round shapes.
  3. Light and shadow: light on the top-left, dark under and inside.
  4. Texture: dabs for leaves and a ground shadow.

Distant trees are smaller, paler, bluer; near trees larger, darker, detailed. (Draw the four stage panels.)

Explain how water and reflections are painted.

Water reflects the sky: lighter near the horizon, darker near you. Reflections are the same height as the object but upside-down, a little darker and softer. Paint with horizontal strokes; ripples break the reflection into bands; leave small white gaps for sparkle. Use wet-on-wet for calm water and dry brush for sparkle.

Explain light and shadow in a landscape painting.

Fix one light direction. Each object has a lit side (lighter, warmer) and a shaded side (darker, cooler). Cast shadows fall away from the light, are the same direction for all objects, are long when the sun is low and short at noon. Do not use pure black: mix blue and red or blue and burnt sienna.

Describe the painting kit and the care of brushes and colours.

Kit: drawing sheet and board, HB pencil and eraser, palette, round and flat brushes, water jar, sponge/tissue, water colours or pastels.

Care: wash the brush after each colour; keep bristles up while drying; never leave brushes standing in water; keep the palette clean; keep paper flat; keep pastels in a box so that colours do not mix.

Explain the common mistakes in landscape painting and how to avoid them.
  • Dark colours early → use light washes first.
  • Black outlines → use thin pencil and coloured lines.
  • One green and same tree sizes → mix many greens; vary sizes.
  • Horizon in the middle → place on a third.
  • Upright reflections → invert and darken them.
  • Different shadow directions → fix the light first.
  • Too much water → use less and let areas dry.
Choose a theme "A Village in the Rain" and plan the painting.

Time/weather: rainy afternoon. Colours: grey-blue sky, dull greens, dark wet path; one bright spot (red umbrella).

  1. Frame and horizon on the lower third.
  2. Light sketch: huts in the middle ground, trees on both sides, path leading in.
  3. Wet-on-wet grey sky; lift slightly for lighter patches.
  4. Pale distant trees; darker nearer ones.
  5. Huts with wet-looking roofs; puddles reflecting the sky.
  6. Rain streaks with a white/light colour; a figure with a bright umbrella as the focal point.

14True / False

Read the statement, then click the yellow strip to check.

  1. Landscape is painted from the foreground to the background. False — Work far to near: background first, foreground last.
  2. The horizon should always be at the exact centre. False — It is better on a third line.
  3. Warm colours appear to come forward. True — Warm advance, cool recede.
  4. Black is the best colour for shadows. False — Use mixes like blue + red.
  5. A graded wash fades from dark to light. True — Used for skies.
  6. Wet-on-wet gives hard edges. False — It gives soft, blended edges.
  7. Dry brush is suitable for grass texture. True — It leaves a broken texture.
  8. Reflections in water are upright. False — They are inverted.
  9. Reflections are slightly darker and softer than the object. True — Yes, with ripples breaking them.
  10. Cast shadows can fall in different directions in one picture. False — They must follow one light direction.
  11. Distant objects are paler and bluer. True — Aerial perspective.
  12. Pastels are wet liquid colours. False — They are dry sticks.
  13. Nimbus clouds are rain clouds. True — Dark and heavy.
  14. A human figure is about 3 heads tall. False — About 7–8 heads.
  15. Leading lines guide the eye to the focal point. True — Roads and rivers do this.
  16. Too much water can buckle the paper. True — Fix the sheet on a board and use less water.
