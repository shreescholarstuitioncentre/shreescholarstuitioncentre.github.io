1. Introduction & Overview

This chapter explains how the idea of the nation-state — a state in which most citizens share a common identity, history and culture — emerged in 19th-century Europe and redrew the political map of the continent.

Frédéric Sorrieu's dream (1848): The French artist made four prints visualising a world of "democratic and social Republics". In the first print, people of Europe and America — men and women of all ages and classes — march past the Statue of Liberty, who holds the torch of Enlightenment in one hand and the Charter of the Rights of Man in the other. Shattered symbols of absolutist institutions lie on the ground.
  • Nation (Ernst Renan, 1882): a large-scale solidarity built on a shared past of sacrifices and the present wish to live together.
  • Nationalism: a sense of collective belonging to one's nation, with loyalty to it above other loyalties.
  • Before the 19th century there were no nation-states as we know them; Europe was a patchwork of kingdoms, duchies and empires.

2. The French Revolution and the Idea of the Nation

Nationalism first took clear shape in France. In 1789 France was a full-fledged territorial state under an absolute monarch. The revolution transferred sovereignty from the king to the French people.

Measures to create a sense of collective identity

  • Ideas of la patrie (the fatherland) and le citoyen (the citizen) stressed a common cause and equal rights under a constitution.
  • A new tricolour flag replaced the royal standard.
  • The Estates General was renamed the National Assembly, elected by active citizens.
  • New hymns were composed, oaths taken, and martyrs commemorated — all in the name of the nation.
  • A centralised administrative system made uniform laws for all citizens.
  • Internal customs duties were abolished; a uniform system of weights and measures was adopted.
  • Regional dialects were discouraged; French, as spoken in Paris, became the common national language.

The revolutionaries declared that it was France's mission to liberate the peoples of Europe from despotism. Students and educated people set up Jacobin clubs; French armies moved into Holland, Belgium, Switzerland and much of Italy and Germany in the 1790s.

Napoleon and the Civil Code (1804)

  • Napoleon destroyed democracy in France and returned to monarchy, but introduced revolutionary principles to make administration rational and efficient.
  • Civil Code of 1804 (Napoleonic Code): abolished privileges based on birth, established equality before the law, secured the right to property.
  • Administrative divisions simplified, feudal system abolished, peasants freed from serfdom and manorial dues; guild restrictions removed; transport and communication improved.
  • Reaction: in places such as Switzerland, Italy and Germany the French were first welcomed as liberators, but increased taxation, censorship and forced conscription into the French armies outweighed the benefits.

3. The Making of Nationalism in Europe

Europe before nation-states

  • Germany, Italy and Switzerland were divided into kingdoms, duchies and cantons with their own rulers.
  • Eastern and Central Europe were under autocratic monarchies, e.g. the Habsburg Empire (Austria-Hungary) with many languages and peoples.
Social groupFeatures
Landed aristocracyDominant class; owned estates; spoke French (diplomacy/high society); small in number.
PeasantryMajority; in the west mostly small owners/tenants; in the east and centre, serfs.
Middle classNew class in the west and centre — industrialists, businessmen, professionals; educated and liberal; spread ideas of national unity.

What did liberal nationalism stand for?

Liberalism comes from Latin liber = free.

  • Politically: government by consent, end of autocracy and clerical privileges, a constitution, representative parliament, inviolability of private property.
  • But equality before law ≠ universal suffrage. In revolutionary France only men above 25 who paid taxes (active citizens) could vote. Jacobins gave all adult men the vote; Napoleon's Code restricted it again and made women minors under fathers/husbands.
  • Throughout the 19th century women and non-propertied men demanded equal political rights.
  • Economically: freedom of markets and abolition of state restrictions on the movement of goods and capital.

Zollverein — the economic dimension

  • Napoleon left a confederation of 39 states in Germany, each with its own currency, weights and measures.
  • In 1833 a merchant going from Hamburg to Nuremberg crossed 11 customs barriers and paid about 5% duty at each.
  • In 1834, on Prussia's initiative, the Zollverein (customs union) was formed. It abolished tariff barriers and cut the number of currencies from over thirty to two.
  • Railways (with the growth of trade) further tied the economy together and strengthened national feeling.

4. A New Conservatism after 1815

  • After Napoleon's defeat (1815) European governments were driven by conservatism — preserving traditional institutions: monarchy, Church, social hierarchy, property, family.
  • Conservatives did not want a simple return to pre-1789; Napoleon's modernisation had actually made monarchies stronger.

The Congress of Vienna (1815)

  • Hosted by the Austrian chancellor Duke Metternich; delegates of Britain, Russia, Prussia and Austria.
  • Bourbon dynasty restored in France; France lost territories annexed by Napoleon.
  • States set up on France's borders to stop expansion: Netherlands (with Belgium) in the north, Genoa added to Piedmont in the south.
  • Prussia got new western territories; Austria got control of northern Italy; Russia got part of Poland, Prussia part of Saxony.
  • Napoleon's German Confederation of 39 states was left untouched.
  • Aim: restore monarchies overthrown by Napoleon and create a new conservative order.
  • Conservative regimes were autocratic and intolerant of criticism; censorship laws controlled newspapers, books, plays and songs.

The revolutionaries

  • Repression pushed liberal-nationalists underground — secret societies trained revolutionaries and spread ideas.
  • Giuseppe Mazzini (b. Genoa, 1807): member of the Carbonari; exiled in 1831 after a revolt in Liguria; founded Young Italy (Marseilles) and Young Europe (Berne).
  • He believed nations are God's natural units of mankind; Italy must be a unified republic, not a patchwork of states — inspiring movements in Poland, Germany, France and Switzerland.
  • Metternich called him "the most dangerous enemy of our social order."

5. The Age of Revolutions: 1830–1848

  • July Revolution, 1830 (France): Bourbon kings, restored after 1815, were overthrown by liberal revolutionaries who installed a constitutional monarchy under Louis Philippe. Metternich: "When France sneezes, the rest of Europe catches cold."
  • Belgium broke away from the United Kingdom of the Netherlands.
  • Greek War of Independence (from 1821): Greeks fought the Ottoman Empire; helped by Greeks in exile and by West Europeans who admired ancient Greek culture. Poets and artists (e.g. Lord Byron, who raised funds and fought) hailed Greece as the cradle of European civilisation.
  • The Treaty of Constantinople (1832) recognised Greece as an independent nation.

6. Romanticism and National Feeling

  • Romanticism was a cultural movement that created a particular form of nationalism. Romantic artists criticised the glorification of reason and science and stressed emotion, intuition and mystical feelings.
  • Aim: build a shared collective heritage and common cultural past as the basis of a nation.
  • Johann Gottfried Herder (German philosopher): true German culture lay among the common people — das volk; folk songs, poetry and dances had to be collected.
  • Emphasis on vernacular language and folklore recovered the national spirit and carried the message to a largely illiterate audience.
Poland case study: Partitioned by Russia, Prussia and Austria in the late 18th century. Karol Kurpiński turned folk dances (polonaise, mazurka) into nationalist symbols through operas and music. After the failed 1831 revolt against Russia, Polish clergy used the Polish language as a weapon of resistance — in Church gatherings and religious teaching; many priests were jailed or sent to Siberia for refusing to preach in Russian.

7. Hunger, Hardship and Popular Revolt; the 1848 Revolution

Economic hardship (1830s)

  • Population rose; more job-seekers than jobs; migration to overcrowded city slums.
  • Small producers could not compete with cheap machine-made English imports.
  • Peasants in aristocratic regions carried feudal dues; bad harvests and rising food prices caused widespread pauperism.
  • 1845 – Silesian weavers' revolt: weavers demanded higher wages from contractors; troops were called and 11 weavers were shot.
  • 1848 – Paris: food shortages and unemployment sent people to the streets; barricades; Louis Philippe fled; a Republic was proclaimed with suffrage for all adult men above 21 and the right to work (national workshops).

1848: the revolution of the liberals

  • In Germany, Italy, Poland and the Austro-Hungarian Empire, the educated middle class joined constitutionalism with national unification: constitution, freedom of press, freedom of association.
  • Frankfurt Parliament: on 18 May 1848, 831 elected representatives marched in procession to the Church of St Paul. They drafted a constitution for a German nation headed by a monarch under a parliament.
  • The offer of the crown to Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia was rejected; he joined other monarchs against the assembly. Middle-class dominance cost it worker/artisan support; troops dissolved it.
  • Women had formed political associations, founded newspapers and joined demonstrations, yet were denied the vote; in St Paul's Church they were only observers in the gallery.
  • Aftermath: conservatives suppressed the liberals but could not restore the old order. Monarchs of central and eastern Europe began granting concessions: serfdom abolished in the Habsburg lands and Russia; Hungarians got more autonomy in 1867.

8. The Making of Germany

  • After 1848 nationalism moved away from democracy and revolution; conservatives used it to promote state power.
  • The liberal Frankfurt initiative was crushed by the combined forces of monarchy, military and the large Prussian landowners (Junkers).
  • Prussia took the leadership. Otto von Bismarck was the architect, using the Prussian army and bureaucracy.
  • Three wars in seven years — against Denmark (1864), Austria (1866) and France (1870–71) — ended in Prussian victory.
  • In January 1871, Prussian king William I was proclaimed German Emperor at Versailles.
  • The new state emphasised modernising currency, banking, legal and judicial systems; Prussian measures became a model for the rest of Germany.

9. The Making of Italy

  • Mid-19th century Italy was divided into seven states; only Sardinia-Piedmont was ruled by an Italian royal house. The north was under the Austrian Habsburgs, the centre under the Pope, the south under Bourbon kings. Italian had no single common form.
  • Mazzini proposed a unitary republic (Young Italy), but the uprisings of 1831 and 1848 failed.
  • Leadership passed to Sardinia-Piedmont under King Victor Emmanuel II. Chief minister Cavour — neither a revolutionary nor a democrat — formed a diplomatic alliance with France and defeated Austria in 1859.
  • Armed volunteers under Giuseppe Garibaldi marched into the South and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies in 1860, winning peasant support and driving out the Spanish (Bourbon) rulers.
  • In 1861 Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed King of united Italy. Venetia joined in 1866 and Rome in 1870.

10. The Strange Case of Britain

  • British nationalism was not a sudden upheaval but the result of a long-drawn-out process. Before the 18th century there was no 'British nation' — people identified as English, Welsh, Scot or Irish.
  • England grew in wealth and power; its Parliament seized power from the monarchy in 1688 and became the instrument for a nation-state centred on England.
  • Act of Union (1707) created the United Kingdom of Great Britain; Scottish culture and institutions were suppressed — Highlanders were forbidden to speak Gaelic or wear national dress, and many were driven out.
  • Ireland: deeply divided between Catholics and Protestants. After the failed revolt of Wolfe Tone and the United Irishmen (1798), Ireland was forcibly incorporated into the UK in 1801.
  • The new British nation was promoted through symbols: the Union Jack, the national anthem, the English language.

11. Visualising the Nation

  • Artists personified the nation as a female figure — an allegory, not a real woman, giving the abstract idea a concrete form.
  • In France she was called Marianne, a popular Christian name, stressing a people's nation. Traits of Liberty and the Republic: red cap, tricolour, cockade. Statues stood in public squares; her image appeared on coins and stamps.
  • Germania became the allegory of the German nation; she wears a crown of oak leaves (the German oak stands for heroism).

12. Nationalism and Imperialism

  • By the last quarter of the 19th century nationalism lost its liberal-democratic idealism and became a narrow creed; nationalist groups grew intolerant of each other and ready for war.
  • Major powers manipulated the nationalist aspirations of subject peoples to further their own imperialist aims.
  • The Balkans (modern Romania, Bulgaria, Albania, Greece, Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Slovenia, Serbia, Montenegro) — inhabited by Slavs, largely under the Ottoman Empire. Romantic nationalism plus Ottoman disintegration made the region explosive.
  • Russia, Germany, England and Austria-Hungary each wanted to extend control and counter the others → series of wars → First World War.
  • Colonised peoples later developed their own anti-imperial nationalist movements — nationalism became a major force in the world in the 20th century.

15. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. Who was Giuseppe Mazzini? What did he do?
An Italian revolutionary (b. 1807, Genoa), a Carbonari member, exiled in 1831. He founded Young Italy and Young Europe, wanted a unified Italian republic and inspired nationalist movements across Europe; Metternich called him "the most dangerous enemy of our social order".

Q2. How did the French Revolution create a sense of collective identity?
Through la patrie and le citoyen, the tricolour, the National Assembly, new hymns and oaths, uniform laws, abolition of internal customs, uniform weights and measures, and French as the common language.

Q3. What was the Zollverein? Why was it important?
A customs union formed in 1834 by Prussia. It abolished tariff barriers, reduced currencies from 30+ to 2, and (with railways) created economic unity that strengthened nationalism.

Q4. What were the main provisions of the Congress of Vienna (1815)?
Bourbons restored in France; buffer states (Netherlands, Genoa-Piedmont); Prussia and Austria gained territory; German Confederation of 39 states kept; conservative order established.

Q5. Describe the role of women in nationalist struggles.
They formed associations, founded newspapers and joined demonstrations, but were denied suffrage; at the Frankfurt Parliament they were only allowed as observers in the gallery.

Q6. How was Germany unified?
Under Prussia and Bismarck through three wars (Denmark 1864, Austria 1866, France 1870–71); William I was proclaimed Emperor at Versailles in January 1871.

Q7. Describe Cavour's, Garibaldi's and Victor Emmanuel II's role in Italy.
Cavour: diplomat, alliance with France, beat Austria 1859. Garibaldi: led volunteers into the South in 1860. Victor Emmanuel II: Sardinia-Piedmont's king, proclaimed King of united Italy 1861.

Q8. Why did the Balkans become explosive?
Slav nationalism + decline of Ottoman rule + great-power rivalry (Russia, Germany, England, Austria-Hungary) → wars and finally the First World War.

Q9. How did nationalism develop in Britain differently?
Gradually, not by revolution: Parliament (1688), Act of Union (1707), suppression of Scottish culture, Ireland's incorporation (1801) and use of national symbols.

Q10. Explain Marianne and Germania.
Female allegories of the French and German nations — Marianne with red cap and tricolour; Germania with an oak-leaf crown and sword.
