✎ Manufacturing Industries
Class 10 SST · Geography · Ch 11 (NCERT · UP Board)

1. Introduction

Manufacturing means turning raw materials into more valuable finished goods in large quantity (e.g. cotton → cloth, iron ore → steel). It is a secondary activity.

Importance:
  • Backbone of the economy – modernises agriculture and creates jobs in secondary & tertiary sectors.
  • Reduces dependence on agriculture, removes unemployment and poverty.
  • Exports of manufactured goods bring foreign exchange; trade expands.
  • Agriculture and industry go hand in hand: agro-industries raise farm productivity (fertilisers, pipes, machines).
  • The NMCC aims to raise manufacturing's share in GDP to 25%; it is about 17% now.

3. Agro-based Industries

(a) Cotton Textile

  • First successful mill: Mumbai, 1854. Mumbai = "Cottonopolis". Early growth in Maharashtra & Gujarat (black soil, humid climate, port, markets).
  • Spinning is concentrated in Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu; weaving is decentralised (handloom, powerloom, mills) – gives most jobs.
  • India: major exporter of cotton yarn to Japan; also to USA, UK, Russia, France, Africa.
  • Problems: erratic power, outdated machinery, low labour productivity, stiff competition from synthetic fibres.

(b) Jute

  • India = largest producer, second largest exporter (after Bangladesh). Mostly in Hugli basin; first mill at Rishra (1859).
  • Why Hugli? near jute-growing belt, cheap water transport, railways & roads, plentiful water for processing, cheap labour from Bihar, UP, Odisha, and Kolkata as port.
  • Problems: competition from synthetics and from Bangladesh, Brazil, Philippines, Egypt, Thailand. National Jute Policy 2005 improves quality & farmers' income.

(c) Sugar

  • India: 2nd largest producer; seasonal industry; mills in UP, Bihar (north) and Maharashtra, Karnataka, TN, AP, Gujarat (south/west).
  • Shifting south and west: cane has higher sucrose, cooler climate gives longer crushing season, and co-operatives are strong.

4. Mineral-based Industries

(a) Iron & Steel – the "basic" industry

Called basic because all other industries (machines, tools, bridges) depend on it. Raw materials are heavy, so plants are near iron-ore + coal fields. Needs: iron ore, coking coal, limestone (flux), manganese. TISCO, Jamshedpur (1907) was the first. Major plants: Bhilai, Durgapur, Rourkela, Bokaro, Vijayanagar, Salem. India is the largest producer of sponge iron. SAIL markets steel from public plants.

🔥 Diagram: Iron & Steel Production (animated)

Explanation: Ore, coke and limestone are fed from the top of the blast furnace. Coke burns and gives heat, limestone combines with impurities to form slag, and molten iron (pig iron) flows out. In the steel furnace, carbon is reduced and manganese/alloys are added to make steel, which is rolled into sheets, rails and rods.

(b) Aluminium Smelting

2nd most important metallurgical industry. Light, corrosion-resistant, good conductor, malleable and becomes strong when mixed with other metals. Uses: aircraft, utensils, wires. Raw materials: bauxite and electricity (so plants near power). Located in Odisha, WB, Kerala, UP, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra, TN.

(c) Chemical & Fertiliser

Chemicals contribute ~3% GDP; both organic (petrochemicals, plastics, dyes) and inorganic (acids, alkalis, soda ash). Fertilisers: nitrogenous (urea), phosphatic, ammonia; rose after the Green Revolution. Gujarat, TN, UP, Punjab and Kerala produce about half.

(d) Cement

Needs bulky limestone, silica, alumina, gypsum. First plant: Chennai (1904). Uses: construction. Located in Gujarat and near limestone belts and railways.

(e) Automobile

Trucks, buses, cars, motorcycles, scooters, three-wheelers. Centres: Delhi, Gurugram, Mumbai, Pune, Chennai, Kolkata, Lucknow, Indore, Hyderabad, Jamshedpur, Bengaluru. Liberalisation brought foreign investment.

(f) IT & Electronics

Bengaluru = electronic capital. Other hubs: Mumbai, Delhi, Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Kolkata, Lucknow, Coimbatore. About 30 software technology parks give jobs and foreign exchange (BPO).

5. Industrial Pollution & Control

  • Air: SO₂, CO, smoke → acid rain, smog, health issues (Bhopal gas tragedy, 1984).
  • Water: dyes, chemicals, fly ash, phosphogypsum dumped in rivers; thermal pollution from hot water of power plants.
  • Noise: machines, generators, drills cause stress and hearing loss.
Control: recycle water (2 or 3 uses), rain-water harvesting, treat hot water & effluents before release, install electrostatic precipitators/scrubbers, use CNG, silencers and ear-plugs, trees as noise buffer.

B. Short Answer

1. Why is manufacturing called the backbone of economic development?

It modernises agriculture, creates jobs, reduces poverty, earns foreign exchange through exports and adds value to raw material, which strengthens the economy.

2. Why is iron & steel a basic industry?

Machines, tools, vehicles, bridges and buildings all use steel, so almost every other industry depends on it.

3. Why is the sugar industry shifting to the south and west?

Cane there has higher sucrose, the cooler climate lengthens the crushing season, and co-operative societies manage mills well.

4. Differentiate public and private sector industries.

Public sector is owned and run by the government (SAIL, BHEL); private sector is owned by individuals or companies (TISCO, Bajaj).

5. Name any two measures to control water pollution.

(i) Treat hot water and effluents before releasing into rivers. (ii) Recycle and reuse water in factories.

C. Long Answer

1. Explain the problems and importance of the cotton textile industry.

Importance: largest organised industry; gives jobs to farmers, ginners, spinners and weavers; earns foreign exchange through yarn exports. Problems: erratic power supply, old machinery, low labour productivity, and competition from synthetic fibres; also needs better long-staple cotton.

2. Why are jute mills concentrated in the Hugli basin? Mention challenges.

Jute fields nearby, cheap water transport, railways, abundant water for processing, cheap labour from Bihar/UP/Odisha and Kolkata port. Challenges: synthetic substitutes, competition from Bangladesh and others; the National Jute Policy 2005 helps quality and farmers' income.

3. How do industries pollute the environment? How can it be reduced?

Industries release gases (air), effluents and fly ash (water, soil), hot water (thermal) and loud sounds (noise). Reduce by scrubbers/precipitators, effluent treatment, recycling water, CNG, silencers and green belts.
