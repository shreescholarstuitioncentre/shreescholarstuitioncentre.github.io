The Age of Industrialisation

Class 10 · Social Science (History) · Chapter 4 · Detailed Notes

1. Introduction & Overview

Today the world is industrial: goods are mass-produced in factories. Popular images of industrialisation focus on technological inventions, machines and factories — but this chapter shows that the story is more complicated. Hand labour, small workshops and traditional crafts survived alongside factories, in Britain and in colonial India.

  • Industrialisation did not mean only factory growth; it involved changes in trade, labour, markets, consumption and politics.
  • The Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the late 18th century; in India it developed under colonial rule, with its own limits.

2. Before the Industrial Revolution — Proto-industrialisation

  • Even before factories, in the 17th and 18th centuries, merchants from towns in Europe began moving to the countryside, supplying money to peasants and artisans and persuading them to produce for an international market. This phase is called proto-industrialisation.
  • Why the countryside? World trade was expanding and colonies were being acquired, so demand for goods rose. But in towns, guilds (associations of producers that trained craftspeople, controlled production, regulated competition and prices, and restricted entry) were powerful, and rulers had given them monopoly rights. So merchants could not expand production in towns.
  • At the same time, in the countryside, open fields were disappearing and commons were being enclosed; poor peasants and cottagers had little land and looked for additional income. They welcomed merchants' offers.
  • Under the putting-out system a clothier bought wool from a stapler, carried it to spinners, then the yarn went to weavers, fullers and finally to dyers/finishers in London before export. Peasant families worked at home, combining farming with cloth production.
  • Proto-industrial production was part of a network of commercial exchanges controlled by merchants — but the work was still done in homes, not in factories.

3. The Coming Up of the Factory

  • The first factories in England came up by the 1730s, but their number multiplied only in the late 18th century.
  • The cotton mill became the symbol of the new era. A series of inventions in the 18th century improved carding, twisting and spinning, and rolling — increasing output per worker and allowing stronger threads.
  • Richard Arkwright created the cotton mill; costly new machines were purchased, set up and maintained in one place under the owner's supervision, enabling control over quality, labour and production — impossible in the countryside.
  • James Hargreaves' spinning jenny (1764) spun many threads at once. James Watt improved Newcomen's steam engine (patent 1781), later powering mills and railways.
  • Cotton boom: raw cotton imports rose from 2.5 million pounds (1760) to 22 million pounds (1787).

4. The Pace of Industrial Change

  1. Cotton and metals led. Cotton was the leading sector till the 1840s; then iron and steel took over, with railways (England from the 1840s, colonies from the 1860s). In 1873 Britain's iron and steel exports (~£77 million) were about double its cotton exports.
  2. Traditional industries survived. Even at the end of the 19th century less than 20% of the workforce was in technologically advanced industry. Much textile output came from domestic units, not factories.
  3. Steam did not lead everything. Pottery, glass-work, tanning, furniture and tool-making remained hand-based.
  4. Technology spread slowly. Machines were expensive; repairs costly; they were often not as effective as inventors claimed. Industrialists were cautious.

Hand labour and steam power

  • In Victorian Britain there was no shortage of human labour: poor peasants and vagrants flowed into cities for jobs. Industrialists therefore had no wage-cost pressure to adopt costly machines.
  • In industries with seasonal demand (gas works, breweries, bookbinding, printing, Christmas-related work) hand labour was cheaper.
  • The aristocracy and bourgeoisie preferred hand-made goods — a mark of refinement, class and fine finish — while machines made uniform goods for the mass market.

The life of workers

  • Plentiful labour hurt workers: jobs were scarce, seasonal, and dependent on contacts; migrants waited at bridges or night refuges ('Poor Law' workhouses). In depression years unemployment in some areas reached 35–75%.
  • Wages rose slightly, but price rises (e.g. during the Napoleonic wars) reduced real incomes.
  • Workers resented new machines: women in the wool industry attacked the spinning jenny that threatened their jobs.

5. Industrialisation in the Colonies: Indian Textiles

The age of Indian textiles

  • Before machine industries, Indian silk and cotton goods dominated the international market in fine textiles; coarser cotton came from other countries.
  • Overland, Armenian and Persian merchants carried goods from Punjab to Afghanistan, eastern Persia and Central Asia. By sea, Surat (Gujarat) connected India to the Gulf of Hormuz (West Asia), while Masulipatam (Coromandel) and Hooghly (Bengal) traded with South-East Asia.
  • A network of Indian supply merchants, bankers, weavers financed production and carried goods.
  • From the 1750s, this network broke down: European companies gained power; the old ports Surat and Hooghly declined and Bombay and Calcutta grew as colonial ports.

What happened to the weavers?

  • After establishing political power, the East India Company asserted a monopoly right to trade, wanting to eliminate competition, control costs and ensure regular supplies of cotton and silk goods.
  • Step 1: It eliminated existing traders and brokers and developed direct control over weavers, appointing a paid servant, the gomastha, to supervise weavers, collect supplies and examine cloth quality.
  • Step 2: It prevented Company weavers from dealing with other buyers through the advance (loan) system: weavers who took loans had to hand over cloth only to gomasthas — they lost the power to bargain.
  • Gomasthas were outsiders, with no social link to villagers; they acted arrogantly, using sepoys and peons, and punished weavers for delays (beating, flogging). Weavers deserted villages and migrated, or revolted.

Manchester comes to India

  • As British cotton industries developed, industrialists pressed the government to restrict textile imports and open Indian markets. Indian cotton goods' share in exports fell from about 33% (1811–12) to about 3% (1850–51); raw cotton exports rose.
  • By the 1860s, when the American Civil War cut off US cotton, raw cotton exports from India surged; prices soared and Indian weavers were starved of supplies and forced to buy at high prices.
  • By the end of the 19th century, Indian factories and British imports flooded the market with cheap machine cloth, creating a new crisis for weavers.

6. Factories Come Up in India

  • Bombay: first cotton mill in 1854; by 1862, four mills. Bengal: first jute mill in 1855 (second 1862), mostly European-owned. Kanpur: Elgin Mill in the 1860s. Ahmedabad: first cotton mill 1861. Madras: first spinning and weaving mill by 1874.

Early entrepreneurs

  • Dwarkanath Tagore made his fortune in the China trade and set up six joint-stock companies in the 1830s–40s.
  • Dinshaw Petit and Jamsetjee Nusserwanjee Tata (Parsis) built huge industrial empires; Seth Hukumchand (Marwari) set up the first Indian-owned jute mill in Calcutta (1917); G.D. Birla also built a business empire. Capital often came from the China trade; Madras merchants traded with Burma, the Middle East and East Africa.
  • Colonial control: after the Europeans took control of trade, Indian merchants were barred from trading manufactured goods with Europe and confined to exporting raw materials and food grains (raw cotton, opium, wheat, indigo).
  • European Managing Agencies (Bird Heilgers & Co., Andrew Yule, Jardine Skinner & Co.) mobilised capital, set up joint-stock companies and ran them; Indian business had a smaller share.

Where did workers come from?

  • Most mill workers came from nearby districts — peasants and artisans without work. In Bombay mills, many came from Ratnagiri; in Kanpur from villages around. Many kept their ties with villages and returned at harvest and festivals.
  • The jobber (mistri) was an old, trusted worker employed to recruit workers from his village; he ensured jobs, helped migrants settle and gave loans — but gradually demanded money and gifts and controlled workers' lives.
  • Number of factory workers: 5.84 lakh (1901) → 24.36 lakh (1946).

7. Peculiarities of Industrial Growth

  • European Managing Agencies dominated early industry, investing in products for export (tea, coffee, jute, indigo) rather than Indian markets.
  • Small-scale industry predominated: in 1911, 67% of workers in large industry were in Bengal and Bombay; elsewhere small units dominated. Only about 5% of the industrial workforce was in registered factories (early 20th century); the rest worked in small workshops and household units.
  • Handlooms did not die: handloom cloth output nearly tripled between 1900 and 1940. Weavers adopted the flying shuttle (faster, less labour): by 1941 over 35% of handlooms had it. Mills made coarse cloth; weavers specialised in fine/speciality cloth (e.g. Banarasi, Baluchari saris) that mills could not copy.
  • First World War (1914–18): Manchester was busy with war production, and its exports to India fell. Indian mills got a huge home market, and also supplied war needs — jute bags, army cloth, tents, leather boots, saddles. New factories were set up, old ones ran multiple shifts, and workers increased. After the war, Manchester could never recapture its old position in India.
  • In the 20th century, the swadeshi movement and nationalist boycotts of foreign cloth further helped Indian mills.

8. Market for Goods: Advertising and Consumers

  • New products need new consumers: advertisements make products desirable and create a need. From the late 19th century, advertisements helped expand the market for manufactured goods.
  • Labels on Manchester cloth showed the place of manufacture ('Made in Manchester') — a mark of quality; labels also carried brand names/trade marks.
  • Images of Indian gods and goddesses and, later, Indian leaders and rulers on labels and calendars made imported goods familiar and respectable to Indian buyers. Calendars were hung in tea shops, offices and homes — even by those who could not read.
  • When Indian manufacturers advertised, the nationalist message was clear: if you care for the nation, buy products that Indians produce. Advertisements thus became vehicles of nationalism.

11. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. What was proto-industrialisation? Why did merchants move to the countryside?
It was the early phase of production for world markets by rural households under town merchants. Town guilds were strong and restricted production, while rural poor needed extra income, so merchants turned to villages.

Q2. Why did some industrialists in Britain prefer hand labour to machines?
Labour was cheap and plentiful; machines were costly; seasonal demand favoured hand labour; and elites preferred hand-made goods.

Q3. How did the East India Company control weavers?
It gave monopoly status to itself, removed traders, appointed gomasthas, and used an advance system so weavers could sell only to it.

Q4. Why did Indian weavers face a crisis in the 19th century?
Competition from cheap Manchester imports, shortage and high price of raw cotton during the US Civil War, and later Indian factory cloth.

Q5. Who set up the first cotton and jute mills in India?
First cotton mill: Bombay 1854; first jute mill: Bengal 1855; Ahmedabad 1861; Kanpur's Elgin Mill (1860s); Madras 1874. Early entrepreneurs: Dwarkanath Tagore, Tata, Petit, Hukumchand, Birla.

Q6. What was the role of jobbers?
They recruited workers from their villages, got them jobs, helped them settle and gave loans — but demanded money and gifts and controlled workers.

Q7. How did the First World War help Indian industries?
British imports fell, and Indian mills met war demand (jute bags, uniforms, tents, boots); new factories opened, shifts increased, production rose.

Q8. Why did handloom industries survive?
Weavers adopted the fly shuttle and made special/fine cloth for niche markets that mills could not copy; mills mostly made coarse cloth.

Q9. How did advertisements shape consumption?
Labels, brand names, calendars and pictures of gods/leaders made goods familiar; Indian advertisers added nationalist messages.

Q10. Why is it wrong to see industrialisation only as factory growth?
Traditional industries, hand labour and small workshops continued; in 19th-century Britain under 20% of workers were in advanced industries.

12. Quick Revision

  • Proto-industry: merchants + village households (guilds blocked towns); putting-out system.
  • Factories: 1730s; cotton mill (Arkwright), jenny (1764), Watt engine (1781). Cotton → iron & steel (1840s).
  • Britain: change was slow; <20% in advanced industry; cheap labour, hand goods valued.
  • India: pre-1750s textile trade (Surat, Masulipatam, Hooghly) → Company control (gomastha, advances) → Manchester imports → Indian mills (1854 Bombay; 1855 jute).
  • Workers: jobbers; 5.84 lakh (1901) → 24.36 lakh (1946).
  • WWI boom; handlooms survive (fly shuttle); small units dominate. Advertising: labels, calendars, nationalist ads.
