Forest and Wildlife Resources

Class 10 · Social Science (Geography) · Chapter 7 · Detailed Notes

1. Introduction: Biodiversity

India is one of the world's richest countries in terms of its biological diversity (biodiversity). It has nearly 8 per cent of the total number of species in the world (estimated at 1.6 million). This rich heritage is, however, under severe pressure — many species are declining or vanishing.

  • Flora = plants of a particular region; Fauna = animals of a particular region.
  • India has about 47,000 plant species (including about 15,000 flowering species) and about 81,000 faunal species.
  • Over 10% of India's wild flora and 20% of its mammals are on the threatened list.
  • Many once-common species such as the cheetah and the pink-headed duck have disappeared; others (e.g. Indian rhino, Asiatic buffalo, Indian wild ass) are in danger.

Why conserve forests and wildlife?

  • Ecological: they preserve the ecological diversity and our life-support systems — water, air and soil.
  • Genetic: they preserve the genetic diversity of plants and animals for better growth of species and breeding.
  • Economic: agriculture and fisheries still continue to depend on traditional crop varieties, livestock breeds and wild species.
Remember: Ecological crisis = the loss of biodiversity. When forests are cleared, whole food chains, water cycles and soil protection are damaged together.

2. Flora and Fauna: Categories of Species

The International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources (IUCN) classifies plants and animals into categories according to their extent of existence (the "Red List").

3. Forest Cover and Types of Forests

India has a recorded forest area that is larger than the actual forest cover. The recorded area includes land legally classed as forest (even if it has no tree cover). The Forest Survey of India assesses the actual cover. Dense forest, open forest, mangroves and scrub are part of the assessment — and the actual cover is a much smaller share of the country than the legal record might suggest.

In India, much of the forest and wildlife resources are either owned or managed by the government through the Forest Department or other government departments. They are classified under the following categories:

  • Reserved Forests: more than half of the total forest land has been declared reserved forest. Reserved forests are regarded as the most valuable as far as the conservation of forest and wildlife resources is concerned.
  • Protected Forests: almost one-third of the total forest area is protected forest. These are protected from any further depletion.
  • Unclassed Forests: other forests and wastelands belonging to both government and private individuals and communities.
  • Reserved and protected forests are also referred to as permanent forest estates, maintained for the purpose of producing timber and other forest produce and for protective reasons.
  • Madhya Pradesh has the largest area under permanent forests, about 75 per cent of its total forest area.
  • In several states (such as Jammu & Kashmir, Andhra Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal and Maharashtra) a large share of the forest is reserved. In Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Odisha and Rajasthan much of the forest is protected.
  • All the north-eastern states and parts of Gujarat have a very high percentage of their forests as unclassed, managed by local communities.

4. Depletion of Forests and Wildlife Resources

Between 1951 and 1980, over 26,200 sq km of forest area was converted into agricultural land all over India. Substantial parts of tribal belts, especially in the north-eastern and central India, have been deforested or degraded by shifting cultivation (slash and burn).

Causes of depletion

  • Expansion of agriculture: forests were cleared for farmland after Independence.
  • Colonial period: the British used forests for railway sleepers, shipbuilding and plantations (tea, rubber, coffee), leading to large-scale clearance.
  • Shifting cultivation: slash-and-burn in north-east, Odisha, Madhya Pradesh.
  • Large-scale development projects: since 1951 over 5,000 sq km of forest was cleared for river valley projects. Example: the Narmada Sagar dam in Madhya Pradesh would have submerged 40,000 ha of forest.
  • Mining: it is a major threat; e.g. dolomite mining at Buxa Tiger Reserve (West Bengal) has disturbed the habitat of many species including the Indian elephant.
  • Grazing and fuelwood collection: excessive pressure on forests.
  • Poaching, hunting and habitat destruction: illegal trade in wildlife products.
  • Forest fires, pollution and overexploitation also add to the depletion.

Unequal consumption and its effects on the poor

The inequality of access, control and management of forest resources is an important cause. Rich industrialised nations consume much more of the world's resources than they regenerate. The poor, who depend on forests for fuel, fodder, food and medicines, suffer most. Women bear the greatest burden — they have to walk longer distances to collect fuelwood, fodder and water, which affects their health and household work.

Case study: The Himalayan Yew in trouble

The Himalayan Yew (Taxus wallichiana) is a medicinal plant found in the temperate Himalayan regions of Arunachal Pradesh and Himachal Pradesh. A chemical called taxol, extracted from the bark, needles, twigs and roots of the tree, is used to treat some cancers; it is the biggest-selling anti-cancer drug in the world. The species has been over-exploited, and in recent years thousands of yew trees have dried up in various parts of Arunachal Pradesh and Himachal Pradesh.

Lesson: a plant valuable for medicine can still be pushed to the edge when harvest is unsustainable — conservation must go together with use.

5. Conservation of Forest and Wildlife

Conservation preserves the ecological diversity and our life-support systems; it also preserves genetic diversity of species. Conservation strategies gained momentum in the 1960s and 1970s, when environmentalists demanded a national wildlife protection programme.

The Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972

  • The Act provided an all-India list of protected species.
  • It banned hunting, gave legal protection to habitats and restricted trade in wildlife.
  • Many national parks and wildlife sanctuaries were established; the central government announced several projects for protecting specific animals that were gravely threatened — including the tiger, the one-horned rhinoceros, the Kashmir stag (hangul), three types of crocodiles (freshwater crocodile, saltwater crocodile and the Gharial), the Asiatic lion and others.
  • In 1980 and 1986, several species of butterflies, moths, beetles and one dragonfly were added to the list of protected species. In 1991, for the first time, six species of plants were also added.

Project Tiger (1973)

The tiger is one of the key wildlife species in the faunal web. In 1900 there were about 55,000 tigers; by 1973 the count had fallen to just 1,827.

  • Major threats: poaching for trade (tiger bones in traditional medicines), shrinking habitat, depletion of prey base species, growing human population.
  • Poaching also affects tigers in other countries; the trade in tiger skins and bones is global.
  • Project Tiger was launched in 1973 — one of the well-publicised wildlife campaigns in the world. Tiger reserves have been set up across the country, such as Corbett National Park (Uttarakhand), Sunderbans National Park (West Bengal), Bandhavgarh (Madhya Pradesh), Sariska Wildlife Sanctuary (Rajasthan), Manas Tiger Reserve (Assam), and Periyar Tiger Reserve (Kerala).
  • Extra context: the 2018 tiger census estimated about 2,967 tigers, and the 2022 estimate was higher still (about 3,600+) — evidence that targeted protection can help.
Remember: Act = 1972; Project Tiger = 1973. Tiger numbers: 55,000 (1900) → 1,827 (1973).

6. Community and Conservation

In India, forests are home to many communities, and many have been protecting forests for centuries. Conservation strategies are not new in our country; what is new is the realisation that local communities must be involved.

  • Sariska Tiger Reserve (Rajasthan): villagers have fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act.
  • Villages in Alwar district: five villages have declared 1,200 hectares of forest as the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri', made their own rules and regulations, and do not allow hunting.
  • Chipko Movement in the Himalayas: it resisted deforestation across the country and showed that community afforestation with indigenous species can be successful.
  • Beej Bachao Andolan (Tehri) and Navdanya: farmers showed that diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals is possible and economically viable.
  • Sacred groves: forest patches left untouched by communities and protected by religious sentiments — e.g. the Khasi and Jaintia Hills (Meghalaya), Aravalli (Rajasthan), Western Ghats (Karnataka and Maharashtra), Sarguja (Chhattisgarh), and Chotanagpur region. Some societies revere certain trees such as mahua, peepal, tulsi and mango.

Joint Forest Management (JFM)

The programme of Joint Forest Management furnishes a good example of involving local communities in the management and restoration of degraded forests. It has been in formal practice since 1988, when the state of Odisha passed the first resolution for JFM.

  • Local village communities (through village institutions) join the forest department to protect and manage degraded forest land.
  • In return, villagers get intermediary benefits such as non-timber forest produce, and a share in the timber harvested by 'successful protection'.
  • Arabari (West Bengal), 1972: the forest department involved villagers in the protection of degraded sal forest in the Arabari Forest Range of Midnapore district; for their contribution, villagers were given employment and 25 per cent of the final harvest. The once-degraded forest recovered.

9. Important Questions and Answers

Q1. Why is biodiversity important for human lives?

Biodiversity maintains ecological balance, preserves air, water and soil, supports agriculture and fisheries through genetic variety, and provides food, medicine, fuel and fodder. Loss of species disturbs food chains and endangers human survival.

Q2. Distinguish between endangered, vulnerable and rare species.

Endangered — in danger of extinction now. Vulnerable — population declining and likely to become endangered. Rare — small population that may become vulnerable or endangered.

Q3. Distinguish between reserved, protected and unclassed forests.

Reserved forests (more than half) have the highest conservation value; protected forests (about one-third) are protected from further depletion; unclassed forests are other forests and wastelands owned by government, individuals and communities.

Q4. Explain the causes of depletion of forests and wildlife.

Agricultural expansion, colonial exploitation, shifting cultivation, river-valley projects, mining, grazing, fuelwood collection, poaching, forest fires and unequal consumption.

Q5. How have human activities affected the depletion of flora and fauna?

Clearing for farms, dams and mines destroys habitat; over-harvesting (e.g. Himalayan Yew) and poaching push species toward extinction; pollution and fires add further damage.

Q6. Why did the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972 come into being?

Many species were disappearing; the Act gave an all-India list of protected species, banned hunting, protected habitats and restricted trade.

Q7. What was Project Tiger? Why is it important?

Launched in 1973 to protect tigers whose numbers had fallen to 1,827; it created tiger reserves and is one of the best-known conservation campaigns in the world.

Q8. How are local communities involved in conservation? Give examples.

Chipko, Beej Bachao Andolan, Navdanya, Sariska villagers, Alwar villages' Sonchuri, sacred groves and Joint Forest Management (Arabari, Odisha).

Q9. Explain Joint Forest Management.

Villagers join the forest department to protect degraded forests and in return get benefits like non-timber produce and a share of timber. First resolution: Odisha, 1988.

Q10. Why is the Himalayan Yew in trouble?

Taxol, extracted from its bark, needles, twigs and roots, treats cancer, so the tree was over-exploited; thousands of trees have dried up in Arunachal and Himachal.
