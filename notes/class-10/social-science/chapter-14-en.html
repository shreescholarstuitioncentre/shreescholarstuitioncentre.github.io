1. What is Federalism?

Federalism is a system of government in which power is divided between a central authority and the constituent units (states/provinces) of the country. In a unitary system there is one level of government, or the sub-units are subordinate to the centre, which can give them orders (e.g., Sri Lanka). Belgium shifted from unitary to federal after 1993; Sri Lanka is still unitary.

Key features of federalism:
  • Two or more levels (tiers) of government.
  • Different tiers govern the same citizens, but each has its own jurisdiction in legislation, taxation and administration.
  • Jurisdictions are specified in the Constitution, so each tier's existence and authority is guaranteed.
  • Fundamental provisions cannot be changed by one level alone; both levels must agree.
  • Courts (High Courts and Supreme Court) act as umpires in disputes between levels.
  • Sources of revenue are specified, ensuring financial autonomy.
  • Dual objectives: safeguard and promote unity, and accommodate regional diversity. This needs mutual trust and agreement to live together.

3. Federalism in India

The Constitution calls India a Union of States. After Independence, a strong centre was thought necessary for unity. Power is divided through three lists in the Seventh Schedule:

  • Union List (97 subjects): defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications, currency – national uniformity needed; only Parliament can legislate.
  • State List (66 subjects): police, trade, commerce, agriculture, irrigation – only state legislatures can legislate.
  • Concurrent List (47 subjects): education, forests, trade unions, marriage, adoption, succession – both can legislate; if they conflict, the Union law prevails.
  • Residuary subjects (not in any list, e.g., computer software) go to the Union.

Explanation: The Constitution (Seventh Schedule) is the source from which powers flow into three lists. Union List = Centre only, State List = States only, Concurrent List = both (Centre wins on conflict). Subjects left over, such as software, go to the Union.

Safeguards: Parliament cannot change this arrangement alone – an amendment needs a two-thirds majority in both Houses and ratification by at least half of the State legislatures. Courts settle disputes. Union and States have separate taxes (e.g., income tax for Union); both have financial autonomy. Special status existed for J&K under Article 370 (removed in 2019; it is now a Union Territory, with Ladakh). Union Territories (Chandigarh, Lakshadweep, Delhi etc.) are too small to be states and are administered by the Centre.

4. How is Federalism Practised in India?

  • Linguistic states: 1950s – old states were redrawn on language (SRC 1956); later Maharashtra and Gujarat (1960), Punjab and Haryana (1966), and Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand, Jharkhand (2000), Telangana (2014). Some states (Nagaland etc.) were formed on culture, ethnicity or geography. It made the country more united.
  • Language policy: No national language. Hindi is the official language (mother tongue of ~40%); 22 Scheduled languages; English continues for official use (after Tamil Nadu protests). States have their own official languages.
  • Centre–State relations: Earlier one party ruled both, so the Centre dominated. After 1990, coalition governments and regional parties gave states more say; the Supreme Court (S.R. Bommai case, 1994) made arbitrary dismissal of state governments difficult.

5. Decentralisation – the Third Tier

Many issues are best solved locally, so power is shared further with local governments. In 1992 the Constitution was amended to make them stronger: regular elections; reserved seats for SC, ST and OBC; one-third seats for women; State Election Commission; states share power and revenue.

B. Short Answer

1. What is federalism?

A system where power is divided between a central authority and constituent units like states, each with its own sphere, set by the Constitution.

2. Differentiate unitary and federal governments.

Unitary: one level, or sub-units subordinate to the centre. Federal: two or more levels with constitutionally guaranteed powers of their own.

3. What are the three lists in the Seventh Schedule?

Union List (97 subjects), State List (66) and Concurrent List (47); residuary subjects go to the Union.

4. Why are linguistic states formed?

To respect regional language and culture, make administration easier and strengthen unity in diversity.

5. What is a Gram Sabha?

The body of all voters in a village that elects and oversees the Gram Panchayat.

C. Long Answer

1. Explain the main features of federalism.

Two or more tiers; each with own jurisdiction over same citizens; powers specified in the Constitution; basic provisions amendable only with consent of both levels; courts as umpires; separate revenue sources; and dual aim of unity and diversity based on mutual trust.

2. How is power divided between the Union and States in India?

By the Seventh Schedule: Union List (97 subjects, Parliament), State List (66, state legislatures), Concurrent List (47, both; Union law prevails). Residuary subjects go to the Union. The arrangement can be amended only by a two-thirds majority in Parliament with ratification by half of states, and courts settle disputes.

3. Describe the three-tier system and importance of local government.

The third tier is the Panchayati Raj (Gram Panchayat, Panchayat Samiti, Zila Parishad) in villages and municipalities/corporations in towns/cities. Since the 1992 amendment they have regular elections, reserved seats (one-third for women) and a State Election Commission. They solve local problems, deepen democracy and bring people into governance.
