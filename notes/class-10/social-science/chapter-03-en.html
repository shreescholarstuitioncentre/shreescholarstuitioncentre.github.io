The Making of a Global World

Class 10 · Social Science (History) · Chapter 3 · Detailed Notes

1. Introduction & Overview

Today we live in a globalised world — food, clothes, phones and ideas come from across the planet. This chapter traces how the world economy took shape: from ancient trade links, through colonial conquest and the industrial age, to the wars, depression and post-war order that shaped modern globalisation.

  • Globalisation: growing economic, cultural and political interconnection between countries.
  • Historians identify three types of flows: trade (goods), migration (labour/people) and capital movement (investment, loans).
  • Until the 18th century China and India were among the richest countries; after the 1500s, the centre of world trade shifted towards Europe and the Atlantic.

2. The Pre-modern World

Silk Routes link the world

  • Silk Routes (named after the Chinese silk that travelled on them) existed even before the Christian era and flourished until about the 15th century. They connected Asia with Europe and North Africa, by land and sea.
  • Goods travelled: Chinese pottery, Indian textiles and spices, and — later — precious metals (gold and silver) from the Americas.
  • Ideas and culture travelled too: Christian missionaries, Muslim preachers and Buddhist monks moved along these routes; Buddhism spread from eastern India to many regions.

Food travels

  • Noodles possibly travelled west from China and became spaghetti; or Arab traders took pasta to Sicily — similar foods in different places show old cultural exchange.
  • Many everyday crops were unknown before the Europeans reached the Americas: potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies, sweet potatoes. They arrived in Europe, Asia and Africa only after Columbus, transforming diets (the potato saved many poor Europeans from hunger).

Conquest, disease and trade

  • In the 16th century Europeans found a sea route to Asia and crossed the western ocean to the Americas — the 'New World'. The Portuguese and Spanish began conquest and colonisation by the mid-16th century.
  • Europe's conquest was not due to weapons alone: germs such as smallpox, to which the original inhabitants had no immunity, killed huge populations — the most powerful 'weapon' of conquest.
  • Rich silver mines (Potosi in present-day Bolivia, and Mexico) and the legend of El Dorado (a city of gold) drew Europeans; American silver helped finance Europe's trade with Asia.
  • Until the 19th century, poverty and hunger were common in Europe; epidemics and disease were frequent. Plantations of cotton and sugar in the Americas depended on enslaved African labour.

3. The Nineteenth Century (1815–1914)

In the 19th century, the world economy changed: economic, political, social, cultural and technological factors transformed societies and altered external relations.

A world economy takes shape — food

  • Britain's population grew from the late 18th century, increasing demand for food grain. Landed groups pressured the government to restrict the import of corn: the Corn Laws.
  • Food prices rose; industrialists and urban dwellers pressed for the laws' removal, and the Corn Laws were abolished (1846).
  • Food could now be imported more cheaply than it was grown in Britain. British agriculture could not compete; vast areas were left uncultivated and people migrated to cities or overseas.
  • As incomes rose, British demand for food rose, and new lands in Eastern Europe, Russia, America and Australia were cleared for cultivation — by migrant workers from Europe and elsewhere.
  • By 1890 a global agricultural economy had taken shape: food came from thousands of miles away, grown on large farms (once forest or grassland) by recently arrived workers — with changes in labour movement, capital flows, ecology and technology.

Role of technology

  • Railways carried food from farms to ports; steamships made sea transport faster and cheaper; the telegraph speeded communication.
  • Refrigerated ships: earlier, live animals were shipped to Europe and slaughtered there, taking up huge space. With refrigeration, meat, dairy and perishable food could be transported — lowering the price of meat in Europe.
  • Technology interacted with colonialism: for example, railways were built and ports were expanded to serve colonial trade.

Migration

  • About 50 million people migrated from Europe to America and Australia in the 19th century; worldwide perhaps 150 million moved in search of a better life.
  • Rapid industrialisation in Europe gave jobs in cities but also unemployment and poverty; people moved to the Americas and Australia, where land and work were available.

4. Colonialism, Rinderpest and Indentured Labour

Late-19th-century colonialism

  • Trade expanded, but with it came loss of freedom and livelihood for many. In 1885 European powers met in Berlin to complete the carving up of Africa among themselves.
  • Britain and France made vast additions to their colonies; Belgium and Germany became new colonial powers; the USA took over colonies from Spain in the late 1890s.

Rinderpest — a cattle plague in Africa

  • In the 1890s rinderpest reached Africa's east coast, brought by infected cattle imported from British Asia to feed Italian soldiers invading Eritrea.
  • It spread west 'like forest fire' and reached the Atlantic coast in 1892, and the Cape five years later (1897). Along the way 90% of cattle died.
  • Loss of cattle destroyed African livelihoods. To survive, Africans were forced to work for wages in mines and plantations; the epidemic helped European colonisers conquer and subdue Africa.

Indentured labour migration from India

  • Indentured labourer: a bonded labourer under contract to work for an employer for a specific period to repay the cost of passage to a new country.
  • Workers came from eastern Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, central India and dry districts of Tamil Nadu — drivers: cottage industries declining, land rents rising, mines & plantations demanding workers, famines.
  • Destinations: Caribbean islands (Trinidad, Guyana, Surinam), Mauritius, Fiji; Tamils to Ceylon and Malaya; many to Assam tea plantations.
  • Recruited by agents with false promises about work and living conditions, many did not know the destination or the nature of the journey. Conditions were harsh; workers had few legal rights; many tried to escape.
  • Cultural forms emerged: Hosay (the annual Muharram procession) in Trinidad, chutney music.
  • From the 20th century Indian nationalist leaders opposed the system as abusive and degrading; it was abolished in 1921.
  • Indian entrepreneurs abroad: Shikaripuri shroffs and Nattukottai Chettiars financed agricultural exports in Central and South-east Asia; Hyderabadi Sindhi traders set up emporia in the world's ports.

5. Indian Trade, Colonialism and the Global System

  • Before machine industries, India was a major supplier of fine cotton textiles to the world. With the growth of British cotton manufacturing, industrialists pressed the government to restrict textile imports and open India's market to British goods.
  • Indian cotton cloth exports fell sharply (cotton goods were roughly one-third of India's exports in 1811–12; only about 3% by 1850–51), while exports of raw materials (raw cotton, indigo, opium, wheat) rose.
  • Britain exported opium grown in India to China to pay for its tea imports.
  • Britain had a trade surplus with India and a trade deficit with the USA and other industrialising countries; the surplus from India helped Britain settle its deficits — a multilateral pattern of settlements.
  • India also had to bear 'Home Charges' — interest on external debts, pensions of British officials in India.

6. The Inter-war Economy

The First World War (1914–18)

  • Fought between the Allies (Britain, France, Russia — later joined by the USA) and the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Turkey). The first modern industrial war — machine guns, tanks, aircraft, chemical weapons.
  • About 9 million died and 20 million were injured, mostly the working-age population, shrinking the workforce and household incomes.
  • Industries were restructured for war production. Britain borrowed heavily from the US; the war transformed the US from an international debtor into an international creditor.

Post-war recovery was difficult

  • Britain: huge war debts; lost markets (e.g. Indian textile market to Japan and local mills); unemployment and slow recovery. In 1921 one in five British workers was out of work.
  • Wheat: wartime shortages led Canada, USA, Argentina and Australia to expand production; when Europe recovered, overproduction caused falling prices and farm incomes.

Rise of mass production and consumption

  • Henry Ford pioneered the assembly line (inspired by Chicago slaughter-houses) — cheaper cars, higher output, higher wages.
  • In the 1920s the US had a consumer boom: cars, refrigerators, washing machines, radios, gramophones, bought on hire purchase (credit); a house-building boom added to the demand.
  • The US resumed capital exports in 1923, lending abroad to help Europe's recovery — but this depended on credit.

The Great Depression (1929 – mid-1930s)

  • Causes: (1) agricultural overproduction → falling prices; (2) reliance on short-term US loans — when the US shrank lending abroad, many countries collapsed; (3) Wall Street crash, 1929: share prices fell, banks closed; (4) the US raised import duties, worsening world trade collapse.
  • Effects: unemployment (about a quarter of US workers jobless at its peak), banks failed, firms closed, world trade shrank sharply, income and prices fell. Recovery began slowly in the mid-1930s.

India and the Great Depression

  • India's exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934; wheat prices fell by about 50%. Farmers were hit harder than urban workers: they were indebted and the government refused to cut revenue demands.
  • Bengal jute growers were ruined; rural distress contributed to unrest and the Civil Disobedience Movement (1930).
  • During the depression India became an exporter of gold; the outflow helped British finances and global recovery.

7. Rebuilding a World Economy: the Post-war Era

  • The Second World War (1939–45) caused enormous loss: millions killed, cities destroyed. Economists drew two lessons from the inter-war years: (1) mass production needs mass consumption (high incomes); (2) a stable world economy and full employment need government intervention.
  • Bretton Woods Conference (July 1944, New Hampshire, USA) set up the IMF (to deal with external surpluses and deficits of member countries) and the World Bank (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, to finance post-war reconstruction). The system of fixed exchange rates is the Bretton Woods system. The IMF and World Bank are called the 'Bretton Woods twins'.
  • Decolonisation: many Asian and African countries became independent; but they were poor and burdened. Developing nations felt the IMF and World Bank served mainly industrial countries, so in the 1950s–60s they formed the G-77 and demanded a New International Economic Order (NIEO) — control over their natural resources and fairer trade.
  • In the 1950s–60s, the Western economies grew rapidly with high employment. Transnational corporations (TNCs) expanded, operating in several countries at once.
  • In the 1970s the Bretton Woods system ended; fixed exchange rates gave way to floating exchange rates. Industrial production shifted to low-wage Asia (e.g. China after its economic reforms), which gained from cheaper labour.
  • In the 1990s the Soviet Union and its allies collapsed; China, India, Brazil and others integrated further into the world economy, with rising share of the developing world in global trade.

10. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. How did the Silk Routes help the world?
They connected Asia, Europe and North Africa, carrying silk, pottery, textiles and spices, and spreading religions and ideas such as Buddhism, Christianity and Islam.

Q2. How did the Americas change world food and trade?
Crops like potato, maize, tomato, chilli, soya and groundnut reached Europe, Asia and Africa; silver from Potosi and Mexico financed trade.

Q3. What were the Corn Laws? Why were they abolished?
Laws restricting corn imports into Britain, supported by landowners. Industrialists and urban people, hurt by high food prices, pushed for abolition (1846); cheap food imports followed.

Q4. How did technology shape the 19th-century world economy?
Railways, steamships, the telegraph and refrigerated ships lowered costs and linked distant markets; meat and grain could be traded globally.

Q5. Explain the effects of rinderpest in Africa.
It killed about 90% of cattle, destroying livelihoods; Africans were forced into wage labour, and colonisers strengthened control.

Q6. What was indentured labour? Why did Indians migrate?
A bonded contract labour system; poverty, loss of cottage industry, high rents, and recruiters' false promises pushed Indians to plantations abroad; abolished 1921.

Q7. Why did the Great Depression happen?
Agricultural overproduction, falling prices, reliance on short-term US loans, withdrawal of those loans, the 1929 stock-market crash and protectionist tariffs.

Q8. How did the Great Depression affect India?
Exports/imports nearly halved; farm prices (e.g. wheat) fell ~50%; peasants went into debt; jute growers suffered; gold flowed out.

Q9. What was the Bretton Woods agreement?
July 1944 agreement creating the IMF and World Bank and a fixed-exchange-rate system to stabilise the post-war economy.

Q10. Why did developing countries form the G-77?
They felt the Bretton Woods institutions served industrial countries and demanded a New International Economic Order with control over their resources.

11. Quick Revision

  • 3 flows: trade, migration, capital. Silk Routes → ideas + goods; Americas → new crops, smallpox, silver.
  • 19th c.: Corn Laws (abolished 1846) → cheap food; railways, steamships, refrigeration → global food economy.
  • Colonial: 1885 Berlin; rinderpest (1892 coast, 1897 Cape); indentured labour (abolished 1921).
  • India: textiles ↓, raw materials ↑; Home Charges; trade surplus helps Britain.
  • 1914–18 WWI → 1920s Ford, consumer boom → 1929 Depression (India: prices ~50% ↓).
  • 1944 Bretton Woods: IMF + World Bank, fixed rates → 1970s floating rates, TNCs, Asia rises → 1990s new phase.
