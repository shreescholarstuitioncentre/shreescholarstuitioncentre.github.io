1. Introduction: Agriculture in India

India is an agriculturally important country. Agriculture is a primary activity that produces most of the food we consume and many raw materials for industries (cotton, jute, sugarcane, oilseeds). It also earns foreign exchange through exports of tea, coffee, spices, etc. About two-thirds of India's population depends on agriculture for their livelihood (as per the textbook). Its share in the GDP is falling as industry and services grow, but it remains vital for food security and employment.

Farming varies with physical factors (relief, soil, climate), technology, institutions (land ownership) and people's needs. India grows many crops because of its varied climate and soils.

Note: Agriculture also includes growing crops, fruits, vegetables, flowers, rearing livestock and related activities such as sericulture.

2. Types of Farming

(a) Primitive Subsistence Farming

Practised on small patches of land using primitive tools (hoe, dao, digging stick) and family or community labour. It depends on monsoon, natural soil fertility and suitability of other environmental conditions. It is a "slash and burn" agriculture: farmers clear a patch, burn the vegetation, use the ash to enrich the soil, grow crops for a few years, and then abandon the patch to let the soil recover while they move to a new one.

  • Known as Jhumming in North-East India; Milpa in Mexico and Central America; Conuco in Venezuela; Roca in Brazil; Ladang in Malaysia, etc.
  • Yield is low because no fertilisers or modern inputs are used; it causes deforestation and soil loss.

(b) Intensive Subsistence Farming

Practised in areas of high population pressure on land. It is labour-intensive, with high doses of biochemical inputs and irrigation to get higher production from limited land. Since the right of inheritance divides land among successive generations, the farm size becomes smaller and smaller, forcing farmers to take the maximum output from small plots — and sometimes to shift to other jobs.

(c) Commercial Farming

Uses higher doses of modern inputs (HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers, insecticides, pesticides) to obtain higher productivity. The degree of commercialisation varies — rice is a commercial crop in Haryana and Punjab but a subsistence crop in Odisha.

Plantation is a type of commercial farming: a single crop is grown on a large area, with large capital and migrant labour. The produce is processed in industries or sold in markets, so plantations are linked with transport and communication. Examples: tea, coffee, rubber, sugarcane, banana. Assam and North Bengal grow tea; Karnataka grows coffee and Kerala rubber.

4. Major Crops

A variety of food and non-food crops are grown in different parts of India depending on the soil, climate and cultivation practices. Major crops are rice, wheat, millets, pulses, tea, coffee, sugarcane, oil seeds, cotton and jute.

Food crops

Rice is the staple food crop of a majority of Indians. India is the second largest producer after China. It is a kharif crop needing high temperature (above 25°C), high humidity and annual rainfall above 100 cm; where rain is less, irrigation is used (Punjab, Haryana, western UP, parts of Rajasthan). Wheat is the second most important cereal and the main food crop in the north and north-west; it is a rabi crop needing a cool growing season and bright sunshine at ripening, with 50–75 cm of evenly distributed rainfall. Millets (jowar, bajra, ragi) are coarse grains with high nutritional value (ragi is rich in iron, calcium and micro-nutrients). Maize is both food and fodder, a kharif crop (but in Bihar grown in rabi too). Pulses – India is the largest producer and consumer; they are leguminous and fix nitrogen in the soil, so they are grown in rotation with other crops.

Oilseeds, sugarcane, plantation and fibre crops

India is the largest producer of oilseeds; groundnut is a kharif crop (about half of the major oilseed production; Gujarat is the leading producer). Sugarcane needs hot, humid climate (India is the second largest producer after Brazil) and is the main source of sugar, gur and khandsari. Tea and coffee are plantation beverage crops. Rubber is an equatorial crop. Cotton and jute are important fibre crops; jute is the "golden fibre", while the decline of jute is due to competition from synthetic fibres and substitutes.

Horticulture

India is a producer of tropical and temperate fruits and vegetables, and is the second largest producer of vegetables after China. Famous: mangoes (Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, UP, West Bengal), oranges (Nagpur and Cherrapunji, Meghalaya), bananas (Kerala, Mizoram, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu), litchi and guava (UP, Bihar), pineapples (Meghalaya), grapes (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Maharashtra), and apples, pears, apricots, walnuts (Jammu & Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh).

5. Technological and Institutional Reforms

Agriculture has been practised for thousands of years; with increasing population and little change in techniques, productivity and farmers' conditions remained low. The government therefore introduced institutional and technical reforms after Independence.

Institutional reforms

  • Land reforms: collectivisation, consolidation of holdings, cooperation and abolition of zamindari; some (Kerala, West Bengal) were effective, others not, due to fragmented holdings and political weakness.
  • Bhoodan–Gramdan (1951): Vinoba Bhave's movement — landowners donated land to the landless; called the "Bloodless Revolution".
  • Kisan Credit Card (KCC), Personal Accident Insurance Scheme (PAIS), Grameen banks and cooperative societies for easy credit; Minimum Support Price (MSP) and remunerative procurement prices; special weather bulletins and farming programmes on radio and TV.
  • Comprehensive land development programme in the 1980s–90s with both institutional and technological reforms.

Technological reforms

  • Green Revolution (1960s–70s): use of HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation to raise yields of wheat and rice; made India self-sufficient in food, though it benefited mostly Punjab, Haryana, western UP, parts of Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu.
  • White Revolution (Operation Flood): rise in milk production through cooperatives.
Remember: Green Revolution = technology (seeds + inputs); Bhoodan–Gramdan = institutional reform (land).

6. Contribution to the Economy, Globalisation and Food Security

  • Contribution: Agriculture employs about half of India's workers but its share in GDP is declining (it was around two-fifths in earlier decades and is now well below one-fifth) — a concern, since the sector needs more investment in irrigation, power, storage, marketing and credit.
  • Globalisation: From the 1990s, Indian farmers face competition from developed countries which subsidise their farmers heavily. Indian farmers need better inputs, support and prices to be competitive; the export of crops such as cotton, tea and spices is shaped by global markets.
  • Food security: Government ensures food grains for all through MSP, procurement, buffer stock and the Public Distribution System (PDS).
  • Organic farming: Use of fewer chemicals and more natural manure is promoted for sustainable farming and healthier food.

Way forward

Agriculture must be made sustainable and profitable: improving irrigation, giving farmers credit and fair prices, promoting organic farming, diversifying crops (more pulses, millets, horticulture), and reducing dependence on the monsoon.

8. Important Questions and Answers

Q1. Name one important beverage crop and the regions where it is grown.

Tea — grown in Assam, Darjeeling and Jalpaiguri hills of West Bengal, Tamil Nadu and Kerala; it needs warm, moist, frost-free climate and deep, well-drained soil.

Q2. Which crop is called the 'golden fibre'? Why is its demand falling?

Jute. Its demand has fallen because of competition from synthetic fibres and other substitutes.

Q3. Differentiate between Rabi and Kharif crops.

Rabi: sown October–December, harvested April–June (wheat, barley, gram, mustard). Kharif: sown with the monsoon, harvested September–October (rice, maize, cotton, jute, groundnut).

Q4. What is slash-and-burn farming?

Farmers clear a patch of land, burn the vegetation, use the ash to enrich the soil, grow crops for a few years and then abandon the plot — called Jhumming in North-East India.

Q5. Describe the conditions required for growing rice.

High temperature (above 25°C), high humidity and annual rainfall above 100 cm; irrigation where rainfall is less. It grows in plains, coastal and deltaic areas.

Q6. Why are pulses important? How do they help the soil?

Pulses are the major source of protein in vegetarian diets. Being leguminous, they fix nitrogen and restore soil fertility, so they are grown in rotation.

Q7. What is the Green Revolution? What was its impact?

The use of HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation in the 1960s–70s raised wheat and rice output and made India self-sufficient in food, but benefited mainly a few states.

Q8. What is Bhoodan–Gramdan?

A movement of 1951 by Vinoba Bhave in which landlords gave land to the landless; called the 'Bloodless Revolution'.

Q9. What is plantation farming? Give examples.

Commercial farming of a single crop on a large estate with heavy capital and migrant labour — tea, coffee, rubber, sugarcane, banana.

Q10. How does globalisation affect Indian agriculture?

Indian farmers face competition from heavily subsidised farmers abroad, so they need better support, inputs and prices; sustainable and organic farming are promoted.
