Ch. 20: Money and Credit

1. Money as a Medium of Exchange

Barter system: direct exchange of goods without money (e.g., shoes for wheat). It needs a double coincidence of wants – what one person wants must be what the other has, and vice versa. This is very difficult.

Money solves this – it is a medium of exchange. A person holding money can easily exchange it for any commodity or service. It saves time and removes the need for double coincidence of wants. Money also acts as a unit of account and store of value.

2. Modern Forms of Money

  • Currency: Earlier – grains, metals (gold, silver, copper coins). Today – paper notes and coins. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) issues currency notes on behalf of the central government. In India, no other individual or organisation is allowed to issue currency and the rupee is accepted as legal tender – nobody can refuse payment in rupees.
  • Deposits with banks: People deposit extra cash in banks in demand deposit accounts, which can be withdrawn on demand. Payment can be made through cheque (or digital means like UPI, net banking, cards) without cash. As deposits are accepted widely as payment, they are also considered money.
Cheque: A paper instructing the bank to pay a specific amount from the person's account to the person named on it. Digital payment (UPI etc.) works on the same principle – transfer between bank accounts.

3. Loan Activities of Banks

Banks keep only about 15% of deposits as cash (to pay depositors who withdraw) and use the major portion to extend loans. There is a huge demand for loans for various economic activities. Banks charge higher interest on loans than they pay on deposits; this difference is their main source of income.

Explanation: Depositors give money to bank and receive a small interest; the bank lends most of it at higher interest. The gap is the bank's income. Because people can pull out money any time, banks keep a cash reserve.

4. Credit (Loan) – Meaning and Terms

Credit is an agreement in which the lender supplies the borrower with money, goods or services in return for the promise of future payment. Credit can help people run business (working capital – e.g., buying raw material), earn more and grow – or hurt them when income falls (credit trap).

TermMeaning
Interest rateExtra amount the borrower pays on the loan
CollateralAsset (land, building, vehicle, livestock, bank deposits) the borrower owns and gives as guarantee to lender until the loan is repaid; if not repaid, lender can sell it
DocumentationRequired papers – proof of address, income, ID, etc.
Mode of repaymentHow and when the loan is repaid – instalments, lump sum, period
Terms of credit vary with lender and borrower. For the same need, a bank loan may be cheap (low interest) but with collateral and paperwork; a moneylender may lend easily but at very high interest. Credit is good or bad depending on the situation – with crop failure a farmer can fall into debt trap, unable to repay and may lose land.

5. Formal and Informal Sources of Credit

Formal sectorInformal sector
Banks and cooperatives (also government schemes)Moneylenders, traders, employers, relatives, friends
Supervised by RBI – banks must maintain cash balance, lend to small borrowers, report how much, to whom, at what interestNo organisation supervises; they lend at whatever rate they like
Lower, regulated interest; no illegal practicesVery high interest; unfair practices to recover loans; cheating
Needs documents and collateral, so poor are often left outEasily available; no papers needed
Why more formal credit is needed: Cost of borrowing from informal sources is high → borrower's income left is low → may fall into debt trap. Rich households mostly use formal credit, but poor households depend heavily on informal sources. So formal credit must be expanded so everyone, especially the poor, gets cheaper loans.

6. Self Help Groups (SHGs) for the Poor

People who cannot give collateral are helped by Self Help Groups: 15–20 members (mostly women) of a neighbourhood, who meet regularly and save small amounts (₹25–100 per month). Savings are kept with a bank in the group's name. Members can take small loans from the group at reasonable interest; the group decides purpose, interest, repayment, and takes care of default. After regular savings for a year or two, the group can get bank loans without collateral. Meetings also discuss social issues (health, nutrition, domestic violence).

Diagram 2 – Self Help Group (animated)

Explanation: Members pool savings, lend to each other at fair interest, and the group borrows from the bank on trust. Grameen Bank of Bangladesh (Muhammad Yunus) is a famous example of reaching the poor without collateral.
Role of RBI: India's central bank – issues currency, supervises banks, controls credit flow and interest-related rules, protects depositors.
