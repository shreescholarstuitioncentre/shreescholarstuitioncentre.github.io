1. Introduction: Water, a Renewable Resource

About three-fourths of the earth's surface is covered with water, yet only a tiny part of it is fresh water usable by people. Fresh water comes mainly from rainfall, surface run-off (rivers, ponds, lakes) and ground water, and it is renewed and recharged continuously through the hydrological (water) cycle. That is why water is called a renewable resource.

The hydrological cycle step by step

  1. Evaporation – the Sun heats oceans, rivers and lakes; water turns into vapour (plants add vapour by transpiration).
  2. Condensation – vapour rises, cools and forms clouds.
  3. Precipitation – water falls as rain, snow or hail.
  4. Run-off and infiltration – some water flows into rivers and returns to the sea; some soaks into the ground and becomes ground water.
Remember: All water on earth moves in a never-ending cycle, but the usable fresh-water share is small and unevenly distributed in space and time — hence the need to manage it.

Why is water so important?

  • Needed for drinking, cooking, cleaning and all life processes.
  • Essential for agriculture (irrigation), industry and power generation.
  • India gets nearly 4 per cent of the world's precipitation and ranks around 133rd in the world in terms of water availability per person.

2. Water Scarcity and the Need for Conservation

Water scarcity usually brings to mind dry regions, but even areas with plenty of water face scarcity. The cause is often over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access to water among different social groups — not just low rainfall. Also, by 2025 large parts of India are expected to face absolute water scarcity (as per the projection in the textbook). Total renewable water resources are about 1,897 sq km per year, of which only about 1,122 sq km is actually usable because of topography and other limits.

Main causes of water scarcity

CauseExplanation
Growing populationMore people → more demand for domestic use and for food; unequal access to water.
Irrigation and commercial cropsGreen Revolution crops (wheat, rice) and commercial crops need much water; farmers dig wells and tube wells, so the ground-water table falls.
IndustrialisationAfter independence many industries used huge quantities of fresh water; they are also heavy users of hydro-electricity.
UrbanisationLarge cities with housing societies pump ground water with multiple bore wells, and they depend on it; over-extraction dries up sources.
PollutionIndustrial effluents, chemicals, detergents and sewage pollute rivers and ground water, reducing usable water.

Need for conservation and management

  • Fresh water is limited and costly to treat and carry.
  • Clean water is a basic need and right; scarcity hurts health, farming and industry.
  • Water conservation protects ecosystems, rivers and future generations (sustainable development).

3. Multi-purpose River Projects and Integrated Water Resources Management

Water works through history

Water has been harvested and stored in India since ancient times:

  • Sringaverapura near Prayagraj (1st century BC) – a sophisticated water-harvesting system to channel the flood water of the Ganga.
  • Chandragupta Maurya's time – dams, lakes and sophisticated irrigation systems were built.
  • Bhopal Lake (11th century) – one of the largest artificial lakes of its time.
  • Hauz Khas tank, Delhi (14th century) – built for supplying water to Siri Fort area.

What is a dam?

A dam is a barrier across flowing water that obstructs, directs or retards the flow, often creating a reservoir, a lake or an impoundment. Dams are classified according to structure or material (timber, masonry, embankment/concrete) or according to height (large dams, major dams; low, medium, high dams).

Jawaharlal Nehru proudly called dams the "temples of modern India" because they were thought to integrate agricultural and industrial development with the growth of the village and city economies.

Multi-purpose river projects

A multi-purpose project serves many uses at once: irrigation, hydro-electric power, water supply, flood control, recreation, inland navigation and fish breeding. The rivers are seen as one linked system — so the benefits are shared and water is integrated across uses (e.g. the water released after power generation irrigates fields).

Examples: Bhakra-Nangal (Sutlej-Beas), Hirakud (Mahanadi), Damodar Valley Corporation, Sardar Sarovar (Narmada), Tungabhadra, Rihand.

Benefits and problems

BenefitsProblems / Criticism
Irrigation and food production; hydro-electricity; drinking-water and industrial supply; flood control; navigation; recreation and fish breeding.Dams trap sediment (silt) so the river bed fills and soil becomes less fertile downstream; they block fish migration; forests and land are submerged and species lose habitat; thousands of people are displaced (often tribal and poor) and lose livelihoods; may cause floods (instead of controlling them); changes in cropping pattern (water-intensive crops), salinisation; inter-state water disputes (e.g. Krishna–Godavari) over sharing the benefits.
Protests: Local people started movements such as the Narmada Bachao Andolan (against the Sardar Sarovar dam on the Narmada) and the Tehri Dam movement (Ganga basin). Cropping-pattern changes and water-sharing conflicts also grew — so alternatives like rainwater harvesting are promoted.

4. Rainwater Harvesting

Because of the problems with big dams, rainwater harvesting – collecting and storing rain water and recharging the ground water – is promoted as a low-cost, eco-friendly and sustainable alternative. In ancient India, people had developed many rainwater-harvesting methods suited to their local ecological conditions.

Traditional methods by region

  • Hilly and mountainous regions: "guls" or "kuls" (diversion channels along hill slopes) in the Western Himalayas for agriculture; rooftop rainwater harvesting was common in Rajasthan.
  • Rajasthan (arid and semi-arid): tankas (underground tanks) in Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer; khadins in Jaisalmer and johads in other parts to store water and irrigate.
  • West Bengal: inundation channels to irrigate fields from flood water.
  • Meghalaya: the 200-year-old bamboo drip irrigation.

Roof-top rainwater harvesting

Rain falling on the roof flows through gutters and pipes into a filter, then into a storage tank; the excess may go to a recharge pit to refill ground water.

Tankas of Rajasthan

  • A tanka is an underground tank, often built near or inside the house or courtyard, connected to the sloping roof by pipes. A typical tank in Phalodi is about 6.1 m deep, 4.27 m long and 2.44 m wide.
  • The first spell of rain is not collected to clean the roof and pipes; later rain is stored.
  • The stored water, called "palar pani", is the purest natural water and is used till the next rains — especially in summer when other sources dry up.

Bamboo drip irrigation, Meghalaya

About 200 years ago a system of bamboo pipes was developed to tap the springs of the hills. Around 18–20 litres of water enters the bamboo pipe, and it reaches the plant at about 20–80 drops per minute after flowing through pipes of different sizes.

Rainwater harvesting in cities and states

  • In Shillong, roof-top harvesting is practised even though Cherrapunji and Mawsynram get heavy rain; about 15–25 per cent of water needs are met from the roofs.
  • Gendathur village in Mysuru, Karnataka: about 200 households have roof-top harvesting structures.
  • Tamil Nadu is the first state to make roof-top rainwater harvesting compulsory for all houses.
  • In the semi-arid regions of Rajasthan, people harvested water through tankas; but with the Indira Gandhi Canal, the use of roof-top harvesting has decreased as the canal gives water.

5. Ways to Conserve Water

  • Harvest rain from roofs and fields; recharge ground water.
  • Revive traditional systems – tankas, johads, step-wells, ponds.
  • Use water carefully: drip and sprinkler irrigation, repair leaks, avoid wastage.
  • Reuse and recycle household and industrial water.
  • Prevent pollution of rivers and lakes; treat sewage.
  • Grow suitable crops that need less water in dry regions.
  • Public awareness and community participation, along with sensible laws.

7. Important Questions and Answers

Q1. Why is water called a renewable resource?

Because it is continuously renewed and recharged through the hydrological cycle (evaporation, condensation, precipitation).

Q2. Give three reasons for water scarcity in India.

Rapid population growth; over-use for irrigation and industry; urbanisation and pollution of water sources; unequal access.

Q3. What are multi-purpose river projects? Name their purposes.

Projects built on rivers that serve many purposes together: irrigation, power generation, water supply, flood control, navigation, recreation and fish breeding.

Q4. Why did Nehru call dams the "temples of modern India"?

He believed dams would integrate agricultural and industrial development and the village and city economies, and help in national progress.

Q5. Why are multi-purpose projects opposed?

They cause sedimentation, harm aquatic life, submerge land and forests, displace people, change cropping patterns, cause salinisation and create water-sharing conflicts.

Q6. Name two movements against dams.

Narmada Bachao Andolan (Sardar Sarovar) and the Tehri Dam movement.

Q7. Describe the roof-top rainwater harvesting system.

Roof → gutters/pipes → filter → storage tank, and excess to a recharge pit; the stored water is used later and ground water is recharged.

Q8. What is a tanka? How does it work?

An underground tank in Rajasthan connected to the roof by pipes. First rain is left out to wash the roof; later rain is stored as 'palar pani' for use through the dry season.

Q9. How is water harvested in Meghalaya?

By the 200-year-old bamboo drip irrigation – bamboo pipes bring spring water to plants at a drip rate of 20–80 drops/minute.

Q10. Which state made roof-top harvesting compulsory?

Tamil Nadu — the first state to do so for all houses.
