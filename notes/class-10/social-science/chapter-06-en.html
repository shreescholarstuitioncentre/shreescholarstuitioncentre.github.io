Resources and Development

Class 10 · Social Science (Geography) · Chapter 6 · Detailed Notes

1. What is a Resource?

Everything available in our environment which can be used to satisfy our needs, provided it is technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable, can be termed a resource.

  • Resources are not — they become. A thing is a resource only if people can reach it with technology, can afford to use it, and their culture accepts using it.
  • Example of cultural acceptance: in some regions people accept certain food items; elsewhere the same item is not considered food.
  • Interdependence: human beings interact with nature through technology and create institutions to accelerate economic development. Nature, technology and institutions are interdependent in transforming the environment into resources.

2. Classification of Resources

Resources can be classified on four bases.

(a) On the basis of origin

  • Biotic — obtained from the biosphere and having life: human beings, flora and fauna, fisheries, livestock.
  • Abiotic — all non-living things: land, water, air, minerals (rocks and metals).

(b) On the basis of exhaustibility

  • Renewable — can be renewed or reproduced by physical, chemical or mechanical processes: solar and wind energy, water, forests and wildlife. They are further classed as continuous or flow (e.g. wind, solar) and biological.
  • Non-renewable — occur over very long geological time (formed over millions of years): minerals and fossil fuels. Some, like metals, are recyclable; others like fossil fuels cannot be recycled and get exhausted with use.

(c) On the basis of ownership

  • Individual — privately owned by individuals: plots, houses, plantations, pastures, ponds, water in wells. Land-owning villagers; urban people with house plots.
  • Community-owned — available to all members of the community: village commons (grazing grounds, burial grounds, village ponds), public parks, picnic spots, playgrounds.
  • National — technically, all resources belong to the nation. The country has legal powers to acquire even private property for public good. Minerals, water resources, forests, wildlife, land within the political boundaries and oceanic area up to 12 nautical miles (territorial water) from the coast belong to the nation.
  • International — regulated by international institutions. Oceanic resources beyond 200 nautical miles of the Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) belong to the open ocean; no individual country can utilise them without the concurrence of international institutions.

(d) On the basis of status of development

  • Potential resources — found in a region but not yet utilised. Example: Rajasthan and Gujarat have enormous potential for wind and solar energy, but this has not been developed properly.
  • Developed resources — surveyed, with their quantity and quality determined for utilisation. Development depends on technology and the level of feasibility.
  • Stock — materials which have the potential to satisfy human needs but humans do not yet have the appropriate technology to access them. Example: water is a compound of two gases, hydrogen and oxygen, which can be a rich source of energy — but we lack the technology to use it so.
  • Reserves — the subset of stock which can be put into use with the existing technical know-how but whose use has not begun. Example: river water can generate hydroelectric power, but is used only to a limited extent. Reserves can meet future requirements.

3. Development of Resources & Sustainable Development

  • Resources are vital for human survival and for maintaining the quality of life. It was long believed that resources are a free gift of nature, so human beings used them indiscriminately. This led to the following major problems:
  1. Depletion of resources to satisfy the greed of a few individuals.
  2. Accumulation of resources in a few hands, which, in turn, divided society into two segments — haves and have-nots (rich and poor).
  3. Indiscriminate exploitation has led to global ecological crises: global warming, ozone layer depletion, environmental pollution and land degradation.

Equitable distribution of resources has become essential for a sustained quality of life and global peace. If the present trend of resource depletion by a few countries and individuals continues, the future of our planet is in danger.

Sustainable development: development should take place without damaging the environment, and development in the present should not compromise the needs of future generations.
  • Brundtland Commission Report, 1987 — Our Common Future — introduced the concept of sustainable development and advocated it as a means for resolving the conflict between development and the environment.
  • Rio de Janeiro Earth Summit, 1992: in June 1992 more than 100 heads of state met in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil — the first International Earth Summit. The Summit was convened for addressing urgent problems of environmental protection and socio-economic development at the global level. The assembled leaders signed the Declaration on Global Climatic Change and Biological Diversity, and endorsed the Forest Principles and Agenda 21.
  • Agenda 21: a declaration signed by world leaders to achieve global sustainable development; an agenda to combat environmental damage, poverty and disease through global cooperation on common interests, mutual needs and shared responsibilities. One major objective: every local government should draw its own Local Agenda 21.
  • Gandhiji: "There is enough for everybody's need but not for anybody's greed."

4. Resource Planning

  • Planning is the widely accepted strategy for the judicious use of resources. It is particularly important in a country like India, where the availability of resources is highly diverse.
  • Regional contrasts:
    • Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh — rich in minerals and coal deposits.
    • Arunachal Pradesh — abundant water resources but lacks infrastructural development.
    • Rajasthan — well endowed with solar and wind energy but lacks water resources.
    • Ladakh (cold desert) — relatively isolated; very rich cultural heritage but deficient in water, infrastructure and some vital minerals.
  • This calls for balanced resource planning at the national, state, regional and local levels.

Resource planning is a complex process involving

  1. Identification and inventory of resources across the regions of the country — surveying, mapping, qualitative and quantitative estimation and measurement.
  2. Evolving a planning structure endowed with appropriate technology, skill and institutional set-up for implementing resource development plans.
  3. Matching the resource development plans with the overall national development plans.
  • Since Independence, India has made concerted efforts to achieve the goals of resource planning, beginning with the First Five Year Plan.
  • Availability of resources is a necessary condition for development, but not sufficient: without appropriate technology and institutional changes, development may not occur. India was well endowed with resources during the colonial period, but exploitation by the colonisers left the country poor — resources can contribute to development only with technology, skill and institutional change.
  • Conservation: the over-utilisation of resources without consideration of their replenishment may lead to socio-economic and environmental problems; hence resource conservation at various levels is important.

5. Land Resources

  • Land is a natural resource of utmost importance. It supports natural vegetation, wildlife, human life, economic activities, transport and communication systems. Because land is a finite resource, it must be used with careful planning.
  • India has land of varied relief features: mountains 30%, plains 43%, plateaus 27% of the total area.
    • Mountains — ensure the perennial flow of some rivers, provide facilities for tourism and ecological aspects.
    • Plains — provide facilities for agriculture and industry.
    • Plateaus — reserves of minerals, fossil fuels and forests.

Land utilisation

Land resources are used for the following purposes:

  1. Forests.
  2. Land not available for cultivation: (a) barren and waste land; (b) land put to non-agricultural uses — buildings, roads, factories, etc.
  3. Other uncultivated land (excluding fallow land): (a) permanent pastures and grazing land; (b) land under miscellaneous tree crops and groves (not included in net sown area); (c) culturable waste land — left uncultivated for more than 5 agricultural years.
  4. Fallow lands: (a) current fallow — left without cultivation for one or less than one agricultural year; (b) other than current fallow — left uncultivated for the past 1 to 5 agricultural years.
  5. Net sown area: the area sown with crops and orchards. The area sown more than once in an agricultural year, plus the net sown area, is known as the gross cropped area.
  • The total geographical area of India is 3.28 million sq km; land-use data are available for about 93% of it (most of the north-eastern states except Assam have not been surveyed, nor have parts of J&K under Pakistan and China).
  • Land-use pattern is determined by physical factors (topography, climate, soil types) and human factors (population density, technological capability, culture and traditions).
  • Net sown area varies by state: over 80% in Punjab and Haryana; less than 10% in Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Manipur and Andaman & Nicobar Islands.
  • Forest area: the National Forest Policy (1952) envisaged 33% of the geographical area under forests (needed to maintain ecological balance); the actual forest area is much lower. Forest land is not the same as land under actual forest cover — it also includes demarcated areas.

Land degradation and conservation measures

  • About 130 million hectares of land in India is degraded; of this roughly 28% is forest degraded land, 56% is water-eroded and the rest is affected by saline and alkaline deposits.
  • Causes: human activities such as deforestation, overgrazing, mining and quarrying.
    • Mining: in Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha, deforestation due to mining has caused severe land degradation.
    • Overgrazing: in Gujarat, Rajasthan, Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh.
    • Over-irrigation: in Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh — waterlogging leads to increase in salinity and alkalinity.
    • Mineral processing: grinding of limestone for cement, and calcite and soapstone for ceramics, generates huge quantities of dust; settling of dust retards the process of infiltration of water into the soil.
    • Industrial effluents have become a major source of land and water pollution.
  • Remedies: afforestation and proper management of grazing; planting shelter belts of plants; control of overgrazing; stabilisation of sand dunes by growing thorny bushes; proper management of waste lands; control of mining activities; proper discharge and disposal of industrial effluents and wastes after treatment.

6. Soil as a Resource

  • Soil is the most important renewable natural resource. It is the medium of plant growth and supports different types of living organisms on earth.
  • Soil formation is governed by relief, parent rock or bedrock, climate, vegetation and other forms of life, and time. Nature's forces — change in temperature, actions of running water, wind and glaciers, activities of decomposers — contribute to soil formation. Chemical and organic changes also take place. The soil's colour, texture, age, chemical and physical properties differ with these factors.
  • Humus is the decomposed organic matter in soil; it makes soil fertile.

Classification of soils of India

Alluvial, Black, Red and Yellow, Laterite, Arid and Forest soils.

(1) Alluvial soil

  • The most widely spread and important soil. The entire northern plains are made of alluvial soil, deposited by three important Himalayan river systems — the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra. It is also found in Rajasthan and Gujarat through a narrow corridor, and in the eastern coastal plains, particularly the deltas of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri.
  • Consists of various proportions of sand, silt and clay. Moving inland towards the river valleys, particles are somewhat bigger; in the upper reaches (near the break of slope) the soils are coarse — typical of piedmont plains such as Duars, Chos and Terai.
  • By age: Bangar (old alluvial) has a higher concentration of kankar nodules; Khadar (new alluvial) has more fine particles and is more fertile.
  • Rich in potash, phosphoric acid and lime; ideal for sugarcane, paddy, wheat and other cereal and pulse crops. Regions of alluvial soil are intensively cultivated and densely populated. Colour: light grey to ashy, depending on depth, texture and maturity.

(2) Black soil (Regur)

  • Black in colour; ideal for growing cotton, hence also called black cotton soil. Typical of the Deccan trap (basalt) region of the north-west Deccan plateau, made of lava flows. Covers Maharashtra, Saurashtra, Malwa, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh, extending south-east along the Godavari and Krishna valleys.
  • Made up of extremely fine clayey material; well known for moisture-holding capacity. Rich in calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime; generally poor in phosphoric content.
  • Develops deep cracks in hot weather, helping aeration (so it is 'self-ploughing'); sticky when wet and difficult to work unless tilled immediately after the first shower or before the monsoon.

(3) Red and yellow soils

  • Develop on crystalline igneous rocks in areas of low rainfall in the eastern and southern parts of the Deccan plateau. A long stretch along the piedmont zone of the Western Ghats is red loamy soil; also found in parts of Odisha, Chhattisgarh and the southern middle Ganga plain.
  • Red colour from diffusion of iron in crystalline and metamorphic rocks; looks yellow when occurring in a hydrated form.

(4) Laterite soil

  • Develops under tropical and subtropical climate with alternate wet and dry seasons; the result of intense leaching from heavy rain. 'Laterite' comes from Latin later = brick.
  • Acidic (pH < 6.0); humus content is low because high temperature destroys micro-organisms like bacteria (decomposers). Suitable for cultivation with adequate doses of manure and fertiliser.
  • Mainly in Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Madhya Pradesh and hilly areas of Odisha and Assam. After soil-conservation techniques it is useful for tea and coffee; red laterite in Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Kerala suits cashew nut.

(5) Arid soil

  • Range from red to brown; generally sandy and saline; in some areas the salt content is so high that common salt is obtained by evaporating water.
  • Dry climate and high temperature cause fast evaporation, so the soil lacks humus and moisture. Lower horizons are occupied by kankar (calcium content increases downwards), which restricts infiltration of water.
  • After proper irrigation these soils become cultivable — as in western Rajasthan.

(6) Forest soil

  • Found in hilly and mountainous areas where sufficient rain forests are available. Texture varies with the mountain environment: loamy and silty in valley sides, coarse-grained in the upper slopes.
  • In snow-bound areas of the Himalayas, these soils experience denudation, are acidic with low humus content. Soils in the lower parts of valleys (river terraces, alluvial fans) are fertile.

7. Soil Erosion and Soil Conservation

  • Soil erosion is the denudation of the soil cover and subsequent washing down. Soil formation and erosion normally go on simultaneously and there is a balance between the two. Human activities — deforestation, over-grazing, construction and mining — upset this balance; natural forces (wind, glaciers, water) also cause erosion.
  • Gully erosion: running water cuts through clayey soils and makes deep channels (gullies). The land becomes unfit for cultivation and is called bad land; in the Chambal basin such lands are called ravines.
  • Sheet erosion: water flows as a sheet over large areas down a slope and the top soil is washed away.
  • Wind erosion: wind blows loose soil off flat or sloping land.
  • Ploughing along slopes (up and down) forms channels for quick flow of water, causing soil erosion.

Soil conservation

  • Contour ploughing: ploughing along the contour lines decelerates the flow of water down the slopes.
  • Terrace cultivation: steps cut on the slopes restrict erosion; western and central Himalayas have well-developed terrace farming.
  • Strip cropping: large fields are divided into strips; strips of grass left to grow between crops break up the force of the wind.
  • Shelter belts: planting lines of trees to create shelter also checks wind movement; they have contributed significantly to stabilising sand dunes and the desert in western India.

9. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. What is a resource? When does something become a resource?
Anything in our environment that can satisfy our needs, provided it is technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable.

Q2. Distinguish between renewable and non-renewable resources.
Renewable resources can be replenished by physical, chemical or mechanical processes (solar, wind, water, forests). Non-renewable resources take millions of years to form (minerals, fossil fuels); some, like metals, are recyclable.

Q3. Explain the four types of resources by ownership.
Individual (plots, houses), community (village commons, parks), national (minerals, water, land, territorial waters up to 12 nm), international (oceans beyond 200 nm EEZ).

Q4. Differentiate between stock and reserves.
Stock: has potential but no technology to use (water as H2 + O2 energy). Reserves: a subset of stock usable with existing technology but not yet used (river water for hydropower).

Q5. What problems arose from the indiscriminate use of resources?
Depletion for the greed of a few, concentration in few hands (haves and have-nots), and ecological crises such as global warming, ozone depletion, pollution and land degradation.

Q6. What is sustainable development? What was Agenda 21?
Development without damaging the environment and without compromising future generations' needs. Agenda 21 (Rio, 1992) is a global plan to fight environmental damage, poverty and disease; each local government should draw its Local Agenda 21.

Q7. Why is resource planning necessary in India?
Resources are unevenly distributed — e.g. Jharkhand rich in minerals, Arunachal rich in water but lacks infrastructure, Rajasthan rich in solar/wind but short of water, Ladakh lacks water and infrastructure. Balanced planning is needed.

Q8. Describe the three stages of resource planning.
(i) Identification and inventory; (ii) planning structure with technology, skills and institutions; (iii) matching with national development plans.

Q9. Explain the land-use categories in India.
Forests; land not available for cultivation; other uncultivated land (pastures, tree crops, culturable waste); fallow land (current and other); net sown area. Gross cropped area = net sown + area sown more than once.

Q10. What are the causes and remedies of land degradation?
Causes: mining, overgrazing, over-irrigation, deforestation, industrial dust and effluents. Remedies: afforestation, managed grazing, shelter belts, thorny bushes for dunes, control of mining and treatment of effluents.

Q11. Describe alluvial soil.
Found in the northern plains and eastern coastal deltas, deposited by Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra; sand-silt-clay mix; rich in potash, phosphoric acid and lime; Bangar (old, kankar-rich) and Khadar (new, more fertile); good for wheat, rice, sugarcane.

Q12. Why is black soil ideal for cotton?
Clayey; retains moisture; rich in lime, potash, magnesium and calcium carbonate; self-ploughing due to cracks — typical of the Deccan lava region.

Q13. How is laterite soil formed? Where is it found?
By intense leaching under heavy rain and high temperature with alternate wet and dry seasons; acidic, low in humus; Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu; tea, coffee, cashew.

Q14. Name the types of soil erosion and the conservation methods.
Gully, sheet and wind erosion. Methods: contour ploughing, terrace cultivation, strip cropping and shelter belts.
