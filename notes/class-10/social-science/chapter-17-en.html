1. How Do We Assess Democracy's Outcomes?

People expect many things from democracy – good government, prosperity, equality, peace. So we judge it by what it actually delivers, and compare it with alternatives (dictatorship, monarchy, military rule), not with an ideal. Democracy is not meant to deliver everything; it is a form of government which creates conditions for citizens to seek these goals.

Diagram 1 – Four outcomes of democracy (animated)

Explanation: Democracy's results can be seen in four areas – political (accountable, legitimate government), economic (growth, inequality), social (accommodating diversity) and individual (dignity & freedom).

2. Accountable, Responsive and Legitimate Government

  • Accountable: Citizens have the right to choose rulers and hold them to account; free & fair elections, regular periodic change.
  • Responsive: Government pays attention to citizens' needs and demands, or else is voted out.
  • Transparent: Citizens can examine decision-making – Right to Information Act, 2005 lets them seek information on government work.
  • Legitimate: It is people's own government, so people accept it even if slow or imperfect.
  • Cost: Decision-making is slow (consultation, debate), but decisions are more acceptable and effective.
In India, participation of the poor, illiterate and SC/ST/OBC voters is high; people feel they can change governments through votes.

3. Economic Growth and Development

Diagram 2 – Growth rate: Dictatorship vs Democracy (1950–2000, animated)

Explanation: Dictatorships have a slightly higher growth rate (4.42% vs 3.95%); for poor countries almost equal. Growth depends on population size, global situation, cooperation from other countries and national priorities. So democracy can't be rejected for slightly lower growth, and its other advantages are bigger. Conclusion: We can expect democracy to be no worse than dictatorship in growth, but it can't guarantee growth.

4. Reduction of Inequality and Poverty

  • Democracy is based on political equality (one person, one vote), but economic inequality exists – a few rich own a large share of income and wealth; the poor share is tiny.
  • In many democracies the poorest are still poor; in India a large population lives in poverty and needs government support; some poor countries (e.g., Bangladesh) face the same.
  • Democracy hasn't ended poverty, but it must be pushed – through votes – to do so. Amartya Sen: no famine has occurred in a functioning democracy.

5. Accommodation of Social Diversity

Democracy produces a harmonious social life by accommodating different groups peacefully. Two conditions: ① the majority must work with the minority so that government reflects the general view; ② majority and minority are not permanent – "majority" is formed by changing combinations. Belgium succeeded in power sharing; Sri Lanka majoritarianism caused civil war. Democracy also resolves conflicts through dialogue and negotiation, though it can't remove all differences (e.g., caste).

6. Dignity and Freedom of the Citizen

Democracy promotes equality and dignity – every person has a vote and rights. Struggles for dignity by women (e.g., against dowry, for equal status) and by oppressed castes (Dalits) have been strengthened by democracy's value of equality. Citizens expect freedom of speech, faith, movement. Democracy's own success is that people question and demand more, i.e., aspire to greater rights – a sign of deeper democracy.

7. Why Is Democracy Better?

  1. Promotes equality among citizens.
  2. Enhances dignity of the individual.
  3. Improves quality of decision-making (consultation).
  4. Provides a method to resolve conflicts.
  5. Allows room to correct mistakes (can change rulers by votes).
Democracy is accepted as legitimate – people's own form – and can be improved through participation.
