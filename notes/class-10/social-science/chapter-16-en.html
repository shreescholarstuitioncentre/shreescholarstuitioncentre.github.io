1. Meaning of a Political Party

A political party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in government. They agree on policies and programmes for society, aimed at promoting the collective good.

Three components: ① Leaders ② Active members ③ Followers (supporters). Parties reflect fundamental political divisions in society – and are therefore partisan (they take a side).

2. Functions of Political Parties

  1. Contest elections – put up candidates (in India, chosen by top leaders; in USA by members & supporters).
  2. Put forward policies & programmes – voters choose among them; parties group opinions into a few broad positions.
  3. Make laws – laws are debated and passed in legislature mostly by party members following party line.
  4. Form and run governments – ruling party chooses ministers, executive.
  5. Play the role of opposition – criticise government's failures, mobilise opposition.
  6. Shape public opinion – raise issues, run movements, have lakhs of members.
  7. Provide access to government machinery and welfare schemes – people approach party workers/local leaders to get work done.

Diagram 1 – Party as link between People and Government (animated)

Explanation: Citizens vote for a party; the winning party/alliance forms the government and runs it; the others act as opposition. Parties thus connect people with government and make it accountable.

3. Necessity of Political Parties

Without parties, every candidate would be independent – no one would be responsible for the overall policy; a government could not be formed or held answerable. Representative democracy needs parties to bring representatives together for stable government, to support or restrain government, and to present policies. Nearly all countries have them – they are a political response to the needs of large societies.

5. National and State Parties

All parties must be registered with the Election Commission (EC). EC treats all equally but gives a few privileges (symbol, recognition, free time on media) to large and established parties.

  • State party: secures at least 6% of votes in State Assembly election and wins at least 2 seats.
  • National party: secures at least 6% of votes in Lok Sabha or Assembly elections in four states and wins at least 4 seats in Lok Sabha.

Examples (NCERT): INC (1885), BJP (1980), BSP (1984), CPI (1925), CPI(M) (1964), NCP (1999), AITC (1998). The list changes after elections – check the EC for the latest.

6. Challenges to Political Parties

ChallengeMeaning
1. Lack of internal democracyPower concentrated in few leaders; no membership register, organisational elections, or meetings; ordinary members have no say.
2. Dynastic successionTop posts go to family members, unfair to ordinary workers and to democracy.
3. Money and muscle powerParties raise huge funds, give tickets to rich/criminal candidates, may use force to win.
4. Meaningful choiceParties have similar policies; leaders keep shifting; voters get no real alternative.

7. How Can Parties Be Reformed?

  1. Anti-defection law – 52nd Amendment (1985), tightened by 91st (2003): a legislator who changes party loses seat.
  2. Supreme Court order – every candidate must file an affidavit on property and criminal cases.
  3. EC order – parties must hold organisational elections and file income-tax returns.
  4. Suggestions: law to regulate internal affairs; at least 1/3 tickets for women; state funding of elections.
  5. Citizens: pressure through petitions, publicity, movements; join parties – people's participation is the real remedy.
