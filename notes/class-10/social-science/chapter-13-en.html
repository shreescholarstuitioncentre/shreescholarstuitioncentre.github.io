✎ Power Sharing
Class 10 SST · Civics (Democratic Politics) · Ch 13 (NCERT · UP Board)

1. Introduction

Power sharing means that political power is not concentrated in one person or group but is divided among different organs of government, levels of government and social groups. It is the very spirit of democracy. Two stories show why it matters: Belgium (power sharing worked) and Sri Lanka (power was not shared and a civil war followed).

2. Case Study: Belgium

  • Small European country (smaller than Haryana in size) bordering France, the Netherlands, Germany and Luxembourg.
  • Population: 59% Dutch-speaking (Flemish region), 40% French-speaking (Wallonia), 1% German-speaking. In the capital Brussels: 80% French, 20% Dutch.
  • Problem: The French-speaking minority was rich and powerful; the Dutch-speaking majority got the benefits of development and education later. This caused tension in the 1950s–60s, especially in Brussels.
Belgian model (1970–1993 amendments):
  • Equal number of Dutch and French-speaking ministers in the central government.
  • Some laws need a majority from each language group.
  • State governments are not subordinate to the central government.
  • Brussels has a separate government with equal representation of both communities.
  • Community government: elected by one language community (Dutch, French, German) wherever they live; handles cultural, educational and language issues.

3. Case Study: Sri Lanka

  • Island nation south of India; independent in 1948; population about 2 crore (like Haryana).
  • Groups: Sinhala-speakers 74% (mostly Buddhist); Tamil-speakers 18% (Sri Lankan Tamils 13% in north-east, Indian-origin Tamils 5% in plantations – mostly Hindus or Muslims); about 7% Christians.
  • Majoritarianism: 1956 Act made Sinhala the only official language; jobs and university seats favoured Sinhala applicants; the state was to protect and foster Buddhism.
  • Result: Tamils felt alienated; they demanded Tamil as an official language, regional autonomy and equal opportunity. By the 1980s some groups demanded Tamil Eelam (separate state) in the north and east; civil war killed thousands and ended in 2009.

5. Forms of Power Sharing

(a) Horizontal – among organs of government

Legislature, Executive and Judiciary share power at the same level. No organ can exercise unlimited power; each checks the others. This is called separation of powers / checks and balances. (e.g., the judiciary can review laws; ministers are answerable to the legislature.)

(b) Vertical – among levels of government

Power is divided between the Union (central) government, State governments and local governments (panchayats, municipalities). This is the federal division of power; in a unitary system power rests with the centre.

🔁 Diagram: Vertical and Horizontal Power Sharing (animated)

Explanation: Left: power flows down from the Union to State to Local levels, each with its own sphere. Right: the three organs sit at one level and each keeps a check on the others, so power is never concentrated.

(c) Among social groups

Power is shared among religious and linguistic communities. Belgium's community government and India's reserved constituencies for SC/ST in assemblies and Parliament (also seats for women in local bodies) give weaker groups a voice.

(d) Among political parties, pressure groups and movements

Competition among parties gives voters a choice and prevents one party from holding power forever; coalition governments share power. Pressure groups (trade unions, traders' bodies) and movements (e.g., environment, women's) influence decision-making.

B. Short Answer

1. What is power sharing?

Division of political power among different organs, levels of government and social groups so that no one holds all the power; it is the spirit of democracy.

2. What was the main reason for tension in Belgium?

The Dutch-speaking majority was poorer and the French-speaking minority richer and powerful; the conflict was sharpest in Brussels over language and resources.

3. How did majoritarianism affect Sri Lankan Tamils?

Their language was not recognised, they lost job and education chances and felt alienated, which led to demands for autonomy and later a civil war.

4. Give two prudential reasons for power sharing.

(i) It reduces conflict among social groups. (ii) It ensures political stability.

5. What is horizontal power sharing?

Sharing of power among the legislature, executive and judiciary at the same level, so that each organ checks the others.

C. Long Answer

1. Explain the Belgian model of power sharing.

Belgium solved tension between Dutch and French speakers with constitutional changes (1970–93): equal numbers of Dutch and French ministers in the centre; special-majority laws; non-subordinate state governments; a separate, equally represented Brussels government; and community governments elected by language groups for cultural, educational and language matters. This built trust and kept the country united.

2. How did majoritarianism in Sri Lanka lead to civil conflict?

After 1948 the Sinhala majority passed the 1956 Act making Sinhala the sole official language, favoured Sinhalas in jobs and education and promoted Buddhism. Tamils felt discriminated against, their demands for recognition and autonomy were ignored, and by the 1980s groups demanded Tamil Eelam. The civil war claimed many lives and lasted until 2009.

3. Describe different forms of power sharing with examples.

(i) Horizontal: legislature, executive, judiciary check each other. (ii) Vertical: Union, state and local governments, e.g., panchayats. (iii) Among social groups: Belgian community government, reserved constituencies in India. (iv) Among parties, pressure groups, movements: coalition governments, trade unions.
