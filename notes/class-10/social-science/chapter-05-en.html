Print Culture and the Modern World

Class 10 · Social Science (History) · Chapter 5 · Detailed Notes

1. Introduction & Overview

We live surrounded by print — books, newspapers, magazines, posters, calendars. This chapter traces the history of printing: how it began in East Asia, how it transformed Europe, and how it shaped ideas, religion, politics and society in India and the modern world.

  • Print made it possible to produce many copies cheaply and quickly, so ideas could travel beyond small circles of the learned and powerful.
  • It created new readers, new debates and new fears — and gave rise to new forms of protest and nationalism.

2. The First Printed Books

China, Japan and Korea

  • The earliest print technology was hand printing, developed in China, Japan and Korea. From about 594 CE in China, books were printed by rubbing paper against the inked surface of woodblocks.
  • Thin, porous paper could be printed on one side only, so the traditional Chinese 'accordion book' was folded and stitched at the sides.
  • For a long time the imperial state was the main producer of print — textbooks for the huge civil service examination system. In the 16th century, as the number of exam candidates grew, printing also served a wider readership; merchants used print in daily life. In the late 19th century Shanghai became a hub of the new print culture, with mechanical printing replacing hand printing.
  • Japan: Buddhist missionaries from China introduced hand-printing c. 768–770 CE. The oldest Japanese book is the Buddhist Diamond Sutra (868 CE), with six sheets of text and woodcut illustrations. Visual material also printed on textiles, playing cards and paper money. In Edo (Tokyo), colour woodblock prints (ukiyo, 'pictures of the floating world') by artists like Kitagawa Utamaro depicted urban culture; libraries and bookshops were full of books on women, musical instruments, tea ceremony, cooking and famous places.

Printing comes to Europe

  • Marco Polo returned to Italy in 1295 after exploring China, bringing knowledge of woodblock printing. Italians began making books with woodblocks, and the technology spread across Europe.
  • Luxury editions were still handwritten on expensive vellum for aristocrats and rich monasteries; merchants and university students bought cheaper printed copies. Booksellers exported books and book fairs were held; scribes were increasingly employed by booksellers (sometimes 50+ for one bookseller).
  • Yet manuscript copying could not meet demand: it was expensive, laborious and slow; manuscripts were fragile, awkward to carry and read, so circulation stayed limited.

Gutenberg and the printing press

  • Johann Gutenberg (Strasbourg, Germany, 1430s) grew up on a large agricultural estate, saw wine and olive presses, and became a master goldsmith skilled in lead moulds. He adapted the olive press as the model for the printing press and used moulds to cast metal types for the alphabet.
  • By 1448 the system was perfected. The first book printed was the Bible — about 180 copies in three years, fast by the standards of the time.
  • Early printed books resembled manuscripts: metal types imitated ornamental handwriting; borders were hand-illuminated; space was left for buyers to choose decoration.
  • Between 1450 and 1550 presses spread through most of Europe; German printers travelled to start new presses. About 20 million printed copies flooded Europe in the second half of the 15th century, and about 200 million in the 16th century. This shift from hand to mechanical printing is the print revolution.

3. The Print Revolution and its Impact

A new reading public

  • Print reduced the cost of books and the labour and time needed to make them; books flooded the market and created a new culture of reading.
  • Earlier, ordinary people lived in an oral culture — they heard sacred texts read aloud, ballads recited, tales told, collectively. Now a reading public grew alongside the hearing public.
  • But literacy rates were low (till the 20th century in most of Europe). Printers therefore published popular ballads and folk tales with many pictures, which were sung and recited at gatherings in villages and taverns. Oral culture entered print and print was transmitted orally — the line between the oral and written blurred.

Religious debates and the fear of print

  • Print enabled wide circulation of ideas and a new world of debate; even those who disagreed with established authorities could print and circulate their views.
  • Many feared the effects: rebellious and irreligious thoughts might spread and the authority of 'valuable' literature would be destroyed. Religious authorities, monarchs, writers and artists criticised the new print.
  • Martin Luther (1517) wrote the Ninety-Five Theses criticising practices of the Roman Catholic Church; a printed copy was posted on a church door in Wittenberg. His writings were quickly reproduced and widely read, leading to a division in the Church and the Protestant Reformation. His New Testament translation sold 5,000 copies within weeks; he called printing God's greatest gift.
  • Print and dissent: Menocchio, a miller in 16th-century Italy, read locally available books and developed his own view of God and creation, angering the Church. He was tried and executed. Alarmed, the Roman Church imposed controls on printers and booksellers and began maintaining an Index of Prohibited Books from 1558.

The reading mania

  • In the 17th–18th centuries literacy rose across Europe as churches set up schools in villages. By the end of the 18th century, literacy was 60–80% in some parts — a 'reading mania'.
  • New popular literature: almanacs (ritual calendars), ballads, folk tales; penny chapbooks sold by pedlars ('chapmen') in England; the French Bibliothèque Bleue (cheap books in blue covers); romances and 'histories'.
  • Periodical press (from early 18th century) combined news with entertainment. Scientific texts, maps and diagrams were printed; Isaac Newton reached wider readers; writings of Thomas Paine, Voltaire and Rousseau spread ideas of reason and rationality.

4. Print Culture and the French Revolution

Historians argue print culture created the conditions for the French Revolution, through three developments:

  1. Popularised Enlightenment ideas: thinkers like Voltaire and Rousseau criticised tradition, superstition and despotism, and demanded that everything be judged by reason. Their widely read books made people see the world with questioning, critical eyes, eroding the legitimacy of the old order based on Church and state authority.
  2. New culture of dialogue and debate: values, norms and institutions were re-examined by a public aware of the power of reason; ideas of social revolution emerged.
  3. Literature mocking royalty: by the 1780s, writings, cartoons and caricatures showed the monarchy absorbed in pleasure while ordinary people suffered; this literature circulated underground and built hostility to the monarchy.
  • Louis-Sébastien Mercier believed the press was an engine of progress and enlightenment that would sweep despotism away.
  • Caution: people read many kinds of books; some did not accept these ideas, and they could reinterpret what they read. Print did not cause the Revolution on its own but helped create a climate for it.

5. The Nineteenth Century

New readers: children, women, workers

  • Children: with compulsory primary education, children became an important category of readers; textbooks multiplied. In France, a children's press was set up in 1857. The Grimm Brothers collected folk tales from peasants (published 1812) but removed anything unsuitable for children.
  • Women became both readers and writers: penny magazines and housekeeping manuals for women; novels by Jane Austen, the Brontë sisters, George Eliot shaped a new image of woman as a person with will, strength and determination.
  • Lending libraries (from the 17th century in England) became instruments for educating white-collar workers, artisans and the lower middle class. After working hours shortened from the mid-19th century, workers had time to read and write — tracts and autobiographies.

Technological innovations

  • By the late 18th century metal presses replaced wooden ones. By the mid-19th century Richard M. Hoe (New York) perfected the power-driven cylindrical press — 8,000 sheets per hour, ideal for newspapers.
  • Late 19th century: the offset press printing up to six colours. 20th century: electrically operated presses, improved paper feeding, better plates, automatic reels, photoelectric colour registration.
  • Marketing: periodicals serialised novels (shaping how novels were written); the Shilling Series in 1920s England; dust covers (book jackets) are a 20th-century idea; in the Great Depression publishers brought out cheap paperbacks.

6. India and the World of Print

Manuscripts before print

  • India had a rich tradition of handwritten manuscripts in Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian and vernacular languages, copied on palm leaves or handmade paper, sometimes beautifully illustrated, and preserved between wooden covers. They continued to be produced even in the late 19th century.
  • But manuscripts were expensive and fragile, and hard to read because of varied scripts, so they were not widely used in daily life. In pre-colonial Bengal's village schools, students often only learnt to write — teachers dictated from memory — so many became literate without reading texts.

Print comes to India

  • The printing press first came to Goa with Portuguese missionaries in the mid-16th century. Jesuits learnt Konkani and printed tracts; by 1674 about 50 books were printed in Konkani and Kanarese.
  • Catholic priests printed the first Tamil book in 1579 (Cochin) and the first Malayalam book in 1713. By 1710, Dutch Protestant missionaries had printed 32 Tamil texts.
  • The English press grew late though the East India Company imported presses from the late 17th century. From 1780 James Augustus Hickey edited the Bengal Gazette, a weekly that described itself as 'a commercial paper open to all, but influenced by none'. He published advertisements (including on slave trade) and gossip about senior Company officials. Warren Hastings persecuted him and encouraged officially sanctioned papers.
  • By the close of the 18th century several newspapers appeared; the first Indian-run paper was the weekly Bengal Gazette of Gangadhar Bhattacharya, close to Rammohun Roy.

7. Religious Reform and Public Debates

  • From the early 19th century, intense debates arose over religion as colonial society changed. Some criticised existing practices and campaigned for reform; others defended them. These debates were conducted in public and in print, and print shaped them.
  • Reformers and Hindu orthodoxy clashed over widow immolation, monotheism, Brahmanical priesthood and idolatry. Ideas were printed in the everyday spoken language. Rammohun Roy published Sambad Kaumudi (1821); the orthodoxy responded with Samachar Chandrika.
  • 1822: Persian newspapers Jam-i-Jahan Nama and Shamsul Akbar; the Gujarati Bombay Samachar.
  • Muslims (north India): the ulama feared the collapse of Muslim power and colonial interference in personal law. They used cheap lithographic presses to print Persian and Urdu translations of scriptures, religious newspapers and tracts. The Deoband Seminary (founded 1867) published thousands of fatwas on daily conduct and doctrine. Many sects competed in print.
  • Devotional literature: the Ramcharitmanas (Tulsidas) was printed in Calcutta from 1810; from the 1880s the Naval Kishore Press (Lucknow) and Shri Venkateshwar Press (Bombay) published many religious texts in vernacular translation. Printed scriptures could be carried, read anywhere, and spread by the market.
  • Print also helped link people across regions and reinforced both a shared Indian public sphere and, at times, sharper divisions between communities.

8. New Forms of Publication and Social Groups

  • New literary forms: the novel (borrowed from Europe), lyrics, short stories, essays on social and political issues. Print produced new ways of seeing society.

Women and print

  • Women's reading increased in middle-class homes; liberal fathers and husbands educated women at home and sent them to schools opened in towns after the mid-19th century. Journals carried women's writing, explained why women needed education and gave syllabi for home schooling.
  • Not all families agreed: conservative Hindus feared a literate girl would be widowed; some Muslims feared educated women would be corrupted by Urdu romances. Rebel women defied this: a girl in a conservative north-Indian Muslim family secretly learnt Urdu; Rashsundari Debi learnt to read secretly in her kitchen and wrote Amar Jiban (1876), the first full-length Bengali autobiography.
  • Kailashbashini Debi (1860s) wrote about women kept in ignorance and forced into hard domestic labour; in the 1880s Tarabai Shinde and Pandita Ramabai wrote passionately about the lives of upper-caste Hindu women, especially widows.
  • Hindi printing began seriously from the 1870s; a large part was devoted to women's education. Early 20th century: journals written by women for women (e.g. Stri Dharm Pracharak) discussed education, widowhood and the national movement. In Punjab, Ram Chaddha's Istri Dharm Vichar and Khalsa Tract Society booklets urged women to be obedient wives. In Calcutta, Battala was devoted to cheap popular books, which pedlars took to homes.

Print and the poor people

  • Very cheap small books were sold at crossroads in 19th-century Madras towns. From the early 20th century public libraries expanded access (set up by rich patrons for prestige).
  • Caste injustice was written about: Jyotirao Phule's Gulamgiri (1871); B.R. Ambedkar and E.V. Ramaswamy Naicker (Periyar) wrote powerfully on caste and were read across India.
  • Workers were overworked, but Kashibaba, a Kanpur mill worker, wrote Chhote Aur Bade Ka Sawal (1938) linking caste and class exploitation; Sudarshan Chakra published Sacchi Kavitayan (1935). By the 1930s Bangalore cotton mill workers set up libraries, following Bombay workers — sponsored by reformers to curb drinking, spread literacy and sometimes nationalism.

9. Print and Censorship

  • Before 1798 the East India Company was not much concerned with censorship; early curbs targeted Englishmen in India critical of the Company. In the 1820s the Calcutta Supreme Court regulated press freedom and the Company encouraged publications praising British rule.
  • 1835: after petitions from editors, Governor-General Bentinck agreed to revise press laws; Thomas Macaulay formulated rules that restored earlier freedoms.
  • After the revolt of 1857 attitudes changed; Englishmen demanded a clamp down on the 'native' press. As vernacular papers became assertively nationalist, the government passed the Vernacular Press Act (1878), modelled on Irish Press Laws, giving the government rights to censor vernacular reports and editorials. Reports judged seditious led to warnings; if ignored, the press could be seized and machinery confiscated.
  • Despite repression, nationalist newspapers multiplied, reporting colonial misrule and encouraging nationalist activity. Attempts to throttle criticism provoked militant protest, creating a cycle of persecution and protest.
  • When Punjab revolutionaries were deported in 1907, Bal Gangadhar Tilak wrote sympathetically in Kesari; he was imprisoned in 1908, provoking widespread protests across India.

12. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. Why did manuscripts fail to meet Europe's growing demand for books?
They were costly, slow and laborious to copy, fragile and awkward to carry and read, so circulation stayed limited.

Q2. Explain Gutenberg's contribution.
He adapted the olive press into a printing press and used lead moulds to cast metal types; by 1448 he printed the Bible (~180 copies in three years), starting the print revolution.

Q3. How did print popularise the Reformation?
Luther's Ninety-Five Theses and his New Testament translation were reproduced in huge numbers, spreading criticism of the Church and splitting it.

Q4. Why did some fear print? What was the Index of Prohibited Books?
They feared rebellious/irreligious ideas and loss of authority. After Menocchio, the Church kept an Index of banned books from 1558.

Q5. How did print create conditions for the French Revolution?
By spreading Enlightenment ideas, creating a culture of debate, and circulating literature and cartoons mocking royalty.

Q6. What were the effects of print on women's lives in India?
Women read and wrote more (Rashsundari Debi, Kailashbashini Debi, Tarabai Shinde, Pandita Ramabai); journals for women appeared — though many families opposed girls' reading.

Q7. How did print help religious reform and public debate in India?
Newspapers and tracts carried debates (Sambad Kaumudi vs Samachar Chandrika); the ulama used lithographic presses; vernacular scriptures were printed.

Q8. How did print raise issues of caste and class?
Phule's Gulamgiri, Ambedkar's and Periyar's writings, Kashibaba and Sudarshan Chakra; libraries for workers.

Q9. What was the Vernacular Press Act (1878)?
A law modelled on Irish Press Laws allowing the government to censor vernacular newspapers, warn editors and seize presses — meant to curb nationalist criticism.

Q10. How did technology change printing in the 19th century?
Power-driven cylindrical press (8,000 sheets/hour), offset (6 colours), electric presses, better paper feed and plates.
