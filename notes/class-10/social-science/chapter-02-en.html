Nationalism in India

Class 10 · Social Science (History) · Chapter 2 · Detailed Notes

1. Introduction & Overview

In Europe nationalism grew with the formation of nation-states. In the colonies, it grew through the anti-colonial movement — people discovered their unity in the struggle against British rule. Shared suffering under colonialism gave Indians a bond, but different groups understood this unity and freedom in different ways.

  • Mahatma Gandhi returned from South Africa in January 1915, where he had successfully fought racist rule using the method of satyagraha.
  • The Indian national movement became a mass movement under his leadership (1919–1934 covered here).
  • Key idea: nationalism spreads when different groups come together in a common struggle — yet each group has its own aspirations.

2. The First World War, Khilafat and Non-Cooperation

The new economic and political situation

  • The First World War created a new situation: huge defence expenditure paid through loans and higher taxes; customs duties were raised and income tax introduced.
  • Prices doubled between 1913 and 1918 — extreme hardship for the common people.
  • Forced recruitment in rural areas caused widespread anger.
  • Crop failures in 1918–19 and 1920–21 led to food shortages; the influenza epidemic killed 12–13 million people (1918–21).
  • People hoped hardships would end after the war — they did not.

The idea of Satyagraha

  • Satyagraha = a method of mass agitation based on the power of truth and the need to search for truth. It emphasised non-violence: a satyagrahi can win a battle without physical force, by appealing to the conscience of the oppressor, not by seeking revenge.
  • Champaran, Bihar (1916–17): peasants forced by planters to grow indigo on part of their land.
  • Kheda, Gujarat (1917): crop failure and plague — peasants could not pay revenue and demanded relaxation.
  • Ahmedabad (1918): satyagraha among cotton mill workers.

The Rowlatt Act (1919)

  • Hurriedly passed by the Imperial Legislative Council despite unanimous Indian opposition. It gave the government enormous powers to repress political activities and allowed detention without trial for two years.
  • Gandhi called for a nationwide hartal on 6 April 1919 — rallies, strikes, shops closed. The British responded with repression; local leaders were picked up in Amritsar and Gandhi was barred from entering Delhi. On 10 April police fired on a peaceful procession in Amritsar; martial law was imposed with General Dyer in command.
  • Jallianwala Bagh, 13 April 1919 (Baisakhi): a large crowd gathered in the enclosed ground; Dyer blocked the exit and ordered firing, killing hundreds. His aim, he said, was to produce a moral effect — to create terror. The brutality in Punjab (crawling lanes, floggings) shocked the country and street violence spread; Gandhi called off the movement.

The Khilafat Movement

  • The defeated Ottoman Emperor of Turkey was the spiritual head (Khalifa) of the Islamic world; rumours spread of a harsh peace treaty.
  • A Khilafat Committee was formed in Bombay (March 1919); leaders like Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali began mass action. Gandhi saw it as a chance to bring Hindus and Muslims together in a united national movement.
  • At the Calcutta special session (Sept 1920) Congress backed Non-Cooperation in support of Khilafat and for swaraj; the Nagpur session (Dec 1920) approved the programme after a compromise.

3. The Non-Cooperation Movement (1921–22)

  • Gandhi's Hind Swaraj (1909) argued that British rule survived only because of Indian cooperation; if Indians refused to cooperate, it would collapse within a year.
  • Stages: first surrender of titles and boycott of civil services, army, police, courts, legislative councils, schools and foreign goods; if the government used repression, a full civil disobedience campaign would follow.
  • Within Congress, many were reluctant to boycott council elections (Nov 1920); a compromise was reached at Nagpur. The movement began in January 1921.

In the towns

  • Middle class took the lead: students left government schools, lawyers gave up practice, headmasters and teachers resigned. Councils were boycotted except in Madras, where the Justice Party (non-Brahmans) saw council entry as the only way to gain power.
  • Foreign goods were boycotted, liquor shops picketed, foreign cloth burnt in bonfires. Imports of foreign cloth halved (Rs 102 crore → Rs 57 crore, 1921–22). Indian textile mills and handlooms boomed.
  • The movement slowed: khadi was costlier than mill cloth and poor people could not afford it; alternative Indian institutions were slow to come up so students and teachers drifted back.

Rebellion in the countryside

  • Awadh (UP): peasants led by Baba Ramchandra (earlier indentured labourer in Fiji) fought talukdars and landlords — high rents, begar, no security of tenure. Demands: reduction of revenue, abolition of begar, boycott of oppressive landlords (nai–dhobi bandh). The Oudh Kisan Sabha was set up in Oct 1920 (Jawaharlal Nehru, Baba Ramchandra and others); within a month 300 branches existed. Congress was unhappy at the militant turn; peasants looted bazaars and clashed with landlords, believing Gandhi had sanctioned this.
  • Gudem Hills (Andhra): hill tribes rose against closure of forests (ban on grazing, firewood, fruit gathering). Alluri Sitaram Raju led a guerrilla war; he was captured and executed in 1924.
  • Plantation workers in Assam: under the Inland Emigration Act (1859) they could not leave gardens without permission. Hearing of Non-Cooperation they left plantations, believing "Gandhi Raj" was coming — but were stranded at railway stations and brutally beaten by police.
Why Chauri Chaura matters: In February 1922 at Chauri Chaura (Gorakhpur, UP) a peaceful demonstration turned violent when police fired; the mob torched a police station and several policemen died. Fearing violence, Gandhi withdrew the Non-Cooperation Movement, convinced satyagrahis needed more training. Younger leaders like Nehru and Subhas Bose, however, pressed for more radical mass struggle.

4. Towards Civil Disobedience

  • Within Congress some (C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru) formed the Swaraj Party to argue for return to council politics from within; younger leaders (Nehru, Subhas Chandra Bose) wanted full independence and mass agitation.
  • Factors in the late 1920s: worldwide economic depression (agricultural prices collapsed, peasants hit; exports fell) and the political impact of the Simon Commission.
  • Simon Commission (1927–28): set up to look into the working of the constitution, but had no Indian member. It was greeted with "Go back Simon" slogans; all parties including the Muslim League joined the protest.
  • Oct 1929: Viceroy Lord Irwin announced a vague offer of "dominion status" — not enough.
  • Lahore Congress (Dec 1929), President Jawaharlal Nehru: formal demand of Purna Swaraj (complete independence). 26 January 1930 was observed as Independence Day.

5. The Salt March and the Civil Disobedience Movement

  • Gandhi found in salt a powerful symbol uniting the nation: consumed by rich and poor alike and one of the most essential items, yet the government held a monopoly and levied a tax on it. He sent Viceroy Irwin a letter with eleven demands (including reduction of land revenue and salt tax) and warned of a campaign if they were not met.
  • Dandi March: Gandhi started on 12 March 1930 from Sabarmati Ashram with 78 volunteers; walked about 240 miles in 24 days and on 6 April reached Dandi, where he ceremonially broke the salt law by making salt from sea-water.
  • This began the Civil Disobedience Movement. Unlike Non-Cooperation, people now broke colonial laws as well as refusing cooperation: salt was manufactured, foreign cloth boycotted, liquor shops picketed.
  • Peasants refused to pay revenue and chaukidari tax, village officials resigned, and in forest areas people violated forest laws.
  • Government arrested Congress leaders; Abdul Ghaffar Khan was arrested (Peshawar), Gandhi was arrested; industrial workers in Sholapur attacked police posts. Police firing killed many; the British took harsh action.
  • Gandhi–Irwin Pact (5 March 1931): Gandhi agreed to attend the Second Round Table Conference (London); political prisoners were released. The first RTC (Nov 1930) had been boycotted by Congress.
  • The 2nd RTC (Dec 1931) broke down; Gandhi returned disappointed; Civil Disobedience was resumed, but by 1934 it lost momentum.

6. How Participants Saw the Movement

  • Rich peasants (Patidars of Gujarat, Jats of UP): producers of commercial crops, badly hit by the trade depression; demanded reduction of revenue; became enthusiastic supporters. Disappointed when the movement was called off in 1931 without revenue being reduced, many did not join the 1932 relaunch.
  • Poor peasants: wanted unpaid rent to landlords remitted; Congress was unwilling to support "no rent" campaigns (fearing offence to landlords), so relations were uncertain.
  • Business classes: wanted protection against imports and a favourable rupee–sterling ratio; formed Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress (1920) and FICCI (1927) (leaders: Purshottamdas Thakurdas, G.D. Birla). They gave financial help and refused to buy/sell imported goods, but after the movement worried about prolonged disruption and the spread of socialism.
  • Industrial workers: mostly stayed away (except Nagpur), as industrialists were close to Congress; some adopted boycott ideas as part of their own movements. Congress was reluctant to include workers' demands.
  • Women: large-scale participation — marched, made salt, picketed foreign cloth and liquor shops; many went to jail. Yet, Gandhi believed their duty was as mothers and wives; Congress long hesitated to give them any position of authority.

7. The Limits of Civil Disobedience

  • Dalits (Depressed Classes): Congress had ignored them for long for fear of offending the sanatanis (orthodox high castes). Gandhi declared untouchability the greatest evil, called them Harijan (children of God), organised temple entry and access to wells, tanks and roads, and cleaned toilets himself.
  • Many Dalit leaders wanted a different political solution: Dr B.R. Ambedkar organised the Depressed Classes Association (1930) and demanded separate electorates at the 2nd RTC. Gandhi opposed it, fearing it would slow their integration; he began a fast unto death.
  • Poona Pact (Sept 1932): Ambedkar accepted Gandhi's position — Depressed Classes got reserved seats in provincial and central legislative councils, but were to be elected by the general electorate. The Dalits' participation in CDM remained limited, as the Dalit organisations were wary of Congress.
  • Muslims: After the Non-Cooperation–Khilafat movement, a large section of Muslims felt alienated. From the mid-1920s Congress was seen as closely linked to the Hindu Mahasabha; communal clashes and riots occurred in many cities.
  • Efforts for a settlement failed: in 1927 Jinnah offered reserved seats in the Central Assembly and proportional representation in Muslim-majority Bengal and Punjab — hopes ended when M.R. Jayakar (Hindu Mahasabha) strongly opposed it. Many Muslim leaders feared minority status and cultural dominance, so participation of Muslims was low in CDM.

8. The Sense of Collective Belonging

  • Nationalism spreads when people feel they all belong to the same nation — a sense of unity created by identity-symbols, images, folklore, songs and history.
  • Figure of Bharat Mata: first created by Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay who wrote "Vande Mataram" (1870s) as a hymn to the motherland, later included in his novel Anandamath. Abanindranath Tagore painted the famous image of Bharat Mata (1905) as a calm, composed, divine and spiritual figure; later versions were painted by various artists. Devotion to this mother figure became a sign of nationalism.
  • Revival of folklore: Nationalists recorded folk tales sung by bards; Rabindranath Tagore collected ballads, nursery rhymes and myths and led the movement for folk revival; in Madras Natesa Sastri published The Folklore of Southern India.
  • Icons and symbols: during the Swadeshi movement in Bengal a tricolour flag (red, green, yellow) with eight lotuses (eight provinces) and a crescent moon (Hindus and Muslims) was designed. By 1921 Gandhi designed the Swaraj flag — a tricolour (red, green, white) with a spinning wheel in the centre representing the ideal of self-help. Carrying it during marches became an act of defiance.
  • Reinterpretation of history: Indians wrote about the glorious ancient past — art, architecture, science, mathematics, religion, law, philosophy, crafts and trade — to counter the British view that Indians were backward and incapable of governing themselves.
  • These efforts were not uniform: images and symbols with a Hindu flavour could alienate other communities.

11. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. Why did Gandhi call for a hartal against the Rowlatt Act?
The Act allowed detention without trial for two years and curbed civil liberties, despite unanimous opposition from Indian members. Gandhi called a nationwide hartal on 6 April 1919 as a non-violent protest.

Q2. Describe the Jallianwala Bagh incident.
On 13 April 1919 (Baisakhi) a large crowd gathered in the enclosed Bagh at Amritsar. General Dyer blocked the exit and fired without warning, killing hundreds, to create terror and a 'moral effect'. The incident outraged the country.

Q3. Why did Gandhi withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement?
After the violence at Chauri Chaura (Feb 1922) he felt satyagrahis needed training in non-violence; the movement was called off.

Q4. How did different social groups join Non-Cooperation?
Middle class in towns (students, lawyers, boycott); peasants in Awadh (Kisan Sabha), tribals in Gudem hills, plantation workers in Assam — each with their own meaning of swaraj.

Q5. Why did Gandhi choose salt as a symbol?
Salt is essential for everyone, and the colonial government taxed it and held a monopoly; attacking the salt law united all classes.

Q6. Why was the participation of industrial workers/Muslims/Dalits limited in CDM?
Workers: Congress hesitant on their demands, industrialists close to Congress. Muslims: alienation after Khilafat ended, communal clashes. Dalits: Congress long neglected them; Dalit leaders wanted separate electorates.

Q7. What was the Poona Pact?
The Sept 1932 agreement between Gandhi and Ambedkar giving Depressed Classes reserved seats in legislative councils but elected by the general electorate, ending the demand for separate electorates.

Q8. How did culture help create a sense of collective belonging?
Through Bharat Mata, Vande Mataram, folklore revival, the Swaraj tricolour with spinning wheel, and glorified accounts of India's past.

Q9. Why did the Simon Commission face protest?
It had no Indian member and was to decide India's constitutional future; people shouted "Go back Simon".

Q10. Differences between Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience?
NCM: refusal to cooperate (boycott). CDM: openly breaking colonial laws (salt law, forest laws) and refusing to pay taxes, in addition to boycott.

12. Quick Revision

  • Satyagraha: Champaran, Kheda, Ahmedabad → Rowlatt & J. Bagh (1919).
  • NCM + Khilafat (1920–22): boycott; Awadh, Gudem, Assam; Chauri Chaura ends it.
  • 1927–29: Simon → Lahore Purna Swaraj → 26 Jan 1930.
  • Salt March: 12 Mar–6 Apr 1930, 78 volunteers, 240 miles. Gandhi–Irwin Pact 1931.
  • Groups: rich peasants, business — yes; workers, Dalits, Muslims — limited.
  • Poona Pact 1932. Symbols: Bharat Mata, Vande Mataram, Swaraj flag.
