Ch. 19: Sectors of the Indian Economy

1. Economic Activities and Sectors

Economic activities are activities that produce goods and services and generate income. They are grouped into sectors in three ways: by nature (primary / secondary / tertiary), by employment conditions (organised / unorganised) and by ownership (public / private).

2. Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Sectors

SectorMeaningExamples
Primary (agriculture & related)Goods produced by exploiting natural resourcesFarming, dairy, fishing, forestry, mining
Secondary (industrial)Natural products converted into other forms by manufacturingCotton → yarn → cloth; sugarcane → sugar; steel, bricks
Tertiary (service)Activities that help primary & secondary sectors; produce services, not goodsTransport, storage, communication, banking, trade, teaching, doctors, IT

Diagram 1 – Interdependence of the three sectors (animated)

Explanation: A farmer grows cotton (primary); a mill converts it into cloth (secondary); trucks, banks and shops carry, finance and sell it (tertiary). Without services, the products would not reach people, so the sectors are interdependent.

3. Comparing the Three Sectors – GDP

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) = value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a given year. Only final goods are counted (intermediate goods are already included in their price) – so double counting is avoided. E.g., value of wheat + flour counted separately would count wheat twice; only biscuits (final) counted.

Historical change: Primary sector was biggest in early times; with development of methods of farming & the Industrial Revolution, secondary rose; later, in developed countries, tertiary became the biggest in production. In India too, from 1950 to now, the tertiary sector share in GDP became largest, while primary share fell.

Diagram 2 – Share in GDP vs share in employment in India (animated, approximate)

Explanation: Agriculture gives under 20% of GDP but employs about half the workforce – so too many people depend on a sector producing little. This is the main problem of India's economy.

4. Why Has the Tertiary Sector Grown?

  1. Development raises need for basic services – hospitals, schools, transport, post, banks.
  2. Growth of primary & secondary sectors creates demand for transport, trade, storage.
  3. Rising income → more demand for tourism, shopping, private hospitals, restaurants, professional training.
  4. New services based on information & communication technology (ICT) – IT, BPO, internet, mobile.
But service sector in India has two types: some highly skilled & well-paid (IT, finance); many low-paid and unprotected (small shopkeepers, repair persons, drivers, casual workers). Only a small part is growing fast.

5. Where Are the Problems? – Underemployment & Employment Generation

Underemployment (disguised unemployment): Seems all are working, but more people are engaged than needed – even if some leave, production will not fall. E.g., a farm of 1 hectare needs 2 workers, but 5 family members work. The extra 3 are disguised unemployed.

Ways to generate employment (primary sector):
  • Better irrigation (small canals, wells, check dams) – more crops, more work.
  • Agro-based industries (food processing, dal mills), cold storage, marketing, transport – non-farm employment.
  • Setting up small industries in semi-rural areas; improving education & health to create jobs (teachers, doctors, nurses).
  • Government schemes: MGNREGA 2005 guarantees 100 days of employment per year to every rural household that demands work (if not provided, allowance is paid) – Right to Work (check latest rules).

6. Organised and Unorganised Sectors

Organised sectorUnorganised sector
Registered with government; follows rules (Factories Act, Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Gratuity Act)Small, scattered units largely outside government control
Regular jobs, fixed hoursJobs irregular, low-paid, no fixed hours
Paid leave, PF, gratuity, medical benefits, pensionNo such benefits; no job security; can be asked to leave anytime
Workers have unions, safety, protectionExploitation, long hours, no overtime pay
E.g., government departments, big companiesE.g., casual labourers, street vendors, small farmers, domestic workers
About 90% of Indian workers are in the unorganised sector (NCERT), but they contribute large part of output. Need to protect them: support small farmers with seeds, credit, marketing, storage; help small units; ensure minimum wages, regulations; reduce exploitation in urban casual work. Organised sector workers also may face casualisation – contract jobs.

7. Public and Private Sectors

Public sectorPrivate sector
Government owns most assets and provides services. Aim: public welfare, not just profit. E.g., Indian Railways, Post offices, ONGC, SAILOwnership of assets & delivery of services is with individuals/companies. Aim: profit. E.g., TISCO, Reliance, Bajaj

Why public sector is needed: Provides things society needs but private sector won't unless sure of profit – roads, bridges, railways, ports, electricity, irrigation dams, health, education; supports agriculture (MSP, procurement, food at fair price via PDS); helps small units; ensures defence, social welfare; takes care of poor – housing, drinking water, basic needs.
