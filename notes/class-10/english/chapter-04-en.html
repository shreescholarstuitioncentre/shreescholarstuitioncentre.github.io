2. Summary in Simple Words

On 10 May 1994, in the amphitheatre of the Union Buildings in Pretoria, Nelson Mandela takes the oath as the first black President of South Africa. Thousands of people have gathered, including dignitaries from 140 countries, for what Mandela calls the largest gathering of international leaders ever seen on South African soil. It is the inauguration of the first democratic, non-racial government of the country. Thabo Mbeki and F.W. de Klerk are sworn in as deputy presidents.

The ceremony has powerful moments. Jets, helicopters and troop carriers fly over in perfect formation. The military and police generals, who not long ago would have refused to salute him and might even have arrested him, now salute and pledge their loyalty. Two national anthems are sung: the one that stood for black freedom and the old one of white South Africa. Mandela sees this as proof that a new, united nation is being born.

Mandela recalls the long history of racial division. After the Anglo-Boer War, white South Africans made peace among themselves and built a system that kept the black majority down, which grew into the cruel apartheid system. Now, he says, out of that long human tragedy must come a society that all humanity can be proud of. He feels humble that this day belongs to the many brave men and women who sacrificed before him, and he remembers great freedom fighters such as Oliver Tambo, Walter Sisulu, Chief Luthuli, Yusuf Dadoo, Bram Fischer and Robert Sobukwe.

He says South Africa is rich in gold and diamonds, but its greatest wealth is its people, who are finer than the purest diamonds. He explains courage: it is not the absence of fear but the triumph over it. Brave people feel afraid but conquer that fear.

Finally Mandela reflects on freedom. As a boy he thought freedom meant running in the fields and swimming in the stream. As a young man it meant earning a living, marrying and raising a family without obstruction. Later he understood that not only he, but all his people, were not free. That hunger for freedom for all drove his struggle. He believes both the oppressor and the oppressed must be freed, because the one who takes away another's freedom is a prisoner of hatred. The day is only the first step: to be free is not just to throw off chains but to live in a way that respects and strengthens the freedom of others.

5. Part-wise Explanation

Part 1 — The historic ceremony

The inauguration takes place in the amphitheatre of the Union Buildings on a bright day. Dignitaries from 140 countries and thousands of citizens watch the first non-racial, democratic government begin. Mandela is sworn in as President, with Mbeki and de Klerk as deputy presidents.

Key point: the event marks the end of white minority rule and the start of democracy.

Part 2 — Show of loyalty and the two anthems

Jets fly overhead, and generals who once opposed him salute. Two anthems are sung, one for each community that had been divided. Mandela sees this as a miracle of change.

Key point: the army and police now serve the elected government; the anthems show unity of black and white South Africans.

Part 3 — Looking back at apartheid

Mandela remembers how, after the Anglo-Boer War, whites built a system that excluded blacks, and this grew into apartheid. He says this long human disaster must now give birth to a society all humanity can be proud of.

Key point: contrast between the dark past and the hopeful present.

Part 4 — Tribute to heroes and the idea of courage

Mandela thanks the brave men and women who came before him, names several leaders, and says South Africa's real wealth is its people. He defines courage as conquering fear.

Key point: success is shared; courage means acting despite fear.

Part 5 — Meaning of freedom

His idea of freedom grows from childhood freedoms to the wish to live without restriction, and then to the freedom of his whole community. This realisation made him join the struggle.

Key point: personal freedom becomes collective freedom.

Part 6 — Freedom for oppressor and oppressed

A person who oppresses is a prisoner of hatred and narrow-mindedness; both are robbed of humanity. Mandela says that political freedom is only the first step and the real test is to respect and strengthen others' freedom.

Key point: freedom comes with responsibility; reconciliation, not revenge.

6. Character Sketches

Nelson Mandela

A courageous, humble and visionary leader. He suffered for years for the freedom of his people but speaks without bitterness. He believes in equality and forgiveness and credits the sacrifices of countless others for the day's victory. He defines courage as overcoming fear, and he understands that freedom also means responsibility towards others.

Thabo Mbeki and F.W. de Klerk

The two deputy presidents. Their presence beside Mandela shows cooperation between earlier opponents and the spirit of national unity.

The military and police generals

Earlier representatives of the old system, they salute the new President and pledge loyalty, proving that democracy is being accepted.

Freedom fighters and ordinary people

Brave men and women who fought and sacrificed so that the day could come. Mandela calls his people the greatest wealth of South Africa.

7. Themes and Message

Freedom and equality

True freedom means the end of oppression for everyone, not only for some.

Courage

Courage is not the absence of fear but the triumph over it.

Reconciliation

Mandela does not seek revenge; the old rulers and the oppressed unite to build a new country.

Unity in diversity

The rainbow nation, with two anthems and dignitaries from all over the world, stands for togetherness.

Responsibility

Freedom is only a beginning; we must live in a way that respects the freedom of others.

Moral

Hatred imprisons the hater too. Only courage, forgiveness and respect for others can create a just society.

11. Long Answer Questions

Q1. Describe the inauguration ceremony and its importance.
The ceremony took place at the Union Buildings in Pretoria on 10 May 1994 with dignitaries from 140 countries. Mandela took the oath as the first black President, with Mbeki and de Klerk as deputy presidents. Jets flew past, and generals who once opposed him saluted. Two anthems were sung. The event ended white minority rule and marked the birth of a democratic, multi-racial nation.
Q2. How did Mandela's idea of freedom change over time?
As a boy, freedom meant running in the fields and swimming in the stream. As a young man it meant earning an honest living and raising a family. Later he realised that his people were not free, and that his own freedom was meaningless without theirs. This made him a freedom fighter. Finally he understood that both oppressor and oppressed must be freed and that freedom means respecting others.
Q3. What does Mandela say about courage and the future?
Mandela says courage is not the absence of fear but conquering it, and he saw it in ordinary men and women. He says South Africa's real wealth is its people. He adds that the inauguration is only the first step on a longer road, and that the real test is to live in a way that respects and strengthens the freedom of everyone.
