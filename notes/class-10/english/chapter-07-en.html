🦁 How to Tell Wild Animals 🐅

Class 10 · English (First Flight) · Poem – Chapter 7 · By Carolyn Wells

1. About the Poet

Carolyn Wells (1862–1942) was an American author, born in Rahway, New Jersey. She is best known for her humorous and nonsense verse, limericks and light poems, and she also wrote many detective novels. She loved wordplay, jokes and comic twists, and this poem is a good example of her playful style.

  • Type of writing: humorous / nonsense verse
  • Style: a pretend "guidebook" tone with a comic surprise in each stanza
  • Poem's aim: to entertain and to give a light warning about wild animals

4. Stanza-wise Detailed Explanation

The poem pretends to be a guidebook that teaches us how to recognise wild animals. Each stanza speaks to the reader as "you" and describes a situation in which you meet an animal in the wild. The clue given for identification is always connected to the animal's most famous behaviour. The humour lies in the fact that, in most stanzas, you can identify the animal only after it has attacked you.

1. The Asian Lion

The poet says that if you ever go to the jungles of the east and a large, yellowish-brown (tawny) beast comes towards you, you should wait for him to roar. When he roars loudly at you, just as you are dying, you will know that it is the Asian lion. The joke is that you recognise him only when it is too late. The lion's loud roar is his famous feature.

2. The Bengal Tiger

If you meet a noble wild animal whose body has black stripes on a yellow background, the poet says to observe whether he eats you. This simple test, he says, helps you identify the Bengal tiger. Again the humour is dark: the test works only if you survive the meeting, which of course you will not. The poet also makes the tiger's stripes the main visible clue.

3. The Leopard

If you see an animal whose skin is covered with spots, you will recognise him as a leopard as soon as he leaps at you. The poet adds that it is useless to cry out in pain, because he will only leap on you again and again. The leopard's agile leaping and his spotted coat are the two clues.

4. The Bear

If a creature seizes you and holds you tightly in a crushing grip, the poet says, you may be sure it is a bear. The humour here is that the "bear hug" is so tight that you cannot do anything. The word "hug" also plays on its friendly meaning, which makes the scene comic yet frightening.

5. The Hyena

If you meet a creature that seems to find everything amusing, laughing loudly as you come near, it is a hyena. The poet suggests that you should not laugh back or take the sound as a friendly sign. The hyena's cry sounds like human laughter, and this is the clue given.

6. The Crocodile

If you meet a creature that appears to weep, shedding tears as it comes closer, it is a crocodile. The idea behind this is the common expression "crocodile tears", meaning false or insincere tears. The poet warns that you should not be taken in by this fake sorrow, because the crocodile is only waiting to eat you.

🌿 Key insight: In every stanza, the clue (roar, stripes, spots, hug, laugh, tears) is real and well known, but the poet makes it funny by showing that it helps you only after the animal has attacked.

6. Central Idea (Board Answer)

Central Idea / Theme: How to Tell Wild Animals by Carolyn Wells is a humorous poem that pretends to be a guide for recognising dangerous wild animals. For each animal (the Asian lion, Bengal tiger, leopard, bear, hyena and crocodile), the poet gives a well-known clue connected with its behaviour or appearance: the roar, the stripes, the spots and leaping, the crushing hug, the laugh and the false tears. The comic twist is that you can identify each animal only after it has attacked you, which is of no help at all. Through irony, exaggeration and light-hearted wit, the poet entertains the reader while giving a humorous warning that wild animals are dangerous and should be avoided.

Key Points to Remember

  • The poem is humorous; its serious subject (danger) is treated in a light and comic tone.
  • Each stanza gives a clue: roar (lion), stripes and eating (tiger), spots and leaping (leopard), hug (bear), laugh (hyena), tears (crocodile).
  • The humour is based on irony: the "guide" works only when it is too late.
  • The poem conveys, in a playful way, that we should keep away from wild animals.

10. Important Questions & Answers

A. Short Answer Questions (30–40 words)

Q1. What is the poem about?

The poem is a funny guide to identifying wild animals. For each animal it gives a clue linked to its behaviour, but the clue helps only after the animal has attacked. The poem is humorous but warns us to avoid wild animals.

Q2. How can you recognise the Asian lion according to the poet?

The Asian lion is a large tawny beast that roars loudly at you. The poet jokes that you will know it is the lion only when it roars at you, just as you are dying.

Q3. How is the Bengal tiger identified?

The Bengal tiger has black stripes on a yellow background. The poet humorously says the sure test is to see whether he eats you. This shows that the identification comes too late.

Q4. What is the clue to identify a leopard?

A leopard has a spotted hide and leaps at you. The poet says that crying in pain is useless because he will leap on you again and again.

Q5. Why is the clue for the crocodile funny?

The crocodile seems to weep as it approaches, but its tears are false. This refers to the idiom "crocodile tears". The humour lies in the sad-looking animal that is actually about to eat you.

Q6. What kind of humour is used in the poem?

The poem uses irony, exaggeration and dark humour. The guide, instead of protecting us, tells us the animal's name only after an attack, which makes a dangerous situation comic.

B. Long Answer Questions (100–120 words)

Q1. Explain the central idea of the poem "How to Tell Wild Animals".

Carolyn Wells' poem is a humorous guide to identifying wild animals. It deals with six animals: the Asian lion, Bengal tiger, leopard, bear, hyena and crocodile. For each, the poet gives a clue like the roar, stripes, spots and leaps, the crushing hug, the laugh and the false tears. The joke is that the clue is useful only after the animal has attacked, so the guide is of no real help. Through irony and exaggeration the poet entertains us, while also hinting that wild animals are dangerous and should be kept at a distance. The poem teaches us to be careful, in a light-hearted way.

Q2. How does the poet create humour in the poem?

The poet creates humour mainly through irony and exaggeration. A guide is expected to help us recognise animals in advance, but here the clue shows up only when the animal attacks: the lion roars as you are dying, the tiger eats you, the leopard leaps on you, the bear crushes you in a hug. Familiar expressions such as "bear hug" and "crocodile tears" are used in a witty way, and the hyena's laugh is shown as mocking. The poet also speaks directly to the reader in a calm, serious guidebook tone while describing danger, and this contrast adds to the fun.

Q3. What message does the poem convey?

Though the poem is a joke, it has a serious message in disguise: wild animals are dangerous, and we should not go near them or try to test them. The poet makes us laugh at the idea of "identifying" an animal while being attacked, but the real lesson is that it is better to avoid contact altogether. The poem also shows how animals have their own typical behaviour: the lion's roar, the tiger's stripes, the hyena's laugh. By presenting these facts in a funny way, the poem entertains and informs at the same time.
