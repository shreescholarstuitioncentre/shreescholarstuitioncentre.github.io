💍 The Proposal

1. About the Author

Anton Pavlovich Chekhov (1860–1904) was a famous Russian playwright and short-story writer, born in Taganrog, Russia. He trained and worked as a doctor, and often said that medicine was his wife and literature his mistress. He is regarded as one of the greatest masters of the short story and of modern drama.

  • Famous plays: The Seagull, Uncle Vanya, Three Sisters, The Cherry Orchard.
  • The Proposal (written about 1889) is one of his short, funny one-act plays, called a farce.
  • He wrote about ordinary people, their small weaknesses and the humour in daily life.

4. Scene-wise Summary and Explanation

Part 1 — Lomov Arrives to Propose

Ivan Vassilevitch Lomov, a well-to-do but nervous landowner, arrives at his neighbour Chubukov's house dressed in formal evening clothes. Chubukov is surprised at his smart dress and asks why he is so dressed up. Lomov says he has come to ask for the hand of Chubukov's daughter, Natalya. Chubukov is delighted. He hugs Lomov and says he has always loved him like a son, and he assures him that Natalya will certainly say yes. He goes out to call Natalya.

Part 2 — Lomov's Nervousness and His "Illnesses"

Left alone, Lomov becomes very anxious. He complains of palpitations, a twitching eyebrow, a numb leg, giddiness and sleeplessness. He says that at the age of thirty-five he must marry and settle down to a calm, regular life, but he is afraid of making a decision. The audience quickly sees that he is a hypochondriac, a person who is always worrying about his health. This makes the scene funny.

Part 3 — The First Quarrel: Oxen Meadows

Natalya enters and greets Lomov politely. Lomov tries to approach the subject of marriage slowly, but instead mentions the Oxen Meadows, a piece of land that lies between their properties. He says it belongs to him; Natalya insists it belongs to her family. Both become angry, trade arguments about whose grandfather or family had the right, and the polite conversation becomes a loud, bitter fight. Lomov's heart starts to flutter, and Natalya mocks him.

Part 4 — Chubukov Joins In; Lomov Collapses

Chubukov returns, hears the quarrel and takes his daughter's side. He becomes just as heated and calls Lomov names. Lomov, upset and unwell, shouts back, staggers about, and finally collapses on a chair as if dead. Natalya and her father think he has died and panic. They call for water. Natalya weeps and realises how silly the fight has been.

Part 5 — Revival and the Engagement

Lomov slowly recovers. Natalya now learns from her father that Lomov had actually come to propose to her. She is horrified and begs her father to bring him back. When Lomov returns, Natalya admits that the meadows can be considered his. Chubukov quickly joins their hands, blesses them and tells them to kiss. The engagement is settled.

Part 6 — The Quarrel Starts Again

No sooner is the engagement made than the couple begins to quarrel again, this time over their hunting dogs, Lomov's Guess and Natalya's Squeezer. Each insists that his or her dog is better. Chubukov, exhausted, tries to calm them, and, hoping the engagement will hold, calls for champagne. The play ends on a humorous note, suggesting that these two will go on quarrelling all their lives.

💡 Key insight: The play shows that people who are ready to marry cannot even manage a polite conversation without squabbling. The proposal is more like a deal between neighbours than a romance.

6. Character Sketches

Ivan Vassilevitch Lomov

  • Age and status: Thirty-five, a wealthy landowner and neighbour of Chubukov.
  • Hypochondriac: He constantly complains of palpitations, numb legs, twitching eyebrow and sleeplessness, although he is large and healthy.
  • Nervous and timid: He is afraid to propose and keeps delaying the subject.
  • Quarrelsome and proud: He is quick to argue about the meadows and his dog and will not give in.
  • Practical: He wants to marry because it is time to settle down, not out of great romance.

Natalya Stepanovna

  • Age and status: Twenty-five, Chubukov's daughter, hospitable and polite at first.
  • Quick-tempered and stubborn: She flares up at once when Lomov claims the meadows and will not admit she may be wrong.
  • Practical about marriage: When she learns he came to propose, she immediately begs her father to call him back.
  • Competitive: She defends her dog Squeezer fiercely.

Stepan Stepanovitch Chubukov

  • Status: A landowner, Natalya's father and Lomov's neighbour.
  • Warm and hospitable at first: He welcomes Lomov and is delighted by the proposal.
  • Hot-tempered: When Lomov claims the meadows, he takes his daughter's side and insults Lomov.
  • Eager to marry off his daughter: He is quick to push the two together as soon as he can.
  • Changeable: He moves from friendliness to rage to friendliness again within minutes.

8. Central Idea (Board Answer)

Central Idea / Theme: The Proposal by Anton Chekhov is a one-act farce that makes fun of human pettiness, pride and quarrelsomeness. Lomov, a nervous and health-obsessed landowner, comes to propose to Natalya, but within minutes the three characters are quarrelling bitterly over the ownership of the Oxen Meadows, and later over whose hunting dog is better. The proposal itself is treated almost like a business deal between neighbours, not a matter of love. Their sudden changes of mood, from friendliness to rage and back again, and Lomov's imaginary illnesses create constant humour. The play satirises the landowning class, who care more about property and pride than about relationships, and shows how trivial disputes and ego can spoil even the most important moments in life.

Key Points to Remember

  • It is a farce: exaggerated characters, sudden changes, comic situations.
  • Both quarrels (land and dogs) are about pride and possession, not real issues.
  • Marriage is shown as a practical arrangement: each person has a reason to marry besides love.
  • The ending shows the couple will go on quarrelling, which is the final joke.

12. Important Questions & Answers

A. Short Answer Questions (30–40 words)

Q1. Why does Lomov visit Chubukov's house?

Lomov visits Chubukov in formal dress to ask for the hand of his daughter Natalya in marriage. He wants to settle down, as he is thirty-five. Chubukov is pleased and goes to call Natalya.

Q2. Why is Chubukov so happy about the proposal?

Chubukov is delighted because Lomov is a wealthy neighbour whom he likes, and he is eager to see his daughter married. He assures Lomov that Natalya will accept without hesitation.

Q3. What is the first quarrel between Lomov and Natalya about?

The first quarrel is about the Oxen Meadows, a piece of land. Lomov claims it belongs to him, while Natalya insists it belongs to her family. Neither will give in, and the argument grows heated.

Q4. What are Lomov's health complaints?

Lomov complains of palpitations, a twitching eyebrow, a numb leg, giddiness and sleeplessness. Although he is healthy, he exaggerates these problems, which shows that he is a hypochondriac and adds to the humour.

Q5. What happens when Lomov collapses?

After a violent quarrel, Lomov falls into a chair as if dead. Natalya and Chubukov panic and call for water. Natalya weeps, realising her mistake, and when she learns of the proposal, she begs her father to bring him back.

Q6. How does the play end?

The engagement is settled, but the couple immediately begin quarrelling again over their hunting dogs, Guess and Squeezer. Chubukov calls for champagne to celebrate. The ending suggests that the two will keep arguing.

B. Long Answer Questions (100–120 words)

Q1. Explain the central idea of "The Proposal".

"The Proposal" is a one-act farce by Anton Chekhov that makes fun of human pettiness and pride. Lomov comes to propose to Natalya, but he and she start quarrelling over the ownership of the Oxen Meadows, and later about whose dog is better. Chubukov, her father, takes sides and joins the quarrel. The proposal is treated more like a practical arrangement than a romance. Lomov's imaginary illnesses and the characters' rapid mood changes create constant humour. The play satirises landowners who care more about property and prestige than about relationships and shows how trivial quarrels can spoil serious moments in life.

Q2. Write a character sketch of Lomov.

Lomov is a thirty-five-year-old wealthy landowner and neighbour of Chubukov. He is a hypochondriac who constantly complains of palpitations, a twitching eyebrow, numb legs and sleeplessness, though he is large and healthy. He is nervous and timid, finding it hard to say that he has come to propose. He is also proud and quarrelsome, as he quickly argues about the meadows and his dog. His decision to marry is practical, since he wants to settle down. Through him, Chekhov shows how a person's pride and anxieties can make even a simple matter comic.

Q3. Why can "The Proposal" be called a farce? Give reasons.

"The Proposal" is a farce because it relies on exaggerated characters and silly situations for humour. A proposal of marriage, which should be romantic, turns into a loud quarrel about land and dogs. Lomov's illnesses are absurdly exaggerated, and he collapses as if dead. The characters change moods very quickly, from friendliness to rage and back again. The action takes place in a single room, at great speed, with short, sharp dialogue. Even after the engagement, the couple go on quarrelling. All this makes the play a comic satire on human weakness.

Q4. What does the play tell us about marriage and human nature?

The play shows that, among the landed class of that time, marriage was often seen as a practical arrangement. Lomov wants a wife to settle down, Chubukov wants his daughter married and Natalya wants a husband, and none of them speaks of love. It also shows that people are easily provoked by small matters such as land or the quality of dogs, and ego makes them forget more important things. The play suggests that if we let pride and petty quarrels rule us, we may spoil our closest relationships, but it presents this idea in a light, amusing way.
