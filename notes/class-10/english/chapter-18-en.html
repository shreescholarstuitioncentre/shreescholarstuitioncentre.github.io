Class 10 · English (First Flight) · Poem · Chapter 18 — Detailed Notes · by W.B. Yeats

1. Poem at a Glance

PoemFor Anne Gregory (First Flight, Poetry, Chapter 18)
PoetWilliam Butler (W.B.) Yeats (1865–1939), an Irish poet and dramatist; winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature (1923)
Published1933
FormA short poem of three stanzas, each of six lines, written as a conversation between an older speaker and a young girl, Anne Gregory
Rhyme schemeThe second, fourth and sixth lines of each stanza rhyme (pattern abcbdb); the last line is repeated as a refrain
SpeakersThe poet (an older, wise speaker) and Anne Gregory, a young woman with beautiful yellow hair
ThemeSuperficial love for outward beauty versus true love for the inner self; unconditional love
Tone and moodGentle, thoughtful, teasing and slightly philosophical

2. Central Idea (as expected in the CBSE Board Exam)

'For Anne Gregory' is a poem about the difference between loving someone for their outward beauty and loving them for what they truly are. The speaker tells Anne that young men will always love her for her beautiful yellow hair and never for herself alone. Anne replies that she can dye her hair brown, black or carrot-coloured, so that young men may love her for herself. The speaker then says that an old religious man has found a text proving that only God can love her for herself alone.

The poet wants to say that human beings are often attracted by looks, which are only superficial, and true, unconditional love, which sees the real self beyond appearance, is rare and belongs to God. So we should not judge people by their looks, and we should value the inner self, character and soul.

  • Outer beauty attracts people: young men are drawn to Anne's yellow hair, not to her real self.
  • Anne wants to be loved for herself: she is ready to change her hair colour to test this.
  • Only God loves unconditionally: He alone can love us for what we truly are.
  • Appearance is superficial: we should look beyond looks to the inner self.

3. Summary in Simple Words

In the first stanza the speaker says that no young man, however deeply in despair he may be over the 'great honey-coloured ramparts' of hair at Anne's ear, will ever love her for herself alone and not for her yellow hair. Her golden hair, which is compared to walls or defences, becomes the very thing that people notice and love first.

In the second stanza Anne answers. She says that it is easy to change this: she can buy a hair-dye and give her hair any colour she likes, such as brown, black or even carrot-coloured, so that young men who are in despair may then love her for herself alone and not for her yellow hair. She wants to prove that she can be loved for herself.

In the third stanza the speaker says that only the night before he heard an old religious man declare that he had found a text (a passage in scripture) which proves that only God, and no human being, could love Anne for herself alone and not for her yellow hair. This is the final answer and message of the poem.

In this way the poem turns a light, teasing conversation about hair into a thoughtful comment on beauty, love and the soul.

    6. Stanza-wise Explanation

    Stanza 1 — The speaker's statement

    The speaker tells Anne that no young man, however deeply he may be in despair over her thick golden hair (the 'ramparts at your ear'), will ever love her for herself alone and not for her yellow hair. The hair is compared to walls, which suggests that it is so striking that it hides the real Anne.

    Key point: people judge by outer beauty; appearance hides the real person.

    Stanza 2 — Anne's reply

    Anne answers that she can easily change this. She can get a hair-dye and give her hair a different colour, brown, black or even carrot, so that young men may love her for herself and not for her yellow hair. Her reply is playful and confident, but also shows a real wish to be loved for who she is.

    Key point: Anne's wish to be loved for her real self; the test of changing outer appearance.

    Stanza 3 — The old man's text

    The speaker says that only the night before he had heard an old religious man declare that he had found a text which proves that only God could love Anne for herself alone and not for her yellow hair. The speaker ends with this final reply, which Anne's hair-dye cannot overcome.

    Key point: only God's love is unconditional; human love is shaped by looks.

    7. Character Sketches and Symbols

    Anne Gregory

    A young, beautiful and spirited woman who wants to be loved for herself, not for her looks. She is confident and playful, as seen in her willingness to dye her hair. She stands for everyone who wishes to be valued for their true self.

    The speaker (the poet)

    An older, wise and gently teasing speaker who knows that people are attracted by outer beauty. He ends with a religious thought, showing a deeper, spiritual view of love.

    The young men

    They represent the ordinary human tendency to love physical beauty more than the person within.

    The old religious man

    A voice of spiritual wisdom, whose text says that only God can love us for ourselves alone.

    Yellow hair

    A symbol of outer, physical beauty, the thing people notice first.

    God

    A symbol of pure, unconditional love, which sees and loves the inner self.

    8. Themes and Message

    Outer beauty vs inner self

    People are drawn to looks, but the real person lies within.

    The desire to be loved for oneself

    Everyone wishes to be loved for their true self, not for their appearance.

    Unconditional love

    Only God's love is free of conditions and sees the inner self.

    Superficiality of society

    Society values outward attractions more than character.

    Moral

    Do not judge people by looks; value character and soul, and learn to love unconditionally.

    12. Long Answer Questions (5–6 marks)

    Q1. What is the central idea of the poem 'For Anne Gregory'?
    The poem shows that people often love others for their outward beauty and not for their true selves. The speaker tells Anne that young men will love her for her yellow hair, not for herself. Anne says she can dye her hair so that she may be loved for herself. The speaker then recalls an old religious man's text that only God can love her for herself alone. So the poem tells us that real, unconditional love is rare and belongs to God, and that we should look beyond appearance to the inner self.
    Q2. How does the poet contrast outer beauty and inner self in the poem?
    Anne's yellow hair stands for outer beauty, which people notice first and which makes young men fall in despair. But Anne wants to be loved for her inner self. The poet shows that human love stops at looks, and that only God's love can reach the inner self. By repeating the refrain 'for yourself alone and not your yellow hair', the poet stresses this contrast between the surface and the soul.
    Q3. Explain the structure and devices of the poem.
    The poem has three stanzas of six lines each, written as a conversation. The speaker speaks in the first and third stanzas, and Anne in the second. The second, fourth and sixth lines of each stanza rhyme, and the last line acts as a refrain. The poet uses metaphor (honey-coloured ramparts), imagery of hair colours, repetition, contrast and symbolism. The tone moves from teasing to serious and spiritual.
