Part A – Factors of Production: Land, Labour, Capital

1. Production and its Factors

Production = creating utility in goods/services. The inputs needed are called factors of production – land, labour, capital, organisation and enterprise.

Easy example: to grow wheat – field (land), farmer (labour), seeds and tools (capital), planning (organisation), and the risk-taker (entrepreneur) ✔

2. Factors and Their Rewards (animated diagram)

📝 Explanation: The five factors together produce goods (orange dots). The income earned is shared among them as rewards (green dots) – this is distribution.

3. Land

In economics, land = all free gifts of nature – soil, water, forests, minerals, climate etc.

  • Gift of nature (free)
  • Fixed supply
  • Immovable
  • Differences in fertility
  • Passive factor
  • Reward = Rent

4. Labour

Labour = any physical or mental effort done to earn income.

  • Cannot be separated from the worker
  • Perishable (today's work cannot be stored)
  • Low mobility
  • Active factor
  • Types – skilled/unskilled, productive/unproductive
  • Reward = Wages
Efficiency of labour rises with: education, health, training, good working conditions and fair wages.

5. Capital

Capital = the part of wealth used to produce more wealth; it is man-made.

  • Man-made factor
  • Mobile
  • Wears out (depreciation)
  • Created by saving (capital formation = saving → investment)
  • Reward = Interest

📝 Explanation: Fixed capital is used again and again, circulating capital is used up at one go, and education and skill make up human capital.

Part B – Organization and Enterprise

6. Organisation

Organisation = gathering land, labour and capital, combining them in the right proportion and managing production. It is done by the organiser/manager. Reward: salary.

7. Enterprise

Enterprise = bearing the risk of production. It is done by the entrepreneur. Reward: profit (or loss).

  • Deciding to start a business
  • Bearing risk and uncertainty
  • Innovation (new methods)
  • Coordination and decision-making

Part C – Consumer's Surplus

8. Meaning (Marshall)

Consumer's surplus = the difference between the price a consumer is willing to pay for a good and the price he actually pays.

Formula: Consumer's surplus = Price willing to pay (total utility) − Actual price × units

9. Basis and Diagram (animated)

It is based on the law of diminishing marginal utility – as units increase, the utility (and willingness to pay) of each falls.

📝 Explanation: The orange dot moves along the red demand curve. All units sell at one market price. The pulsing green triangle is the gain the consumer gets by paying less than he was willing to pay; the blue rectangle is the amount actually paid.

10. Example (market price ₹6)

11. Importance and Limitations

  • Estimates the consumer's gain
  • Helps in taxation and pricing policy
  • Limit: utility is hard to measure
  • Hard to measure for necessities
  • Tastes differ from person to person
⚡ Quick Revision: Factors = land (rent), labour (wages), capital (interest), organisation (salary), enterprise (profit) | Consumer's surplus = willing price − actual price | Based on diminishing marginal utility
