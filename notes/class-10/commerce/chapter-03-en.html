1. Meaning of Bank Reconciliation Statement (BRS)

DefinitionA Bank Reconciliation Statement is a statement prepared on a particular date to find the reasons for difference between the balance as per Cash Book (bank column) and the balance as per Pass Book (bank statement) – and to show that both agree after adjustments.
  • Cash Book = our own record of bank transactions.
  • Pass Book = the bank's record of our account (copy of our account in the bank's books).
  • Both record the same transactions, but at different times or with some extra items – so balances differ.
📌 BRS is only a statement, not a ledger account. It does not change the books by itself.
Animated – two books, two balances, one BRS

🔍 Explanation

  • Left book – the firm's Cash Book shows ₹25,000.
  • Right book – the bank's Pass Book shows ₹25,300 for the same account.
  • Red arrows – the same transactions are written in both books but not on the same day.
  • BRS – the bridge that explains why 25,000 ≠ 25,300.

2. Cash Book vs Pass Book

Withdrawal is recorded onCredit sideDebit side
Favourable balanceDebit balanceCredit balance

⭐ Why reversed? For the bank, our deposit is a liability (they owe us) → credit. For us, it is an asset → debit.

3. Why do the balances differ?

The causes fall into 3 groups:

Animated tree – causes of difference

🔍 Explanation

  • Timing differences – the business has recorded the entry, but the bank has not yet (cheques take days to clear).
  • Only in Pass Book – the bank has done something and told us later (interest, charges, direct payments by standing instruction, direct deposits by customers, a deposited cheque returned unpaid).
  • Errors – by the business or by the bank. Errors of the business are corrected in the Cash Book; errors of the bank are corrected by informing the bank.

4. Objectives & Importance

  • To detect errors and frauds in Cash Book or Pass Book.
  • To know the correct bank balance (true cash position).
  • To find uncleared cheques and take follow-up action.
  • To check unrecorded items – interest, charges, direct deposits – and enter them in Cash Book.
  • To prevent misuse of money by staff (regular checking).
  • To reduce the risk of overdrawing the account.

5. Cheque timing differences (most common reason)

A cheque is not paid by the bank on the day it is written. Watch what happens to a cheque issued by the firm:

Animated – the journey of a cheque issued by the firm

🔍 Explanation

  1. Day 1 – the firm writes the cheque and immediately credits the Cash Book (balance falls).
  2. Day 3 – the party receives it.
  3. Day 5 – the party deposits it in its bank.
  4. Day 8 – our bank pays and debits the Pass Book (balance falls).

If the balance date (red dotted line) falls between Day 1 and Day 8, the Cash Book shows a lower balance than the Pass Book. The reverse happens for a cheque deposited by the firm that the bank has not yet collected.

RememberCheques issued, not presented = "paid by us, not yet paid by bank". Cheques deposited, not collected = "received by us, not yet credited by bank".

6. Add or Deduct? – The rules

The golden idea: Start with one balance and walk to the other.

A. Starting with Cash Book balance (favourable / debit) → reach Pass Book balance

Animated – Add (+) or Deduct (−) when starting from Cash Book

B. Starting with Pass Book balance (credit / favourable) → reach Cash Book balance

Just reverse every sign of the diagram above:

Interest / dividend credited by bank+ Add– Less
Direct deposit by customer+ Add– Less
Bank charges– Less+ Add
Direct payment by bank– Less+ Add

Overdraft (unfavourable) balance? Reverse again – see Section 9.
TrickAsk: "Has the bank already made our balance bigger than the Cash Book shows?" If yes (interest, deposits, uncleared cheques we issued) → ADD while moving from Cash Book to Pass Book.

Question

On 31st March, the Cash Book shows a bank balance of ₹25,000 (Dr). Find the Pass Book balance, given:

  • Cheques issued but not yet presented: ₹3,000
  • Cheques deposited but not yet collected: ₹5,000
  • Bank charges debited by bank only: ₹200
  • Interest credited by bank only: ₹500
  • Direct deposit by customer (not in Cash Book): ₹2,000
Animated BRS (starting with Cash Book)

🔍 Label explanation

  1. Starting balance – ₹25,000 Dr in Cash Book.
  2. +3,000 – we reduced Cash Book, bank has not yet → Pass Book is higher.
  3. –5,000 – we increased Cash Book, bank has not yet credited → Pass Book is lower.
  4. –200 – bank charged us; Cash Book not yet told.
  5. +500 – bank gave interest; Cash Book not yet told.
  6. +2,000 – customer paid directly to bank.
  7. Result – Pass Book balance ₹25,300.
Reverse check: 25,300 + 5,000 + 200 – 3,000 – 500 – 2,000 = 25,000 ✔
After BRSItems that were only in the Pass Book (bank charges, interest, direct deposit) must now be entered in the Cash Book to bring it up-to-date (this gives the "Adjusted Cash Book" balance: 25,000 – 200 + 500 + 2,000 = 27,300). Timing items (uncleared cheques) need no entry.

9. Overdraft case (unfavourable balance)

RememberOverdraft = bank has lent money to us → balance is Credit in Cash Book and Debit in Pass Book. All signs get reversed compared to the favourable case.
Starting with overdraft as per Cash BookTreatment
Cheque issued, not presented– Less
Cheque deposited, not collected+ Add
Interest / dividend / direct deposit credited– Less
Bank charges / direct payment / dishonour+ Add

Mini-example: Overdraft per Cash Book ₹10,000; cheque issued not presented ₹2,000; cheque deposited not collected ₹3,000.
Overdraft per Pass Book = 10,000 – 2,000 + 3,000 = ₹11,000.

10. Formulas, tricks & common mistakes

  • Pass Book balance (from Cash Book) = CB balance + cheques issued not presented + interest/direct deposits – cheques deposited not collected – charges/direct payments
  • Cash Book balance (from Pass Book) = reverse all signs
  • Timing items → no Cash Book entry; bank-only items → entry in Cash Book

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that Cash Book and Pass Book are mirror images (Dr ↔ Cr).
  • Not noticing whether the starting balance is overdraft.
  • Adding bank charges instead of deducting (from Cash Book).
  • Mixing "issued" with "deposited" cheques.
  • Ignoring an item already recorded correctly in both books (it needs no adjustment).

11. Q&A and exam tips

Q1. What is a BRS?
A: A statement that reconciles the Cash Book (bank column) balance with the Pass Book balance on a given date.
Q2. Why is BRS needed?
A: To find the reasons for difference, detect errors and know the true bank balance.
Q3. Is a BRS part of the books of account?
A: No – it is only a statement, prepared outside the books.
Q4. A cheque deposited is dishonoured. How does it affect Cash Book method?
A: Bank has debited our account; Cash Book still shows deposit → Less from Cash Book balance.
Q5. Why is the Cash Book debit balance a credit balance in the Pass Book?
A: Because our asset is the bank's liability.

✅ Exam tips

  • Write the heading: "BRS as on [date]".
  • State clearly: favourable balance or overdraft.
  • Show every item with Add / Less label.
  • Always verify with the reverse method.
  • Do not adjust items already recorded in both books.
