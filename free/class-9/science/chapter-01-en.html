Chapter: Motion

1Distance-Time Graph

Explanation

A distance-time graph shows how far an object has travelled as time passes. Time is always taken on the X-axis and distance on the Y-axis. The shape of the line tells the whole story of the motion, and the slope (steepness) of the line gives the speed.

Working Process (how to read it)

  1. Straight slanting line (A): equal distance in equal time, so the speed is constant (uniform motion).
  2. Horizontal line (B): distance does not change as time passes, so the object is at rest.
  3. Curved line (C): the slope keeps changing, so the speed is changing (non-uniform motion). A curve that bends upward means the speed is increasing.
  4. To find speed: pick two points on the line, find Δd (rise) and Δt (run), then divide: speed = Δd / Δt.
Remember: Steeper line = greater speed. A distance-time graph can never slope downward, because distance never decreases.

2Velocity-Time Graph

Explanation

A velocity-time graph shows how the velocity of an object changes with time. Its two special properties are: the slope gives the acceleration, and the area under the graph gives the displacement of the object.

Working Process

  1. Horizontal line (B): velocity stays constant, so acceleration = 0.
  2. Straight slanting line (A): velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time, so acceleration is uniform. Slope = (v − u) / t.
  3. Curved line (C): the slope changes, so acceleration is not uniform.
  4. To find displacement, calculate the area between the line and the time axis (triangle = ½ × base × height, rectangle = length × breadth).
Formulas: a = (v − u)/t  |  s = area under v-t graph  |  A line sloping downward means retardation (deceleration).

3Uniform Motion Graph

Explanation

In uniform motion an object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time, however small the intervals are. The speed does not change, so the distance-time graph is a perfect straight line passing through the origin.

Working Process

  1. Mark the position of the car after every 1 second: 0, 10, 20, 30, 40 m.
  2. The gaps between consecutive positions are all equal (10 m).
  3. Plot (time, distance) points on a graph. They all fall on one straight line.
  4. Speed = distance / time = 10 / 1 = 10 m/s at every instant.
Remember: Uniform motion means constant speed in a straight line, so acceleration = 0.

4Non-Uniform Motion Graph

Explanation

In non-uniform motion the object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time. The speed is increasing, decreasing or both, so the distance-time graph is a curve. Most real journeys (a bus in city traffic, a falling ball) are non-uniform.

Working Process

  1. Record the distance covered in each second. Here the gaps are 5, 10, 15 m (increasing).
  2. Since the gaps are different, the speed is different in each second.
  3. Plot the points. They lie on a curve, not on a straight line.
  4. Use average speed = total distance / total time = 45 / 4 = 11.25 m/s for the whole journey.
Remember: Curve bending upward = speeding up. Curve flattening = slowing down.

5Distance and Displacement

Explanation

Distance is the total length of the path actually travelled; it is a scalar quantity. Displacement is the shortest straight-line distance from the starting point to the final point, together with the direction; it is a vector quantity. If an object returns to its starting point, its displacement is zero even though the distance is not.

Working Process

  1. Start at A and walk 3 m east to C, then 4 m north to B.
  2. Distance = AC + CB = 3 + 4 = 7 m.
  3. Join A to B with a straight line. Triangle ACB is right-angled at C, so AB = √(3² + 4²) = 5 m (Pythagoras).
  4. Displacement = 5 m in the north-east direction.

Chapter: Force and Laws of Motion

6Balanced Forces

Explanation

Forces are balanced when two or more forces acting on a body are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction and act along the same line. Their resultant (net force) is zero. Balanced forces do not change the state of rest or of uniform motion of a body, although they can change its shape (for example, squeezing a sponge).

Working Process

  1. Two people pull the box with equal forces of 10 N in opposite directions.
  2. Choose one direction as positive: net force = +10 − 10 = 0 N.
  3. Vertically, the table pushes up (N) with exactly the box's weight, so those also cancel.
  4. With zero net force, a box at rest stays at rest; a box already moving keeps its velocity.
Result: Net force = 0 → no acceleration → no change in speed or direction.

7Unbalanced Forces

Explanation

When the forces acting on a body are not balanced, the net force is not zero. An unbalanced force can start a motion, stop a moving body, change its speed, change its direction or change its shape. The body accelerates in the direction of the larger force.

Working Process

  1. Pushing force 20 N acts to the right; friction 8 N acts to the left.
  2. Resultant force = larger − smaller = 20 − 8 = 12 N (to the right).
  3. This 12 N net force gives the box an acceleration a = F/m.
  4. The box speeds up in the direction of the net force until the forces become balanced again.
If the forces act in the same direction, add them. If they act in opposite directions, subtract them.

8Newton's First Law of Motion (Inertia)

Explanation

Newton's first law: A body continues in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless an external unbalanced force acts on it. This tendency to resist change is called inertia. The greater the mass of a body, the greater its inertia, so mass is the measure of inertia.

Working Process

  1. (a) Inertia of rest: the card is flicked away sharply. The coin has no horizontal force on it, so it stays where it was and then falls into the glass because of gravity.
  2. (b) Inertia of motion: the bus and the passenger move together. When the bus brakes, friction stops the feet, but the upper body tries to continue moving, so the passenger leans forward.
  3. Other examples: dust falls out of a beaten carpet; passengers lean backward when a bus suddenly starts.
Types of inertia: inertia of rest, inertia of motion and inertia of direction. A heavier object is harder to start, stop or turn.

9Newton's Second Law of Motion (F = ma)

Explanation

Newton's second law: The rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the applied unbalanced force and takes place in the direction of the force. Momentum p = mass × velocity. From this, F = ma: for a given mass, a bigger force gives a bigger acceleration, and for a given force, a bigger mass gives a smaller acceleration.

Working Process

  1. Apply the same 10 N force to a 1 kg and a 5 kg trolley.
  2. Use a = F/m for each: 10 m/s² and 2 m/s².
  3. In the same time, distance s = ½ a t² is 5 times larger for the lighter trolley.
  4. To double the acceleration of any trolley, either double the force or halve the mass.
Unit of force: 1 newton is the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s². Momentum unit: kg m/s.

10Newton's Third Law of Motion

Explanation

Newton's third law: To every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The two forces always act on two different bodies, so they do not cancel each other. A heavier gun recoils at a lower speed than a light bullet moves forward, because their momenta are equal and opposite.

Working Process

  1. Balloon: the balloon squeezes air out backward (action). The escaping air pushes the balloon forward (reaction) and it slides along the string.
  2. Gun: the burning powder pushes the bullet forward (action). The bullet pushes the gun backward (reaction), giving the recoil.
  3. Conservation of momentum: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = 0, so recoil velocity v = −(m×v of bullet)/(mass of gun).
  4. Same idea: a swimmer pushes water back and the water pushes the swimmer forward; a rocket throws hot gases down and rises up.

11Action and Reaction Forces

Explanation

Forces always occur in pairs. If body A exerts a force (action) on body B, then B exerts an equal force in the opposite direction (reaction) on A. They appear and disappear at the same time. Because they act on different bodies, we cannot add them to get zero.

Working Process

  1. Skater A pushes skater B with force F to the right (action on B).
  2. At the same instant B pushes A with force F to the left (reaction on A).
  3. Accelerations: aB = F/60 and aA = F/40, so A moves away faster.
  4. Momentum: 40 × vA = 60 × vB, so the speeds are in the ratio 3 : 2.
Everyday action-reaction pairs: walking (foot pushes the ground back, ground pushes you forward), rowing a boat, jumping off a boat (the boat moves back), a hammer hitting a nail.
Chapter: Gravitation

12Universal Law of Gravitation

Explanation

Newton's universal law of gravitation: Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres. The force acts along the line joining the two bodies, and the two forces form an action-reaction pair.

Working Process

  1. Take two masses m₁ and m₂ a distance r apart.
  2. Force is proportional to m₁m₂, so doubling one mass doubles the force.
  3. Force is proportional to 1/r²: doubling the distance makes the force 1/4, tripling it makes it 1/9.
  4. Combining both: F = G m₁m₂/r². G is very small, so gravity is noticeable only when at least one mass is huge, like the Earth.
Uses: it binds us to the Earth, keeps the Moon in orbit around the Earth, planets around the Sun, and causes tides on Earth.

13Free Fall of an Object

Explanation

When an object falls towards the Earth only under the influence of gravity (air resistance neglected), it is in free fall. All objects, heavy or light, fall with the same acceleration g = 9.8 m/s² (about 10 m/s²) near the Earth's surface. In a vacuum a feather and a hammer reach the ground together.

Working Process

  1. Released from rest, the ball has u = 0. Gravity gives it a downward acceleration g.
  2. After 1 s: v = 9.8 m/s and s = ½(9.8)(1)² = 4.9 m.
  3. After 2 s: v = 19.6 m/s and s = 19.6 m. After 3 s: v = 29.4 m/s and s = 44.1 m.
  4. The gaps between ghost images grow, which shows the speed keeps increasing at a constant rate.
Why g? g = GM/R², where M and R are the mass and radius of the Earth. It does not depend on the mass of the falling body.

14Mass and Weight

Explanation

Mass is the amount of matter in a body. It is constant, a scalar, and measured in kilogram (kg) with a beam balance. Weight is the force with which the Earth (or any planet) attracts a body. It is a vector pointing to the centre of the Earth, measured in newton (N) with a spring balance, and it changes with the value of g.

Working Process

  1. Hang the 10 kg block from a spring balance on Earth. Gravity pulls it with W = mg = 98 N, so the spring stretches to the 98 N mark.
  2. Take the same block to the Moon. Since gmoon = 1.63 m/s² (about 1/6 of g), the pull is only 16.3 N.
  3. The amount of matter has not changed, so the mass stays 10 kg.
  4. In deep space (g → 0) the weight is nearly zero but the mass is still 10 kg.

15Thrust and Pressure

Explanation

Thrust is the force acting perpendicular (at right angles) to a surface. Pressure is thrust per unit area: P = F/A. Its SI unit is the pascal (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m². For a given thrust, the smaller the area the greater the pressure.

Working Process

  1. The brick's weight (100 N) presses on the sponge. This perpendicular force is the thrust.
  2. Lying flat, the contact area is large (0.5 m²), so the pressure is 200 Pa and the dent is shallow.
  3. Standing on end, the area is only 0.05 m², so the same thrust gives 2000 Pa, ten times more, and the dent is deeper.
  4. Application: needles, nails and knives have sharp points/edges to give large pressure; tractors and camels have wide feet and skiers use skis to reduce pressure.
Formula: Pressure = Thrust / Area  →  P = F / A (Pa). Liquid pressure: P = h ρ g.

16Buoyancy (Upthrust)

Explanation

Any object placed in a fluid (liquid or gas) experiences an upward force called buoyant force or upthrust. It arises because pressure increases with depth: the pressure pushing up on the bottom face is greater than the pressure pushing down on the top face.

Archimedes' principle: When a body is partly or fully immersed in a fluid, it experiences an upward force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by it.

Working Process

  1. Hang the block from a spring balance in air: reading 5 N (its true weight).
  2. Lower it into water slowly. The reading falls to 3 N.
  3. The decrease (5 − 3 = 2 N) is the upthrust, and it equals the weight of the water pushed aside by the block.
  4. Upthrust depends on the volume immersed and the density of the liquid (U = V ρ g), not on the material of the block.
Applications: ships, submarines, hot-air balloons, lactometer and hydrometer.

17Floating and Sinking of Objects

Explanation

Whether an object floats or sinks depends on the comparison between its weight (acting downward) and the upthrust (acting upward). Equivalently, it depends on the object's density compared with the density of the liquid.

Working Process

  1. Density of object < density of liquid: the object displaces liquid weighing as much as itself before it is fully under, so U = W and it floats (cork, wood, ice on water).
  2. Density of object = density of liquid: U = W even when fully inside, so it stays suspended at any depth.
  3. Density of object > density of liquid: even when fully immersed, U < W, so the net force is downward and the object sinks (iron nail, stone).
  4. A ship floats although steel is dense because its hollow shape displaces a very large volume of water, producing a large upthrust.
Law of floatation: a floating body displaces a weight of liquid equal to its own weight.
Chapter: Work and Energy

18Work Done by a Force

Explanation

In science, work is done only when a force acts on a body and the body is displaced in the direction (or against the direction, or partly along the direction) of the force. Work is a scalar quantity. Its SI unit is the joule (J): 1 J is the work done when a force of 1 N displaces a body by 1 m in its own direction.

Working Process

  1. Conditions for work: (i) a force must act, (ii) the body must move through some displacement.
  2. (a) Force and displacement in the same direction: W = F s = positive work (pushing a trolley).
  3. (b) Force at 90° to displacement: W = F s cos 90° = 0 (a person carrying a bag horizontally does zero work on the bag against gravity).
  4. (c) Force opposite to displacement: W = −F s = negative work (friction on a sliding box, brakes on a car).
Zero work cases: pushing a wall (s = 0), a satellite in a circular orbit (force ⊥ motion), a coolie walking horizontally with a load on his head.

19Energy Transformation

Explanation

Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be changed from one form to another. This is the law of conservation of energy: the total energy before and after a transformation is the same. In the process some energy is usually converted into less useful heat, but it is never lost.

Working Process (hydroelectric plant)

  1. Water stored behind the dam at height h has potential energy (mgh).
  2. When released into the pipe, it falls, and the potential energy becomes kinetic energy (½mv²).
  3. The fast-moving water strikes the turbine blades and makes them spin: mechanical energy.
  4. The turbine turns the coil of the generator inside a magnetic field, producing electrical energy.
  5. A bulb converts electrical energy into light and heat.
Other examples: Sun → solar cell (light → electrical); electric fan (electrical → mechanical); microphone (sound → electrical); loudspeaker (electrical → sound); burning candle (chemical → heat + light).

20Potential Energy and Kinetic Energy

Explanation

Potential energy (PE) is the energy a body has because of its position or configuration (height above ground, stretched spring). Near the Earth, PE = mgh. Kinetic energy (KE) is the energy a body has because of its motion: KE = ½mv². Both are measured in joule. A body that has either of them can do work.

Working Process (pendulum)

  1. At the extreme position A the bob is at the greatest height and momentarily at rest: PE is maximum, KE = 0.
  2. As it swings down, height decreases, so PE falls; speed rises, so KE rises.
  3. At the lowest point O: PE = 0 and KE is maximum.
  4. Going up the other side, KE changes back into PE. Neglecting air resistance, PE + KE is the same at every point.
  5. Check: from h = 5 m, mgh = 2 × 9.8 × 5 = 98 J, and at the bottom ½mv² = 98 J gives v = √98 ≈ 9.9 m/s.
Work-energy theorem: the work done on a body equals its change in kinetic energy. Power = work / time (unit watt, 1 W = 1 J/s).
Chapter: Sound

21Longitudinal Wave

Explanation

In a longitudinal wave the particles of the medium vibrate parallel to (along) the direction in which the wave travels. The wave moves as a series of compressions (regions where particles are crowded together) and rarefactions (regions where they are spread apart). Sound in air is a longitudinal wave. It needs a material medium (solid, liquid or gas) and cannot travel through a vacuum.

Working Process

  1. A vibrating source (tuning fork, vocal cords, loudspeaker) pushes the nearby particles forward, forming a compression.
  2. Those particles push on their neighbours and return, while the neighbours do the same; the disturbance passes on along the medium.
  3. When the source moves backward, it leaves a low-pressure region, a rarefaction, which follows the compression.
  4. No particle travels with the wave. Only the energy and the disturbance move forward.
Examples: sound waves, waves in a slinky pushed and pulled along its length, ultrasonic waves. Speed of sound in air at 25°C is about 346 m/s; it is greater in solids than in liquids and gases.

22Compression and Rarefaction

Explanation

A compression (C) is the region of a sound wave where particles are close together, so the density and pressure are high. A rarefaction (R) is the region where particles are far apart, so the density and pressure are low. A compression and the next rarefaction together make one complete wave. The graph of pressure (or density) against distance is a smooth wave with crests at compressions and troughs at rarefactions.

Working Process

  1. When a prong of the tuning fork moves right, it pushes the air in front of it: air is squeezed, a compression forms.
  2. When the prong moves back left, the air next to it is left thin: a rarefaction forms.
  3. These alternating regions move away from the fork at the speed of sound.
  4. A listener's eardrum feels alternate high and low pressure, which we hear as sound.
Wavelength (λ) = distance between two consecutive compressions or rarefactions. Frequency (f) = number of compressions passing a point per second. Speed v = f × λ.

23Transverse Wave (to understand the concept)

Explanation

In a transverse wave the particles of the medium vibrate perpendicular to the direction of the wave. The wave consists of crests (highest points) and troughs (lowest points). Waves on a stretched string, ripples on water and light waves are transverse. Sound in air is NOT transverse; this diagram is given only to compare the two types of waves.

Working Process

  1. Shake one end of a rope up and down. A hump (crest) forms and travels along the rope.
  2. The next downward shake creates a dip (trough) that follows.
  3. Each rope particle only moves up and down about its mean position (red dot).
  4. The distance between two consecutive crests (or two troughs) is the wavelength; the maximum displacement from the mean position is the amplitude.

24Sound Wave: Amplitude, Wavelength and Frequency

Explanation

Every sound wave is described by a few quantities. Amplitude decides how loud the sound is (its loudness is related to the energy carried). Frequency decides the pitch: a shrill sound (whistle) has a high frequency, a deep sound (drum) has a low frequency. Wavelength is the length of one wave. For a given medium the speed is constant, so a higher frequency always means a shorter wavelength.

Working Process

  1. Strike a tuning fork gently, then hard: the prongs move through a larger distance (larger amplitude), so the sound is louder, but the pitch does not change.
  2. Use a shorter tuning fork: it vibrates faster (higher f), so the sound has a higher pitch.
  3. Count the number of complete waves passing a point in one second to get frequency in hertz. Time period T = 1/f.
  4. Use v = f × λ. Example: f = 500 Hz, λ = 0.68 m → v = 340 m/s.
Audible range of human beings: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Below 20 Hz is infrasound; above 20 kHz is ultrasound. Loudness is measured in decibel (dB).

25Human Ear

Explanation

The human ear converts sound waves in the air into electrical signals that the brain understands. It has three parts. The outer ear (pinna and ear canal) collects sound. The middle ear (eardrum and three tiny bones) carries and amplifies the vibrations. The inner ear (cochlea) changes the vibrations into electrical signals. The semicircular canals in the inner ear help us keep our balance; they have no role in hearing.

Working Process (how we hear)

  1. The pinna collects sound waves and directs them into the ear canal.
  2. The waves strike the thin, tight eardrum and make it vibrate with the same frequency (compression pushes it in, rarefaction pulls it out).
  3. The vibrations pass to the three bones: malleus → incus → stapes. They act as levers and amplify the vibrations many times.
  4. The stapes pushes on the oval window of the cochlea, setting the fluid inside it into motion.
  5. Tiny hair cells in the cochlea turn these movements into electrical signals.
  6. The auditory nerve carries the signals to the brain, which interprets them as sound.
Eustachian tube: joins the middle ear to the throat and keeps the air pressure on both sides of the eardrum equal (that is why your ears "pop" during a flight). Hearing range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.
