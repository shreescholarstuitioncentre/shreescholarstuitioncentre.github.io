Real Numbers

Euclid's division lemma
a = bq + r
0 ≤ r < b. Used to find the HCF of two numbers step by step.
HCF and LCM relation
HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b
True only for two numbers, not for three or more.
LCM is a multiple of HCF
LCM(a, b) = k × HCF(a, b)
k is a positive integer.
Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic
N = p1a1 · p2a2 ⋯ pkak
Every composite number has a unique prime factorisation, apart from the order of the factors.
Terminating decimal
pq terminates ⇔ q = 2m · 5n
q must be in its lowest terms; m and n are whole numbers.
Non-terminating repeating decimal
pq repeats ⇔ q has a prime factor other than 2 or 5
Any factor other than 2 and 5 gives a repeating decimal.
Irrational numbers
√p is irrational, for every prime p
Also: rational ± irrational = irrational, and non-zero rational × irrational = irrational.
HCF of fractions
HCF = HCF of numeratorsLCM of denominators
Write all fractions in lowest terms first.
LCM of fractions
LCM = LCM of numeratorsHCF of denominators
Exam tip

The HCF–LCM relation and the terminating-decimal test appear almost every year. Remember the relation holds only for two numbers.

Chapter 2

Polynomials

Zero of a polynomial
p(x) = 0 ⇒ x = α is a zero
The number of zeroes is at most the degree of the polynomial.
Sum of zeroes (quadratic)
α + β = -ba
For ax² + bx + c.
Product of zeroes (quadratic)
αβ = ca
Polynomial from its zeroes
x2 - (α + β)x + αβ = 0
Or k[x² − (sum)x + (product)], for any k ≠ 0.
Sum of zeroes (cubic)
α + β + γ = -ba
For ax³ + bx² + cx + d.
Sum of products taken two at a time
αβ + βγ + γα = ca
Product of zeroes (cubic)
αβγ = -da
Division algorithm
p(x) = g(x) · q(x) + r(x)
Either r(x) = 0, or degree of r(x) < degree of g(x).
Exam tip

If the zeroes are given, build the polynomial as x² − (α + β)x + αβ. Check the signs twice — a sign slip is the commonest error here.

Pair of Linear Equations in Two Variables

Standard form
a1x + b1y + c1 = 0 , a2x + b2y + c2 = 0
a₁, b₁, a₂, b₂ are not zero together.
Unique solution
a1a2 ≠ b1b2
Graph: intersecting lines. The pair is consistent.
Infinitely many solutions
a1a2 = b1b2 = c1c2
Graph: coincident lines. Consistent and dependent.
No solution
a1a2 = b1b2 ≠ c1c2
Graph: parallel lines. The pair is inconsistent.
Cross-multiplication method
xb1c2 - b2c1 = yc1a2 - c2a1 = 1a1b2 - a2b1
Write the equations as a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 first, with signs.
Elimination method
a1b2 - a2b1 ≠ 0 ⇒ unique (x, y)
Multiply to equalise one coefficient, then subtract.
Consistent pair
a1a2 ≠ b1b2 or a1a2 = b1b2 = c1c2
A pair with at least one solution.
Inconsistent pair
a1a2 = b1b2 ≠ c1c2
No solution at all.
Exam tip

Write both equations as ax + by + c = 0 before comparing a₁/a₂, b₁/b₂, c₁/c₂. That one comparison decides unique, infinite or no solution.

Chapter 4

Quadratic Equations

Standard form
ax2 + bx + c = 0 , a ≠ 0
A quadratic equation has at most two real roots.
Quadratic formula
x = -b ± √b2 - 4ac2a
Use when factorisation is not obvious.
Discriminant
D = b2 - 4ac
D decides the nature of the roots.
D > 0
two distinct real roots : -b + √D2a , -b - √D2a
Graph cuts the x-axis at two points.
D = 0
two equal real roots : x = -b2a
Graph touches the x-axis at one point.
D < 0
no real roots
Graph does not meet the x-axis.
Sum and product of roots
α + β = -ba , αβ = ca
Equation from its roots
x2 - (α + β)x + αβ = 0
Completing the square
(x + b2a)2 = b2 - 4ac4a2
Add and subtract (b/2a)² to build a perfect square.
Exam tip

Find D = b² − 4ac first. It tells you how many roots to expect, and saves you from solving an equation that has no real roots.

Chapter 5

Arithmetic Progressions

nth term
an = a + (n - 1)d
a is the first term, d the common difference.
Common difference
d = a2 - a1 = a3 - a2 = …
Constant for the whole progression.
Sum of first n terms
Sn = n2[2a + (n - 1)d]
Sum when the last term is known
Sn = n2(a + l)
l is the nth (last) term.
nth term from the sum
an = Sn - Sn-1
Number of terms
n = l - ad + 1
Sum of first n natural numbers
1 + 2 + 3 + … + n = n(n + 1)2
An AP with a = 1, d = 1.
Three terms in AP
a - d , a , a + d
Sum = 3a. Useful when the sum of three terms is given.
Exam tip

Write down a, d and n before anything else. If the last term is given, Sₙ = n/2 (a + l) is faster than the full formula.

Chapter 6

Triangles

Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales)
DE ∥ BC ⇒ ADDB = AEEC
A line parallel to one side divides the other two sides in the same ratio.
Converse of BPT
ADDB = AEEC ⇒ DE ∥ BC
Similarity criteria
AA · SSS · SAS
AAA and AA are the same test: two equal angles are enough.
Corresponding sides of similar triangles
△ABC ~ △DEF ⇒ ABDE = BCEF = CAFD
Ratio of areas
ar(△ABC)ar(△DEF) = (ABDE)2
Area ratio = square of the side ratio.
Ratio of perimeters, medians, altitudes
perimeter(ABC)perimeter(DEF) = ABDE
Not squared — it stays equal to the side ratio.
Pythagoras theorem
AC2 = AB2 + BC2
In a right triangle, angle B = 90°, AC is the hypotenuse.
Converse of Pythagoras
AC2 = AB2 + BC2 ⇒ ∠B = 90°
Used to test whether a triangle is right-angled.
Equilateral triangle
altitude = √32a , area = √34a2
a is the side.
Exam tip

In area-ratio questions the ratio is squared. In perimeter, median and altitude questions it is not. This is the most common slip in this chapter.

Chapter 7

Coordinate Geometry

Distance formula
d = √(x2 - x1)2 + (y2 - y1)2
Between P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂).
Distance from the origin
d = √x2 + y2
Section formula (internal)
P = ( m1x2 + m2x1m1 + m2 , m1y2 + m2y1m1 + m2 )
Divides PQ in the ratio m₁ : m₂.
Midpoint
M = ( x1 + x22 , y1 + y22 )
The section formula with m₁ : m₂ = 1 : 1.
Points of trisection
1 : 2 and 2 : 1
Apply the section formula twice.
Centroid of a triangle
G = ( x1 + x2 + x33 , y1 + y2 + y33 )
Average of the three vertices.
Area of a triangle
A = 12 |x1(y2 - y3) + x2(y3 - y1) + x3(y1 - y2)|
The vertical bars mean absolute value — area is never negative.
Collinear points
area of triangle = 0
Three points on one straight line.
Exam tip

Draw a rough figure and mark the given points before choosing a formula. For 'find the ratio' questions, take the ratio as k : 1 — the algebra becomes much shorter.

Chapter 8

Introduction to Trigonometry

Primary ratios
sin θ = PH , cos θ = BH , tan θ = PB
P perpendicular, B base, H hypotenuse.
Reciprocal ratios
cosec θ = HP , sec θ = HB , cot θ = BP
cosec θ = 1/sin θ, sec θ = 1/cos θ, cot θ = 1/tan θ.
Ratio relations
tan θ = sin θcos θ , cot θ = cos θsin θ
Identity 1
sin2θ + cos2θ = 1
Valid for every angle θ.
Identity 2
1 + tan2θ = sec2θ
Divide identity 1 by cos²θ.
Identity 3
1 + cot2θ = cosec2θ
Divide identity 1 by sin²θ.
Complementary angles
sin(90° - θ) = cos θ , tan(90° - θ) = cot θ , sec(90° - θ) = cosec θ
And the reverse: cos(90° − θ) = sin θ, and so on.
Range of values
0 ≤ sin θ ≤ 1 , 0 ≤ cos θ ≤ 1 , sec θ ≥ 1 , cosec θ ≥ 1
Standard values
Ratio0°30°45°60°90°
sin θ0121√2√321
cos θ1√321√2120
tan θ01√31√3not defined
Exam tip

Learn the 0°–30°–45°–60°–90° table by heart. Most board questions reduce to reading one value from it and substituting.

Chapter 9

Some Applications of Trigonometry

Line of sight
observer's eye → object
The line drawn from the eye of the observer to the object seen.
Angle of elevation
angle above the horizontal
Used when the object is higher than the observer.
Angle of depression
angle below the horizontal
Used when the object is lower than the observer.
Basic relation
tan θ = heighthorizontal distance
Every such problem is one right triangle.
Height from distance
h = d · tan θ
Distance from height
d = h · cot θ = htan θ
Standard cases
θ = 45° ⇒ h = d · 30° ⇒ h = d√3 · 60° ⇒ h = d√3
Worth memorising — they save a full step in the exam.
Depression equals elevation
angle of depression = angle of elevation
Alternate angles between two parallel horizontal lines.
Exam tip

Always mark the right angle and the angle of elevation in your figure. Heights use tan θ, distances along the ground use cot θ.

Chapter 10

Circles

Radius and tangent
OP ⊥ PT
The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.
Equal tangents
PA = PB
Lengths of the two tangents drawn from an external point P are equal.
Angle at the point of contact
∠OAP = ∠OBP = 90°
OAPB is a cyclic quadrilateral with ∠APB + ∠AOB = 180°.
Length of a tangent
PT = √OP2 - r2
From an external point at distance OP from the centre.
Number of tangents
inside : 0 · on the circle : 1 · outside : 2
Two concentric circles
half-chord = √R2 - r2
The chord of the larger circle that touches the smaller one is bisected at the point of contact.
Tangent parallel to a chord
tangent ∥ chord ⇒ it bisects the arc
The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord.
Exam tip

Every tangent question starts the same way: draw the radius to the point of contact, mark the 90° angle, then use Pythagoras.

Areas Related to Circles

Circumference
C = 2πr = πd
d = 2r.
Area of a circle
A = πr2
Take π = 22/7 or 3.14 as the question says.
Length of an arc
l = θ360° × 2πr
θ is the central angle in degrees.
Area of a sector
A = θ360° × πr2
Area of a sector from arc length
A = 12 × l × r
Shortcut when the arc length is already known.
Area of a segment
A = θ360°πr2 - 12r2 sin θ
Sector minus the triangle formed by the two radii and the chord.
Perimeter of a sector
P = l + 2r
Arc plus the two radii.
Area of a ring
A = π(R2 - r2)
R outer radius, r inner radius.
Exam tip

Convert the angle into a fraction of 360° first. Sector area is simply that fraction of πr², and arc length the same fraction of 2πr.

Surface Areas and Volumes

Slant height of a cone
l = √h2 + r2
Needed before any cone surface area.
Slant height of a frustum
l = √h2 + (r1 - r2)2
r₁ and r₂ are the two radii.
Frustum
CSA = πl(r1 + r2)
Total surface of a frustum
TSA = πl(r1 + r2) + πr12 + πr22
Volume of a frustum
V = 13πh(r12 + r1r2 + r22)
Hollow cylinder
V = π(R2 - r2)h
R outer, r inner radius.
Unit conversion
1 m3 = 1000 litres , 1 litre = 1000 cm3
Keep every measurement in the same unit before substituting.
Conversion of solids
volume before = volume after
Melting, recasting and flowing water questions all use this.

Statistics

Class mark
xi = lower limit + upper limit2
The midpoint of a class interval.
Mean — direct method
x = ΣfixiΣfi
Mean — assumed mean method
x = a + ΣfidiΣfi , di = xi - a
a is the assumed mean.
Mean — step deviation method
x = a + h(ΣfiuiΣfi) , ui = xi - ah
h is the class size. Quickest when the classes are equal.
Mode
Mode = l + (f1 - f02f1 - f0 - f2) × h
l lower limit of the modal class, f₁ its frequency, f₀ the frequency before it, f₂ the frequency after it.
Median
Median = l + (n2 - cff) × h
n total frequency, cf cumulative frequency of the class before the median class, f its frequency.
Empirical relation
3 Median = Mode + 2 Mean
Used to find the third measure when two are given.
Cumulative frequency
cfi = cfi-1 + fi
Add each frequency to the running total.
Exam tip

Read the question carefully: mean, median and mode each need different table columns. Median needs a cumulative-frequency column; mean does not.

Chapter 14

Probability

Theoretical probability
P(E) = Number of favourable outcomesTotal number of outcomes
All outcomes must be equally likely.
Range of probability
0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
Complement
P(E) + P(E) = 1
Ē means 'not E'. So P(not E) = 1 − P(E).
Sure and impossible events
P(sure) = 1 , P(impossible) = 0
Sum of elementary probabilities
Σ P(ei) = 1
Sample space: one die
6 outcomes : 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6
Two dice: 36 outcomes.
Sample space: two coins
4 outcomes : HH, HT, TH, TT
Sample space: a deck of cards
52 cards = 4 suits × 13
26 red, 26 black, 12 face cards (J, Q, K), 4 aces.
Exam tip

Count the sample space first — 6 for a die, 36 for two dice, 52 for a deck, 4 for two coins. Most errors come from a wrong total.
